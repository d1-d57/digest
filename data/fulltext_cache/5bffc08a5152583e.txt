Dark stars — New theories shed light on black holes
By Marianne Freiberger, plus.maths.org, 26 February 2026

Theoretical physicists are investigating whether hypothetical boson stars, or similar objects, could be masquerading as black holes. Gravitational waves play an important role in this research.

"Ladies and gentlemen, we have detected gravitational waves!" — David Reitze (LIGO), 14 September 2015 announcement of the first gravitational wave detection, caused by two colliding black holes over 1 billion lightyears away, confirming Einstein's 1916 prediction.

Ten years on, gravitational waves have provided a wealth of information about black holes and neutron stars, but research continues. Cambridge physicists (Ulrich Sperhake, Seppe Staelens et al., DAMTP) have added to work suggesting not everything that looks like a black hole is one — with implications for dark matter.

Background: gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein's general relativity (space curvature analogy: bowling ball on trampoline). Confirmed physical phenomenon by 1960s; first detected by LIGO in 2015. All detected signals so far are attributed to black hole or neutron star binaries, identified via "fingerprint" matching against theoretically computed waveform templates.

Problem: the template database only contains black hole/neutron star waveforms, so any signal will be matched to those even if the true source is something else ("when you have a hammer, everything looks like a nail").

Boson stars: hypothetical stars made of bosons (vs. familiar fermionic matter), first considered by John Wheeler in the 1950s. Assumed to be made of dark-matter-like particles (e.g. axion-like), invisible to light — a dark matter candidate. Because they're dark, boson stars could also mimic black holes: certain boson star configurations produce a light ring similar to a black hole's, which is the main observational signature we use to identify black holes.

2024 paper (Sperhake, Evstafyeva, Romero-Shaw, Agathos): boson star binaries would produce gravitational wave signals very similar to black hole binaries — confirming they could act as mimickers.

Recent work (Marks, Staelens, Evstafyeva, Sperhake): addressed a theoretical objection that boson stars are unstable and would collapse into black holes under small perturbations — new numerical work shows they are more stable than previously thought, keeping them viable as black-hole mimicker candidates.

Outlook: next-generation gravitational wave detectors (~10 years away) could be precise enough to distinguish these subtle fingerprint differences. The point isn't that literal textbook boson stars will be found, but that they serve as a theoretical proxy pointing toward possible new classes of ultracompact objects beyond black holes and neutron stars.

Quotes from Sperhake and Staelens throughout; interviewed December 2025.
