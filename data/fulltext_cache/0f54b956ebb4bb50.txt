  ON RAVENEL’S TELESCOPE CONJECTURE AND ITS DISPROOF
              [After Burklund–Hahn–Levy–Schlank]

                                  by Christian Ausoni




Introduction

   Ravenel’s Telescope Conjecture is an attempt to account for the periodicity phenom-
ena observed in the stable homotopy category, with for example applications to the
computation of the (stable) homotopy groups of a space or a spectrum in the sense of
algebraic topology.
   Consider a pointed space (X, x0 ), where X is a topological space and x0 ∈ X is a
chosen point called the base point. All maps and homotopies that we consider here are
supposed to be pointed, meaning that they preserve base points. We denote by S n the
Euclidean sphere of dimension n, with a chosen base-point ∗. The n-th homotopy group
of (X, x0 ) is the set of homotopy classes of pointed maps
                             πn (X, x0 ) := [(S n , ∗), (X, x0 )]∗ .
This is a pointed set, and can be given a natural group structure for n ≥ 1, which is
commutative for n ≥ 2.
   The most basic and fundamental example is provided by the spheres, with the problem
of determining all continuous maps
                                          Sn → Sm
up to homotopy, or in other words, of computing πn (S m , ∗). A first interesting example
is given by the following self-map of the circle
                                  fd : S 1 → S 1 , z 7→ z d
where d ∈ Z and where S 1 ⊂ C is viewed as the subspace of complex numbers of
modulus 1. These give all the examples of maps S 1 → S 1 up to homotopy, and are not
homotopic for different values of d. In other words, we have an isomorphism
                                Z → π1 (S 1 , ∗), d 7→ [fd ] .
On the other hand, it can be proven, using for example covering spaces, that πn (S 1 , ∗) =
0 for all n > 1.

   Nevertheless, some aspects of their global structure are know. A major breakthrough
was Adams’ discovery of the infinite periodic α-family of p-torsion elements, for any
prime p. For simplicity, let us suppose that p is an odd prime, the behavior at 2 differing
(but Adams treats it also). Let vp : Z → N ∪ {∞} denote the p-adic valuation.
Theorem 0.2 (Adams, 1966, Theorem 1.5). — Let p be an odd prime. Then for all
k ≥ 1, there exists an element
                                    αk ∈ π2(p−1)k−1 (S)
generating a cyclic subgroup of order pvp (k)+1 that is a direct factor. Moreover, α1 is the
p-torsion element of lowest degree in π∗ (S).
  Note that the p-torsion summands detected by the α-family {αk }k lie in degrees
       2p − 3, 2p − 3 + (2p − 2), 2p − 3 + 2(2p − 2), 2p − 3 + 3(2p − 2), . . .
In other words, they appear periodically with a period of 2(p − 1). This is an example of
v1 periodicity in the sense of chromatic homotopy, as will be explained below. Shortly
later, further periodic families were discovered, with a larger period: Smith (1977) and
Toda (1971) constructed an infinite {βk }k family of period 2(p2 −1) for p ≥ 5, and Miller,
Ravenel, and Wilson (1977) constructed an infinite {γk }k family of period 2(p3 − 1) for
p ≥ 7. These are example of v2 -periodic and of v3 -periodic phenomena, respectively.
   The Telescope Conjecture is precisely addressing the decomposition of stable homo-
topy groups into such periodic families. If we are interested in understanding π∗ (S),
since in positive degrees all groups are finite abelian, we might as well work one prime p
at a time, localizing away from p. Then the periodic families (such as the one described
above) will superpose each other, mixing the different periods, as with the different
wavelengths of light superposing to produce the white. If we look at the global picture,
no periodicity is visible. The purpose of chromatic homotopy theory is to study stable
homotopy by isolating and investigating the vn -periodic phenomena one n at a time.
   Before returning to the construction of the α, β and γ families and discussing higher
vn -periodicity, we briefly introduce spectra.
  All constructions in stable homotopy take place in the category Sp of spectra. This
category can roughly be thought of as a localization of the category Top∗ obtained by
                                         1242–04


inverting the suspension operator Σ. This offers a great simplification, since it is no
longer necessary to “suspend enough” or pass to colimits to study stable phenomena.
Furthermore the category Sp is better behaved, and has a structure that is comparable
to the structure of the derived category D(Z ) of Z -modules, but were Z is replaced
by S. The localization functor Top∗ → Sp is denoted Σ∞ .
  We refer to Lurie (2006, 2017) for the modern construction of Sp, and list below the
notions and notations that we will use in the sequel. The category Sp is a cocomplete,
stable (∞)-category generated by a single object, the sphere spectrum S. It admits a
symmetric monoidal product ⊗, often called the smash product, which admits S as a
unit, and preserves colimits in each variable. It admits a t-structure that is both right
and left complete, and its heart is equivalent to the category of abelian groups under
the Eilenberg–Mac Lane functor H.
  Its homotopy category ho(Sp) is triangulated, and we usually call its triangles
                                  X → Y → Z → ΣX
cofiber sequences. We will often omit ΣX from the notation. For two spectra X and Y ,
the group ho(Sp)(X, Y ) is usually denoted [X, Y ]. The n-th homotopy group of a
spectrum X is defined as
                                  πn (X) = [Σn S, X] .
Note that these are Z -graded. Here π∗ (S) precisely coincides with the stable homotopy
groups introduced in the discussion above, with πn (S) = 0 for n < 0 (by Hurewicz’
Theorem).
  A spectrum E is said to be n-connective for n ∈ Z if πk (E) = 0 for k < n.
  If E is a spectrum, the associated (generalized) homology theory E∗ (−) is defined
defined by

In particular, π∗ (X) = S∗ (X) is the homology theory associated to the sphere spectrum S.

of E. The cohomology theory E ∗ (−) associated to E is defined by
                                  E n (X) = [X, Σn E] .
If A is an abelian group, the ordinary (co)-homology with coefficients in A is the theory
associated to the Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum HA, characterized by πn (HA) = A if
n = 0, and πn (HA) = 0 otherwise. When W is a pointed space and E is a spectrum,
we define En (W ) := En (Σ∞ W ) and E n (W ) := E n (Σ∞ W ).

     As we will learn from the Periodicity Theorem 2.5, this process continues indefinitely,
  featuring vn -torsion and vn -periodicity phenomena of period |vn | = 2(pn − 1) for all
  n ≥ 0. It starts with v0 -periodicity, where v0 = p. We have seen that the only v0 -
  periodic family lies in π0 (S) ∼
                                 = Z , where pk ̸= 0 for all k ≥ 1, since all other groups are
  finite groups. Reducing mod v0 , the periodic class v1 appears in V (0)∗ , allowing for the
  study of v1 -torsion and v1 -periodicity phenomena, and so on. However, these higher
  “primes” vn appear only in S when reducing modulo the lower primes (v0 , . . . , vn−1 ).
     A powerful tool for proving that the classes αk , βk and γk constructed above are
  non-trivial is offered by the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence based on the complex
  cobordism spectrum M U . Inspired by ideas of Jack Morava (published later (Morava,
  1985)), Miller, Ravenel, and Wilson (1977) defined a chromatic filtration on the Adams–
  Novikov spectral sequence expected to reflect the periodicity phenomena described above.
  Ravenel (1984) then published a foundational paper where he stated eight conjectures
  in chromatic homotopy, including the Telescope Conjecture.
     The Telescope Conjecture equates two competing notions of vn -torsion classes, which
  can be used to define two competing chromatic filtrations of the homotopy groups of
  a spectrum. All of these conjectures by Ravenel – except the Telescope Conjecture –
  were proven true within a few years, following the breakthrough achieved by Devinatz,
  Hopkins, and Smith (1988).
     The Telescope Conjecture was known to hold in height 0 and 1; nevertheless, it
  was apparently believed to be too optimistic by experts, resisting until 2023, when
  in groundbreaking work Burklund, Hahn, Levy, and Schlank (BHLS) announced its
  disproof at all primes and all heights ≥ 2, producing explicit counter-examples provided
  by algebraic K-theory.

   1. The chromatic filtration of the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence

Remark 1.11. — Jack Morava’s program for studying the chromatic filtration of the
stable homotopy category was to relate it to the height filtration of the moduli stack of
(1-dimensional) formal groups, the chromatic height of a complex oriented cohomology

Definition 3.1. — Let E be a spectrum, and E∗ the associated homology theory.

    isomorphism.
 3. A spectrum X is called E-local if for any E-acyclic spectrum W we have [W, X] =
    0.

       (a) the spectrum LE (X) is E-local, and
       (b) the map ηE,X : X → LE (X) is an E-equivalence.

   It follows immediately from the definition that if such a localization ηE : id → LE
exists, then for any spectrum X, ηE,X is terminal among E-equivalences out of X, and
initial among maps out of X to an E-local object. In particular, it is unique.

  For any spectrum X, we observe that X is E-acyclic if and only if LE (X) = 0. We
denote by ker(LE ) the full-subcategory of spectra X with LE (X) = 0.
  Note that in Definition 3.1, the notion of E-equivalence, and hence of E-local objects

example, the spectra E and E ⊕ E have the same class of associated acyclic objects,
thus the localization functors LE and LE⊕E are naturally equivalent. This motivates
the following definition.

Definition 3.3. — Two spectra are called Bousfield equivalent if they have the same
class of acyclic objects. The Bousfield equivalence class of a spectrum E is denoted ⟨E ⟩.

only if the class of F -acyclic spectra contains the class of E-acyclic spectra.

it a successful source of counter-examples for the Telescope Conjecture.
   Algebraic K-theory is a functor
                                     K : Alg(Sp) → Sp
                                         1242–29


from structured (or A∞ ) ring spectra to spectra; if the input is a structured commutative
(or E∞ ) ring-spectrum, so is the output, and algebraic K-theory thus specializes to a
functor
                               K : CAlg(Sp) → CAlg(Sp) .
When applied to the Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum of an ordinary ring R, π0 (K(R))
coincides with Grothendieck’s group K0 (R), and its first and second homotopy groups
correspond to classical algebraically defined invariants. Quillen (1973) gave the first
definition of higher algebraic K-theory, where the n-th K-theory group Kn (R) is defined
as the homotopy group of a connective spectrum K(R). Grothendieck’s group K0 (R) it
obtained from group-completing the monoid of (isomorphism classes) of finitely gener-
ated projective R-modules, with the direct sum. In an informal sense, higher algebraic
K-theory corresponds to a higher-categorical group-completion of the same underlying
monoid, but taking into account the isomorphisms (i.e. group-completing a groupoid).
Quillen’s +-construction and Q-construction were first realizations of this idea; this
was later generalized by the S• -construction of Waldhausen (1985). Barwick (2016) ex-
tended the S• -construction to suitable ∞-categories, providing the modern framework
for algebraic K-theory.
  If A is a ring spectrum, Perf(A) denotes the full subcategory of the category of left
A-modules spanned by A under finite colimits, desuspensions and retracts. For an
ordinary ring R, objects of Perf(R) coincide with bounded chain complexes of finitely
generated projective R-modules. The K-theory of A is then defined as

                                  K(A) := K(Perf(A)) .

This functor is difficult to compute; Quillen (1972), in a tour de force, computed K∗ (Fp ),
while K∗ (Z ) has been only partially determined because of number theoretical issues.
The functor K has many fascinating properties; the ones that interest us most here
concern its interactions with chromatic homotopy theory.
  The K-theory spectrum K(S) is the receptacle of an invariant used in the study
of the geometric topology of manifolds, see Waldhausen, Jahren, and Rognes, 2013.
Waldhausen (1984) proposed to study K(S) by climbing the chromatic tower

               K(S) · · · → K(Lf2 (S)) → K(Lf1 (S)) → K(Lf0 (S)) = K(Q ) ,

which motivated the study of K(A) when A is for example a T (n)-local spectrum for
small values of n, and also motivated the development of chromatic homotopy theory.
   Note that when A is a non-zero commutative ring spectrum, K(A) is never a finite
spectrum; in fact its chromatic behavior sharply contrasts with the chromatic behavior
of finite spectra as stated in the Periodicity Theorem 2.5. A first example is the following
theorem. Recall that K(n) denotes the n-th Morava K-theory at a prime p. If R is an
ordinary ring, then K(1)∗ (R) = 0, but for example we have K(1)∗ (K(Z )) ̸= 0.
                                          1242–30


Theorem 6.2 (Mitchell, 1990, Theorem A). — For any (ordinary) ring R, any prime p
and any n ≥ 2, the n-th Morava K-theory of K(R) vanishes:
                                    K(n)∗ (K(R)) = 0 .

   This provides examples where algebraic K-theory increase the chromatic complexity
of its input from 0 to 1 (but no more). Another example, to which we will return
below, is given by the Adams summand ℓ introduced in Definition 5.13. Note that
LT (1) (ℓ) = L ̸= 0 while LT (2) (ℓ) = 0. In (Ausoni and Rognes, 2002), we showed that if
p ≥ 5, then LT (2) (K(ℓ)) ̸= 0 while LT (3) (K(ℓ)) = 0.
   Such examples led John Rognes to state the Redshift Conjectures (Ausoni and Rognes,
2008; Rognes, 2014) on the chromatic and arithmetic behavior of the algebraic K-
theory of ring spectra. Here Redshift refers to the idea that K-theory promotes the vn -
periodicity of its input to vn+1 -periodicity of its output, thus “increasing” the wavelength
(shifting towards red). This has recently been proven in full generality for commutative
ring spectra.
   Hahn (2016) proved than if A ∈ CAlg(Sp) and if n ∈ N , then T (n)∗ (A) = 0 implies

   Let A be a ring spectrum. For n ≥ 1, the canonical map A → LT (n−1)⊕T (n) (A)
induces an equivalence
                       LT (n) (K(A)) → LT (n) (K(LT (n−1)⊕T (n) (A))) .

  Topological cyclic homology (denoted T C), which builds on topological Hochschild
homology (denoted T HH), has been an extremely powerful tool for performing com-

(unpublished), and T C first appeared in Bökstedt, Hsiang, and Madsen (1993). Nikolaus
and Scholze (2018) offered new foundations for the theory. For A ∈ Alg(Sp), topological
Hochschild homology of A, as a spectrum, is given by

circle T = S 1 . Nikolaus and Scholze promote T HH(A) to a cyclotomic spectrum: they
endow it with a T/Cp -equivariant map

where Cp ⊂ T is the cyclic subgroup of order p, and where T HH(A)tCp denotes the

                                  K(A)
                                             trc   / T C(A)

                                  f∗                       f∗

                                  K(B)

F of the cyclotomic trace K(A) → T C(A)) is the same as the difference between

group-completion, which is a much more mysterious construction.
