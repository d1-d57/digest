Memorial Tribute
Remembering Joe Auslander (1930–2025)
Ethan Akin
Mike Boyle
A Mathematician’s Life
Joseph Auslander (known to all as Joe) was an enduring presence in topological dynamics. He published mathematics in eight decades (the 1950s through the 2020s). In April 2025, while working on mathematics at his desk, Joe fell asleep for the last time.
Joe got his PhD in 1957 under Walter Gottschalk, one of the founders of topological dynamics as a distinct discipline. Following positions with the Carnegie Institute of Technology and the Research Institute for Advanced Studies, in 1963 Joe joined the math department at the University of Maryland in College Park. He retired to professor emeritus status in 2002, but to the end he remained active in the intellectual life of the department and his world wide network of friends and colleagues.
Joe grew up in New York City. His coming of age was shaped by the passionate anti-fascism of his friends and extended family. He shared their love of classical music, art, literature, storytelling, and Jewish-inflected humor. He remained active in progressive politics throughout his life. He was a lifelong consumer of the
New York Times
and its crossword puzzles. He loved to cook and presided, with his wife Barbara, over many dinner parties with friends old and new. An amateur clarinetist, he particularly loved Mozart and Haydn, and above all Beethoven.
For a lovely and much more extensive review of Joe’s rich life and family history, we recommend
https://markauslander.com/2025/04/12/remembering-joe-auslander-1930-2025/
.
Joe Auslander’s Mathematics

The subject of topological dynamics grew out of the study of recurrence and stability for solutions of differential equations. The foundations of the modern subject were laid in the book of Gottschalk and Hedlund
GH55
. In this context the object of study is a
flow
,
$(X,T)$
a jointly continuous action of a topological group
$T$
(often discrete) on a nonempty Hausdorff space
$X$
(usually compact). The flow is called
minimal
when
$X$
contains no proper, closed,
-
$T$
invariant subset. Minimal flows are fundamental objects for topological dynamics. Much of Joe’s work focused on the study of minimal flows on a compact space. His book
Aus88
was a survey of the subject which emphasised the structure theory of minimal flows. It is a beautiful and useful book.
A flow given by an action of
$\mathbb{Z}$
on a compact metric space is called a
cascade
. Initial examples of minimal flows are the cascades given by finite periodic orbits and irrational rotations on the circle. These examples are equicontinuous. An equicontinuous, minimal cascade is just a translation on a compact monothetic topological group. However, the class of minimal cascades is much richer than these examples suggest. As the subject of chaos was gaining attention, Auslander wrote a much-cited paper with Yorke
AY80
containing the
Auslander-Yorke Dichotomy Theorem
: a minimal cascade is either equicontinuous or chaotic, i.e., has sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
For a flow
,
$(X,T)$
one can view
$T$
as a subset
$\widetilde{T}$
of the semigroup
$X^X$
of maps from
$X$
to
,
$X$
endowed with the product topology. (So, apart from trivial cases, this compact space is not metrizable.) The
enveloping semigroup
of a flow
$(X,T)$
is the closure of
.
$\widetilde{T}$
The pioneering work of Robert Ellis on the enveloping semigroup of a dynamical system had a crucial influence on Joe. In one of his early works
Aus60
, Joe observed an important consequence of Ellis’s work which is called today the
Auslander-Ellis lemma
. A pair of points is proximal when the two orbits approach each other arbitrarily closely. The lemma asserts that every point
$x$
of a dynamical system is proximal to a point
$y$
contained in a minimal subset of the orbit closure of
.
$x$
This lemma later attracted the interest of logicians who were interested in its set theoretic nature.
The regional proximality relation, introduced by Gottschalk and Ellis, is a closed relation which extends the, not usually closed, proximality relation. For a minimal system, the regional proximality relation is trivial, i.e., equals the diagonal, exactly when the flow is equicontinuous. On the other hand, the proximality relation is trivial when the system is distal, meaning for every pair of distinct points, the orbit closure of the pair is separated from the diagonal. The Auslander-Ellis lemma easily implies that every distal system decomposes into mutually disjoint minimal subsets.
The regionally proximal relation was of paramount interest to Joe, who in several works made great efforts to achieve a better understanding of this somewhat mysterious object. His studies of general dynamical systems used tools that he had devised: the prolongation relation
Aus64
, a closed relation extending the orbit relation, as well as the capturing relation. The notions of highly proximal extension and maximally highly proximal flow were introduced and studied in
AG77
. They later played an important part in recent works by Zucker and by Le Boudec and Matte Bon.
Joe’s work continued up to the present, see, e.g.,
AF94
,
AGW07
,
AAG08
, and
AN24
.

By
Ethan Akin
City College, New York City, USA
After reading Joe’s book in the early nineties, I found his phone number and dialed (!) him up. He was a bit wary at first about a phone call out of the blue, but he soon warmed up and we began to work together. During my hour commute on the Saw Mill parkway, I would think about the problems we were working on, and when I got to the office I would call him up.
All that phoning was because I was ignorant of the most basic uses of computers. Finally, at Joe’s urging I signed up for email and also, directed by him, learned to use LaTeX. Aside from these secretarial skills, I must confess that most of the work I have done in topological dynamics was built upon foundations laid by Joe. In addition to the several papers that we wrote together, I am proud of the Memoir on Ellis actions that we and Eli Glasner produced.
Joe’s long-standing engagement with radical politics combined with his gregarious personality. One of his hopes was to make a new friend on his last day on earth. He came close. At a Hands Off (protect the rule of law) rally held two days before his death, Joe’s daughter, Bonnie, met a lady from out of town and invited her out. The new friend and Joe had a wonderful time chatting over dinner.
By
Ken Berg
University of Maryland, USA
Joe was a major reason I applied for a job at Maryland in 1967, and he played a significant role in my getting the job. I was not the only young person he helped. He seemed to know people everywhere.
We had many discussions and some joint papers over the years, mixing Joe’s topological dynamics and my measure theoretic inclinations. We also jogged together; sometimes Joe would bring his kids, Mark and Bonnie.
Joe never stopped. We continued discussing mathematics, occasionally getting new results, long after we both retired.
By
Mike Boyle
University of Maryland, USA
Joe’s mathematical influence was not confined to papers. For example, Joe once posed to me a question: must a finite entropy cascade have a subshift cover? That was the beginning of my work which led to papers with the Fiebigs and with Tomasz Downarowicz—among my happiest and most thrilling collaborations.
Joe was an activist. He could rail in department email, e.g., against the American Mathematical Society “facilitating” grant support from the Department of Homeland Security, or the federal government obstructing international academic communication. Joe is the only person I knew who entered emeritus status wanting to do more committee work. Within our dynamics group, Joe promoted visitors, speakers and conference topics.
A long mathematical life has its moments. When Joe was about 90, I emailed him with certain queries and partial results on recurrence in zero-dimensional dynamical systems. Joe suggested Eli Glasner for the queries. Eli referred me to a paper which answered every question and more. The authors were Glasner, Weiss and … Auslander.
By
Tomasz Downarowicz
Wroclaw University of Science and Technology, Poland
I owe to Joe Auslander my introduction to the international mathematical society. He was the one who found my early work from the 1980s interesting and convinced Dan Rudolph and others at the University of Maryland to invite me for a month of activities in dynamical systems. There I met other mathematicians, including Hillel Furstenberg, Mike Boyle, and Vitaly Bergelson (in the future the latter two became my close friends and collaborators). Joe boosted my self-esteem, always claiming that my initials T.D. stand, by no coincidence, for “topological dynamics.” Over the years, Joe visited me in Poland several times, and I visited him in Maryland. He was particularly interested in visiting the concentration camp in Auschwitz. We hiked together in the Polish Karkonosze Mountains and jogged together in College Park and Washington, DC (he still jogged when he was at least 85).
In his late years, Joe became interested in the problem of reliability of commonly accepted mathematics. His essay “When is a proof a proof” became the chapter “On the roles of proof in mathematics” in the book
Proof and Other Dilemmas
edited by Bonnie Gold and Roger Simons. I think Joe’s interest in this matter originated from the fact that many years back (around 1970) he refereed the proof of the variational principle. Joe overlooked a gap in the proof and for nearly half a decade the Variational Principle was commonly accepted based on a faulty proof (a correct proof was given in 1975 by Misiurewicz).
By
Hillel Furstenberg
Hebrew University, Jerusalem, Israel
It was Joe who introduced me to the work of Bob Ellis. The Ellis enveloping semigroup became an important tool for my own work, e.g., my structure theorem. Later on, my collaborator Izzy Katznelson was skeptical about doing real mathematics with very abstract tools; he had to proclaim “wonders and miracles” when the enveloping semigroup was crucial for something we proved. We toyed with the idea of writing a paper “Ellis in Wonderland.”
Altogether I spent many months in Maryland, and my time at UMD was spent mostly with Joe. Of course, we spoke of many things, and probably, if I had a better memory I could tell about some of the more interesting discussions. What stands out in my mind was my telling Joe of my problems getting a driver’s license for driving in Maryland, on account of my English name “Harry” not coinciding with “Harry Hillel” on my Connecticut license (I had spent time at Yale), and what made it worse, that my Israel passport had me as just “Hillel.” Joe calmed me down saying, “Harry, you can spend your time here doing mathematics, or you can spend it fighting the Motor Vehicle Bureau.” I chose the former.
By
Anima Nagar
Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, India
I met Joe Auslander a couple of days after his 80th birthday, while visiting the University of Maryland. Joe was very warm in this first meeting, and the very next day he forwarded me a problem regarding equivalence of two dynamical properties. Joe entrusted me to think of a counterexample if possible; luckily, I could instead prove the equivalence. Thus initiated our collaboration which sustained till his soul left the mortal body. Our last preprint together is still awaiting its final destination.
Our discussions encompassed not just mathematics, on which we had absolutely no differences, but also his interests in music and politics—where we had different background and opinions. I learned a lot from Joe. He was particularly active in encouragement of women and people from marginal background.
To the end, Joe lived for mathematics—he attended a dynamics meeting at UMD on Saturday and left for the heavenly abode on Monday.
By
Xiangdong Ye
University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, China
My academic engagement with Joe Auslander’s work began during my graduate studies, when his seminal monograph “Minimal Flows and Their Extensions” became the foundation of my research in topological dynamics. We first met in 2002, at the Maryland conference “Topological dynamics” (held in honor of Joe’s retirement). During my presentation, I discussed work done with Huang, Li and Shao on sequence entropy pairs. Notably, we had developed a direct proof demonstrating that factors of equicontinuous systems remain equicontinuous, a result previously established only through semigroup methods. Joe expressed particular appreciation for this approach.
Subsequent years afforded numerous opportunities for intellectual exchange at international conferences. Our final meeting occurred in Poland at the 2016 conference celebrating the 60th birthday of Tomasz Downarowicz. Approaching age 86, Joe delivered an elegant lecture and posed an insightful question regarding the extension of Veech’s theorem (characterizing regionally proximal relations) to higher-order regional proximality, a concept introduced by Host, Kra, and Maass that had long been central to my research. This inquiry ultimately bore fruit through recent joint work with Qiu, where we successfully resolved the problem.
Throughout his life, Joe maintained an extraordinary dedication to dynamical systems. His scholarly generosity persisted well into his nineties, evidenced by his continued service as a referee for Discrete and Continuous Dynamical Systems.
