Octonions and the Standard Model

Paul Schwahn and I have come out with a new paper about octonions and the Standard Model:

• 
The Standard Model gauge group from the exceptional Jordan algebra

It builds on things I’ve discussed here, but it goes further.   Let me explain a bit.

A bit is just a binary alternative: 1 or 0, true or false.  That’s how it works in classical logic.  We could also have a ‘trit’, meaning 3 alternatives.

In quantum physics we instead have qubits and qutrits.

Qubits and qutrits are usually described using complex numbers.  The algebra of observables of a qubit is the 
Jordan algebra
 
, consisting of 
 self-adjoint complex matrices.   Similarly, the algebra of observables of an qutrit is the Jordan algebra 
, consisting of 
 self-adjoint complex matrices.

We can also study systems with more than 3 alternative ways to be.  They work the same way, using the Jordan algebras

But we can also do quantum mechanics using other number systems!   The options have been mapped out, and the largest allowed number system for this purpose is the algebra of 
octonions
.

A weird thing is that Jordan algebras built using octonions can describe qutrits, but not quantum systems with more than 3 alternative ways to be.   The  algebra of observables of an octonionic qutrit is the so-called ‘exceptional’ Jordan algebra 
, consisting of 
 self-adjoint octonion matrices.  What makes it exceptional is that 
 is not a Jordan algebra when 
 is bigger than 3.

So, there’s something special about octonionic qutrits—and it turns out that every symmetry in the gauge group of the Standard Model is a symmetry of an octonionic qutrit!

Not every symmetry of an octonionic qutrit is a symmetry of the Standard Model.  But those that do have a simple description.  They are those that restrict to give symmetries of an ordinary qutrit sitting inside the octonionic qutrit… and an ordinary qubit sitting inside that!

Kirill Krasnov is a good friend of mine: I met him soon after he came from Ukraine to work in the United States; now he teaches at the University of Nottingham, 360 kilometers south of me, and I’ll be seeing him in July at the workshop Latham Boyle and I are holding on 
Exceptional structures and the Standard Model
.

There’s a lot to say about the relation of Kirill’s work, 
summarized by him here
, and what Paul Schwahn and I just did.  Kirill describes not just the gauge group of the Standard Model but also its representation on particles—one generation of fermions.  There are 
two ways to embed the Standard Model gauge group
 in Spin(10).  He is working with the “right” one for grand unified theories: the one where the representation of the Standard Model gauge group on one generation of fermions extends to a representation of Spin(10).  The exceptional Jordan algebra leads naturally to the “wrong” one, where the representation of the Standard Model gauge group on one generation of fermions 
does not
 extend to a representation of Spin(10).  However, it’s not at all clear that the Spin(10) grand unified theory is correct.  While it’s beautiful, it predicts proton decay and monopoles, neither of which have been seen.  (It is not quite ruled out by the current upper bound on proton decay, and this is one of the most exciting slow-running dramas in fundamental physics.)  If grand unified theories are not the right path, maybe the exceptional Jordan algebra is.
