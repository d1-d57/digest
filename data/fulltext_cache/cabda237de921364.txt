The year 2025 is the centennial anniversary year since Elbert Frank Cox earned a PhD in mathematics from Cornell University in 1925, an iconic achievement in the annals of mathematics history. This article provides details regarding this American mathematician’s life and legacy, and describes the impact of this achievement in the mathematical sciences over the past century.
In 1925, Elbert Frank Cox became the first known African American to earn a PhD in mathematics. In fact, he is the first known Black person in the world to earn a PhD in mathematics. His PhD was awarded by Cornell University. It is interesting to note that Cox was the second Black person to earn a PhD from Cornell University in any discipline. The first, Thomas Wyatt Turner, had earned his PhD in biology four years earlier (1921). During this time, the 1920s, it was hard for a nonwhite person to get admitted or get a degree from almost any R1-University in the US. However, Cox had done his homework. Cox was aware of Turner’s degree, and Cox was aware that in 1890, Cornell had awarded a BS degree in STEM to Jane Eleanor “Nellie” Datcher, its first Black female graduate. Just as important, Cox knew that the founder and the first president of Cornell University made sure that Cornell would be a perfect fit for students like Cox
2
6
. Ezra Cornell (1807–1874) founded Cornell University with hopes that it would provide equal opportunities for all who wished to be educated, and he greatly opposed the practice of slavery and Jim Crow laws. Andrew Dickson White (1832–1918), the first president of Cornell, promoted the admittance of both women and people of color to Cornell, honoring the founder and his views.
After breaking the racial and stereotype barriers that Black people were not capable of being scholars in certain disciplines or able to do complex mathematics at the PhD level, Dr. Cox became a legend for Black people in mathematics and STEM, as well as for other underrepresented minorities and women in those areas. Dr. Cox spent a lifetime exhibiting excellence in scholarship and teaching, inspiring those he taught and mentored, while holding the door open behind him for others to follow. Dr. Cox wanted his legacy to be significant, not only for his academic achievements, but also for his role as a trailblazer and visionary for future generations of African Americans in mathematics and STEM. Indeed, his life story is a testament of forging resilience and perseverance in the face of adversity and acknowledging the importance of advocating quality education to overcome societal barriers and obstacles.

1
.
Cox’s Early Life and Education
On December 5, 1895, Elbert Frank Cox was born the son of Johnson D. and Eugenia D. Talbot Cox in Evansville, Indiana (IN), where he and his brother, Avalon, were raised by their parents and maternal grandmother in a racially mixed district
13
. In Evansville, the Cox family lived at 715 Oak Street on a block with three Black families and five white families. However, the children on the street did not all attend the same schools, since the schools were racially segregated. Johnson Cox was a dedicated educator and, eventually, principal for about forty years at Third Ave Elementary School for Blacks. He had taken college classes at Evansville College and some courses at Indiana University. The Cox family was deeply religious, highly respected, and strong advocates of quality education for all students. Elbert graduated from Clark High School in Evansville, IN, in 1913. He enrolled as a first-year college student that same year at Indiana University (IU) to study mathematics. However, in high school, Elbert exhibited talents in several areas that included mathematics, physics, and music. He was a talented violinist. During his last year of high school Elbert was awarded a music scholarship, which would have enabled him to travel to Europe to study at the Prague Conservatory of Music. Despite the woefully funded Black elementary schools that did not provide an environment that encouraged outstanding achievement, by high school Elbert had developed a sense of striving for excellence in all of the endeavors that he pursued. It appears that his father advocated aiming for high aspirations, and Elbert adopted the same for his endeavors.
2
.
Indiana University (IU)
It was Elbert’s great love for mathematics that led him to enroll at IU in Bloomington, IN. Elbert’s brother, Avalon, also attended IU while Elbert was there. Elbert joined the Black fraternity Kappa Alpha Psi during his period of study at IU, and his pursuit of excellence led him to earn an AB degree in mathematics with distinction. He had an exemplary academic honors record and some of his professors said that he was one of the best mathematics students that they had taught. Three of Cox’s professors, Cora Hennel, Schuyler Davisson, and Tobias Dantzig, were involved with IU’s Euclidean Circle—an elite group of mathematics students and faculty members who met periodically to discuss and engage in various mathematical activities. Cox was the first Black student elected to the Euclidean Circle (15 March 1915) and was the only Black in the Circle during his tenure at IU. Both Elbert and his brother were elected members of the Physics Club. Elbert served as secretary of the Physics Club during his senior year. Although Elbert’s outstanding work gained the attention of a number of his peers and faculty, it did not lead to changes in the racial mores and folkways of IU.

During Elbert’s tenure at IU, Black students were not allowed to sit in the lecture halls during lecture classes
1
. “In class at Indiana, while everyone else was in the lecture hall, the Black students had to sit out in the hallway with the door open so that they could hear,” recalls Elbert Lucien Cox Sr., one of Cox’s four sons, noting that despite this unequal treatment, “my father received an A in every mathematics course he took.” Elbert Cox was denied election to the Honor Society, Phi Beta Kappa, due to a racial divide by the selection committee, despite the fact that several other students were elected whose academic records were far less exemplary than Elbert’s
14
. Elbert graduated from IU in 1917, along with three other Black students. All four Black students’ names were listed at the end of the graduation list, not because of alphabetical order or their academic standing, but because of their race. At graduation, the diploma that Black students received had “COLORED” printed across it.
3
.
After Indiana University
7
15
After graduation from IU, Cox accepted an appointment as a mathematics teacher at Alves Street School in Henderson, KY. Early in 1918, he resigned that position and enlisted in the army to fight for his country in World War I. To get accepted in the Army, Elbert Cox wrote and asked the president of IU for a letter of recommendation. The president of IU graciously provided the requested letter of recommendation. Elbert Cox enlisted in the Army as a private, and six months later, he was promoted to a staff sergeant after completing basic training at Camp Dodge in Des Moines, Iowa. Now an officer in the US Army, he was immediately sent to France to join the fighting forces in World War I. He remained in France until 1919, when the war had ended. Upon completing his military tour of duty and being honorably discharged, Cox accepted an instructor position at Shaw University, a historically Black college/university (HBCU) in Raleigh, NC, where he remained until 1922. At Shaw, he became chair of the Department of Natural Sciences. In December 1921 he applied for a graduate scholarship at Cornell University, then one of only seven universities in the US offering a PhD program in mathematics. One of the IU faculty who sent a reference letter to Cornell for Cox, gave him a very good recommendation without reservations. The second one praised Cox’s academic skills, but added the caveat: “…expect difficulty from this young man because he is of the Colored Race.”
4
.
Cornell University
Cox received the scholarship in May 1922 and entered Cornell in September 1922. As mentioned earlier, the founder and the first president of Cornell University made sure that Cornell would be a perfect fit for students like Cox. To Cox’s good fortune, a young faculty member named William Lloyd Garrison Williams was assigned to chair Cox’s special committee. Later, Professor Williams became Cox’s doctoral studies supervisor. These two young scholars related well. They respected each other as serious professionals who enjoyed doing work in mathematics. In 1924 Cox was awarded an Erastus Brooks Fellowship, providing him $400 a year. Cox spent some time that year at McGill University in Montreal, Canada, since his advisor, Professor Williams, was there for the year. On September 26, 1925, Cornell University awarded Cox his PhD in mathematics after he defended his dissertation entitled:
The polynomial solutions of the difference equation
.
$af(x+1)+bf(x)=\phi (x)$
5
.
Cox’s Iconic 1925 Achievement: The First Black Student to Earn a PhD in Mathematics
Professor Williams
2
6
learned from inquiry that Cox’s PhD in mathematics was the first one awarded to a Black person in the US. Professor Williams then made a similar inquiry of foreign (European and Asian) universities, which at the time were the only other places where PhDs in mathematics were awarded. The responses led Williams to conclude that Cox was the first Black person in the world to earn a PhD in mathematics. Professor Williams then decided that Cox should have recognition of his degree and dissertation by a university outside the United States. Universities in England and Germany refused to acknowledge Cox’s doctoral degree and dissertation, but the Imperial University in Sendai (Miyagi), Japan, accepted both.
6
.
The Careers of Professors Lloyd Williams and Elbert Cox
As a Black person in the US, there were other difficulties that Cox had to be aware of while he pursued his doctorate and professional career. For example, even though America awarded a total of 28 PhDs in mathematics in 1925, including Cox’s, it was also the era when the Ku Klux Klan was known to have murdered at least 31 African Americans in 1926 by lynching. Indeed, when Elbert was seven, there was a race riot in his hometown that left 12 dead and 50 wounded. Many Black people left Evansville, but the Cox’s family remained. Elbert’s father was an educator and principal, jobs that he maintained for many years, and he was sure that his children could get a better education in Evansville than elsewhere.

.
Clockwise from upper left: W. Lloyd G. Williams, David Harold Blackwell, W. W. S. Claytor, and Dudley W. Woodard.
However, during the 1920s, 1930s, and 1940s, no predominantly white institution (PWI) with a graduate and/or PhD program in mathematics would hire Cox or any Black mathematicians with a PhD. Even Professor David Blackwell, who in 1941 was the seventh African American to earn a PhD in mathematics and who had spent a productive postdoctoral year in 1941–1942 at the Institute of Advanced Study (IAS) at Princeton University, could not get a position at a PWI with a graduate and/or PhD program until the mid-1950s. Williams and Cox each significantly impacted the number of new PhDs produced in America over the next few decades and beyond. Professor Williams became one of the cofounders of the Canadian Mathematical Congress (Society). Cox was an excellent teacher at the MS-graduate level at Howard University, and he inspired, guided, or taught many other African Americans who earned PhD degrees in mathematics. One of Cox’s early students was William W. Schieffelin Claytor, who in 1933 was the third African American to earn a PhD degree in mathematics. The faculty member who attracted Dr. Cox to Howard was Dr. Dudley W. Woodard, who in 1928 was the second African American to earn a PhD in mathematics.
Cox, Woodard, Claytor, Blackwell and others helped to establish a strong and reputable MS degree mathematics program at Howard. Dr. Cox also taught his son, Elbert Lucien Cox, who became a mathematician and taught at Howard with his father. Dr. Cox also inspired the founding of the National Association of Mathematicians (NAM)
10
12
, and he and Dr. Williams continued to communicate after Cox left Cornell.
7
.
West Virginia State College
After earning his PhD degree in mathematics, Cox accepted an appointment as professor of physics at West Virginia State College (WVSC), an HBCU that was poorly funded at the time. Cox was WVSC’s second faculty member to hold a doctorate. Cox immediately began to improve the quality of the college, beginning with updating the curriculum and establishing a science library. On September 14, 1927, Cox married Beulah P. Kaufman, the daughter of a former slave; she was an elementary school teacher. The two had met in 1921, and they dated for six years. Beulah had also worked with Elbert’s brother, Avalon. In 1929, Dr. Cox accepted an appointment at Howard University. The Cox family moved to Washington, DC. Over several years, Elbert and Beulah had four sons: Elbert Lucien, James, Eugene, and Kenneth.
8
.
Howard University
Howard University was founded in 1867 and was open to students of any race, color, or creed. It was named after General Oliver Otis Howard, who persuaded Congress to provide funding to support advanced studies for Black students. Today, Howard is one of the leading HBCUs and a nationally respected university. Howard is the first HBCU that established a PhD program in mathematics, which it did in 1976. Dr. Cox was a member of the mathematics faculty at Howard from 1929 until his retirement in 1966. During his tenure at Howard, he served as associate professor and full professor of mathematics. In addition to mathematics, Dr. Cox had extensive training in physics. When Howard merged the mathematics and physics departments in 1957, Dr. Cox was requested to serve as department chair again. He served in this last chair position from 1957 to 1961. Prior to this, he had twice earlier served as chair of mathematics at Howard.
During World War II, Cox also taught engineering science and war management in 1942–1944, and he headed a special US Army Training Program in 1943–1945. At Howard, Cox had a desire to do research, publish articles, and build a reputation as a researcher. However, because his workloads were so heavy, he chose quality teaching and inspiring students to learn mathematics as his highest priorities. He was an excellent teacher and an inspiring scholar. However, he did publish two articles while at Howard. He published his dissertation in 1934 in the Tohoku Mathematical Journal
3
, and he published an article in 1947 titled “On a class of interpolation functions for a system of grading”
4
. In mathematical research, Dr. Elbert Frank Cox is perhaps most known for exploring and expanding the Boole summation formula based on the work of the Danish mathematician and astronomer Niels Erik. Dr. Cox guided and explored research topics frequently with his master’s students, and he had plans to do more research for publishing near or during retirement.
Regarding Dr. Cox’s excellent teaching and his inspiration as a mathematical scholar, here are comments from two colleagues who knew him well
8
9
13
15
.
•
Professor David Blackwell, who joined Dr. Cox on Howard’s mathematics faculty in 1944, said
11
that “Year after year, our master’s students consistently did much better in departmental oral examinations on materials they had studied with Cox than on materials they had studied with me or from our colleagues: Woodard, Claytor, and others.”
•
Howard’s President Dr. James M. Nabritt declared
5
that Professor Cox had supervised more master’s theses than any other faculty member on Howard’s faculty and that his students performed better than those of his colleagues’ students. He was popular with students, and they admired his scholarly talents in mathematics and his quality teaching. Dr. Cox was the advisor of 30 master’s theses in mathematics at Howard.
Dr. Cox joined the faculty at Howard to help build a strong MS degree program in mathematics. Although he retired a decade before Howard launched its PhD program in mathematics in 1976, it is clear that it was Cox’s iconic 1925 achievement, his scholarly inspiration, his commitment to learning mathematics, his excellent teaching, and his interest in research that attracted some of the most talented Black mathematics faculty and students, both undergraduates and graduates, to Howard’s Mathematics Department. His influence and contributions significantly impacted the establishment of the current reputable PhD degree mathematics program at Howard today. While Cox did not live to see the inauguration of Howard’s mathematics PhD program, he helped lay the foundation for its existence by helping to develop the department to the extent that a PhD program became a natural next step. He provided the department a great deal of credibility as an oasis for learning mathematics. As Professor Blackwell once stated
5
in the 1940s, “as a Black mathematician developing one’s career, Howard is the best place to be.”
Dr. Cox retired from Howard in 1966 and died on November 28, 1969.

Recognitions, Awards, and Honors Related to Dr. Elbert Frank Cox
(AA=African American)
A.
In 1915, Elbert F. Cox was elected to IU’s prestigious Euclidean Circle (first AA).
B.
In 1916, Cox was elected as secretary of IU Physics Club (first AA).
C.
In 1917, Cox graduated from IU (AB in mathematics) with a stellar academic record.
D.
In 1922, Cox was the first AA to receive a graduate scholarship to study math at Cornell.
E.
In 1924, Cox was the first AA to be awarded the Erastus Brooke Fellowship in math at Cornell.
F.
In 1925, Cox was the first AA and the first Black person in the world to earn a PhD in math.
G.
In 1929, Cox was the second AA with a PhD in math to join Howard University mathematics faculty.
H.
In 1934, Cox was the first AA to publish a PhD thesis in Japan.
I.
Cox was the first AA to hold membership in the American Mathematical Society, which he joined for one year in 1925, and subsequently rejoined in the late 1940s.
J.
In 1975, Howard established the Elbert F. Cox Scholarship Fund for Graduate Study in Math.
K.
In 1975, Howard placed a portrait of Dr. Elbert F. Cox in the Mathematics Department.
L.
In 1976, Howard initiated the first math PhD program at an HBCU, for which Professor Cox helped to lay the foundation.
M.
In 1980, NAM had its Annual National Meeting, now held annually at the JMM, and established the Cox–Talbot Address.
N.
In 2002, Cornell Mathematics Department honored Elbert Cox during Black History Month.
O.
In 2006, Evansville recognized Cox’s pioneering achievement with a commemorative marker.
P.
In 2017, IU Library Archives remembered Cox’s mathematics excellence in an article about Cox on the centennial of his graduation from IU.
Q.
In 2025, beginning with the JMM in January, NAM, AMS, MAA, and other mathematics groups, including the London Mathematical Society and math groups in African countries, held special activities to celebrate the centennial year of Cox’s iconic achievement and his legacy. Howard University had the largest, September 26–27.

Professor Elbert Frank Cox Inspired the Author and Many Others
The author was a cofounder of NAM in January 1969. Dr. Cox passed in November 1969. Dr. Cox’s Legacy inspired us to establish NAM
10
12
, and one of the author’s personal and professional regrets is that he never had the distinct privilege of meeting Dr. Cox in person. If I were to write Professor Cox a letter, in memorial, in 2025, this is what I would write:
Greetings Professor Cox: As different groups/units of the mathematical sciences community exhibit ‘Events/Special Activities’ in 2025, to celebrate the centennial year of your iconic achievement in 1925, may we continue to remind present and future African Americans and other minorities, who aspire to reach their highest goals in mathematics and STEM, to persevere, to keep moving forward with excellence as Dr. Cox did (prior to 1925 and beyond). May we never forget to remind them that, as others have done by following your legacy, they too can become their own success stories in mathematics and STEM by doing similarly.
An honored beneficiary of your iconic achievement (1925) and your legacy,
Johnny L. Houston
