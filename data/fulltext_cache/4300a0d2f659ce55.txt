Ramin Zabih on the Future of arXiv
Ramin Zabih
Mark C. Wilson
Communicated by
Notices
Associate Editor Jose Israel Rodriguez
Ramin Zabih is executive director of arXiv, which was founded by Paul Ginsparg in 1991 and is indispensable for mathematical researchers. I interviewed him in early 2025. —MW
MW:
What is your role at arXiv?
RZ:
My model of a university is that major parts are owned by either vice presidents or deans. Academic units are owned by a dean, others are owned by a vice president. arXiv reports to the dean of Cornell Tech, Greg Morrisett. I’m a faculty member in computer science, and it’s natural to have a Cornell Tech professor in charge.
As usual with arXiv, it’s a little complicated. We revised the internal bylaws about two years ago, to make the structure explicit. The executive director reports to the dean, but takes input from many places. Another way of looking at it is to imagine it’s a University facility with a big scientific instrument. Typically the way those work at universities is that they’re under some department—in this case, computer science. There’s a professor in charge of it, and associated staff. There are 20 or so full-time Cornell employees who I supervise (9 of these were hired specifically to work on our technology migration for a few years). They are loosely divided into two groups: software developers and people who are in charge of the operations side.
Among the few people who don’t fit into that category, a particularly interesting one is Steinn Sigurdsson, the scientific director. He’s a professor not at Cornell, but at Penn State. There’s an arrangement between Cornell and Penn State, where essentially they lend him to us. He and I are active scientists on the arXiv staff.
Steinn is a physicist. He’s been the emissary from the scientific community to arXiv. We’re making that role more explicit. Steinn is the person with final authority over the fate of submissions. He’s quite actively involved in a whole lot of scientific meetings, for anything that touches on physics, and even some that are not so much about physics. He also talks to the operations staff.
MW:
What do the operations staff do?
RZ:
They deal with a whole host of issues involving running arXiv on a daily basis. One category is user interactions. arXiv has millions of users. We get on the order of a thousand submissions a day. The most problematic 1% of those, about 10, take a lot of work.
The other piece of what they do, which is probably more of their focus, is to help implement arXiv’s policies. We have a very complicated set of evolving policies, and, for want of a better word, practices that have evolved over many years, and we keep running into new sorts of situations that we have to adapt to. Those policies involve things that are relatively trivial, for example, do we accept papers with line numbers (I think that the answer is no) to things that are more consequential, such as dealing with citation rings or various kinds of fraud or accusations of fraud.
Because of the volume of arXiv we get a lot of edge cases. My favorite example is joke papers submitted on April Fool’s Day. arXiv has a long tradition of accepting these papers. Some people love them and some people hate them. Although the focus of arXiv is obviously to disseminate science, we don’t see the need to stop submitters from having a little fun on a day when it is expected.
A lot of the operations of arXiv are not visible. My community in computer vision views arXiv as kind of like Reddit—you write a post and it goes up, they think ’What’s the problem?’ But that’s because we generally write papers that don’t have issues. But arXiv gets a lot of papers with issues of some kind, and there’s a wide range of issues. There are universities out there where students submit their homework assignments to arXiv. Please don’t have your students do this! There does seem to be a certain group where master’s theses are required, either officially or de facto, to be submitted to arXiv. Now, master’s theses actually can have, of course, very substantial content. There are some quite famous master’s theses out there. But generally that’s not quite what arXiv is looking for. So the view of arXiv that you send things in and they just fly through is a view of the world that in my experience is fairly common among academics. But it’s not actually the majority of what happens, or at least, it’s not the majority of what is challenging for us. There are all these weird special cases. There’s a fair amount of automatic software flagging of things. Flagging of papers does not in general reject them, but puts them on hold for moderator review, and then the moderators implement the policy, along with the paid staff.
You can think of arXiv as having concentric circles. There are the people who are paid, there are people who devote substantial time as council chairs and advisor but are unpaid, and there is a broader pool of volunteer moderators who contribute to arXiv on a weekly basis. It’s sort of a collection of journals, one for computer science, one for math, etc., and then there is an editorial board. The role of the moderators is closer to that of an editorial board member than a reviewer. There are more than 200 people who do this, for whom we are all enormously grateful—arXiv couldn’t function without volunteers. Another way to think of it is a cross between a collection of free journals and Wikipedia.
In addition to the Editorial Advisory Council, which functions as the editorial board of arXiv, there is a Science Advisory Council and an Institutions Advisory Council. When dealing with challenges, we often need input from the scientific community and stakeholders from the library and scholarly communications communities. Getting that input is challenging, so we have some people who volunteer significant amounts of their time to help us figure it out.
MW:
What are important recent developments at arXiv?
RZ:
I took over almost three years ago. We’ve had substantial funding from the Simons Foundation to modernize software and to move it into the cloud. That’s a work in progress. Software always takes longer than you possibly think that it could. To summarize the state of the cloud migration, there’s the part of arXiv that is most of our traffic, which is people reading papers. That’s not so complicated and has been done for a while already. The other part of arXiv, of course, is the submission and moderation system, and that’s much more difficult in a variety of ways. The flowchart in Figure

It’s a bit challenging, and it’s very arXiv-specific. So we have ideas for making that into something that’s sane. But we’re not there yet.

So I would describe cloud migration as both migrating to the cloud and modernizing arXiv’s infrastructure. We are well underway with that project, thanks to the Simons Foundation, but it’s definitely not complete. We have hit some some major milestones. Our development staff has ramped up sharply, and we now have sufficient resources to make these crucial improvements.
There are a number of issues that have accumulated over the years. It’s an odd match for a university, and, while Cornell has kept arXiv going and helped it become the super successful thing that it is, it isn’t a great match to the things that universities do. It’s not research, it’s not teaching, it’s not a journal. It feels like some combination of a big, important scientific instrument that lots of scientists use plus a library plus a journal. It’s very confusing. And so it lived for years in the library. Then it briefly moved into the the unit that houses computer science before coming to Cornell Tech. The library is awesome at many things. But software development, Kubernetes and keeping abreast of containerization and all that kind of stuff, that’s not really what libraries are optimized for—no disrespect to them. They’re great at their thing, but that’s not in their sweet spot. So arXiv is a very confusing thing. Everybody at Cornell who touches arXiv understands that. It’s fantastic, but it also had a fair number of issues arising from confusion over what it is. Until I took over it didn’t have a faculty member in charge of it for many years (although of course it was founded by Paul Ginsparg, who is a Cornell professor). That’s also a recipe for confusion, because faculty have a special role within the university.
We also restructured arXiv’s formal structure to ensure that there is a scientist at the head of it, and to make clear the lines of authority. They were many people who were devoted to arXiv, and who had their fingers in the pie, all of whom had good intentions, but many of whom were pulling in different directions. The only person who actually had any clear authority over arXiv was the dean, who of course has many other responsibilities.
MW:
What plans do you have for the near future?
RZ:
The thing that I am personally most proud of is accessibility. It’s a major change we have made, and we have very ambitious plans to improve on it. In particular, this means making content accessible to the blind or visually impaired. We have NSF funding that supports this initiative. Our key accomplishment, since roughly a year ago, was to roll out HTML versions of all newly announced papers, in addition to PDF; and we plan to eventually go back and create HTML versions for earlier papers. Our PDFs are machine readable, but the HTML version provides a better experience for those using screen readers, as well as for mobile devices in general. It’s gotten an impressive amount of uptake and a lot of really positive informal feedback from the affected communities.
But we’re not done—the next step is audio. We are currently in talks with a couple of places to provide some kind of audio version of papers. There’s a system from Google called
Notebook LM
, that turns a PDF into a podcast using generative AI, there’s a different take in the audio space by
ScienceCast
that is also very interesting. We are planning to jump into this, though we need to be careful to ensure that authors can correct any errors that generative AI introduces into the audio versions.
I will point out that arXiv converts to HTML, essentially by default. We don’t ask the authors. We do that because accessibility is important, and we are likely to do the same thing for audio. It’s the price of admission that you have to make your material accessible to a wide range of people. Of course, HTML is also very useful for people who do not have any form of visual impairment. It allows them to read papers on screen readers or phones. We will probably try to advance the state of the art in terms of HTML conversion of, say, commutative diagrams. My guess is that at some point we will fail, but there’s still quite a bit of value there.
MW:
How are they going to have audio of formulas?
RZ:
That is a very interesting question. What I really know about these things is secondhand. I think what they are trying to do is essentially to summarize the paper. Now that’s quite challenging in a discipline such as mathematics, where the meat of the paper really is proofs and formulae. But even in mathematics, I think it would be of some value. At least we’re moving in the right direction. I think it really fits into arXiv’s mission of making scientific content broadly accessible.
Beyond that, there are projects that are vaguer because we just don’t know what’s going to happen. But we are looking at releasing more sophisticated tools that build on top of arXiv. One idea is to have some kind of opt-in version of arXiv. The usual arrangement with a social media company is that they say “here’s some basic information, but if you really want the good stuff you have to surrender some of your privacy to us so that we can experiment on you.” If you don’t opt in, you’ll still have same arXiv, and it’ll continue to improve. But we want to be able to try fancy things and in particular, if you think about recommendations of papers, we can do much better if we actually know who we’re talking to, and we know what papers you’ve read in the past, and we know a little bit about your background. Then we can do all the recommendation system stuff that everybody does on on the web these days. So the way that I envision this happening is, look, if you’re happy with the current thing, great. But if you are interested in playing with new things, sign up for this opt-in version and we’ll present you with some cool new things, some of which you’ll like and some of which you’ll hate. We’ll use your experience with that to gain feedback, and that in turn will allow quite a bit of innovation within arXiv. The most obvious places are things like better search and better recommendation.
We get a lot of traffic from Google Scholar. But once you’re actually on arXiv, you’re often going to other arXiv papers. There’s a lot of things we can do within arXiv that I think would substantially improve the experience. You could discover communities. Most of us in science kind of know about them already. There are people who work on this topic, and including several researchers at Cornell. But none of that is currently reflected in arXiv. But you might be able to discover communities that are working on similar things who ought to be talking to each other, but who currently aren’t. There are lots of ways that the volume of arXiv, plus its free nature, could potentially accelerate scientific innovation. That is extremely exciting.
Another example is more personalized discovery—suggestions of things that you know. You might be interested in the arXiv daily email that lists papers in essentially chronological order, which is totally stupid. But it’s what we currently do. And one could imagine doing something, because, among other things it encourages gamesmanship. Believe it or not we see: “I’m going to submit my paper at 1159, and 30 seconds.” There’s a lot of opportunities using modern technology. I hesitate to use the term AI, but it’s kind of in that space. We want to both make arXiv more useful for its readers, but also potentially improve the speed of scientific discovery, the propagation of information within science. We’re really excited about that with a caveat that I’ll be personally pretty happy if we have some opt-in stuff going by the end of the year. You can’t do much without getting initial consent from people.
MW:
The idea of people adding services on top of arXiv—is that something that you are happy about? Are you facilitating it?
RZ:
My general view is that our job is to get our scientific material out there. We’re a nonprofit, since we are part of a university which is a nonprofit. We have a mission to promote public access to science within a small number of legal constraints. If someone has a way of getting our content out there more, that’s all to the good. So I don’t really view any of these efforts as being competitors in any sense. There are legal agreements with authors, and we have to defer to Cornell’s legal interpretation of these things, but Cornell of course has supported arXiv’s mission for over 20 years.
MW:
What are your views on the relation of arXiv and peer review?
RZ:
We have our hands full getting to the point where the software is stable and modern, and adding a few essential innovations. I think that will keep us from making very substantial changes for a while. But there’s a lot of cool stuff to be done in that area, and in particular I would point you to
OpenReview
. Some of the enormous success of machine learning has to do with a combination of OpenReview and arXiv and the conferences—everything is open, nothing is secret, there’s no one incentivized to make money along the way.
Suppose arXiv were to do something of the flavor of what
alphaXiv
is doing—allowing users to comment on articles. There are two hurdles to overcome. First, just as a very practical matter, you saw that spaghetti diagram, right? You want to make that more complicated to handle reviews? I don’t think so. That probably puts it out realistically a few years before we could make any kind of substantial change, I would guess 3 years, but it could easily be more. Second, at arXiv we try to reflect the consensus of the scientific community. We don’t always ask. We just did HTML accessibility because we thought it was obviously the right thing. But something like reviews is much more complicated. You end up doing content moderation. It’s not hard to imagine things going poorly with legal liability and public relations. arXiv already does very limited content moderation. We put papers on hold, and sometimes we bounce them back and say, this isn’t for arXiv. That’s relatively rare, but we do it some of the time, and that relatively rare event is a big piece of the spaghetti diagram in Figure
1
. So that’s a very major change, and I think it is likely arXiv would be trying to get some kind of consensus from the scientific community.
If, say, some particular branch of mathematics really loved this and said “we understand all these headaches, but we think it would be just absolutely fantastic for us,” I could imagine arXiv trying to do it for them on a pilot basis. But I’ve been personally somewhat surprised, and maybe a little bit disappointed, that even things that you think are obviously good, when you survey the community it’s never 100% supported. There will always be people who don’t agree. My interactions with arXiv give me great confidence that if I had actually asked people “Do you want HTML?” a few of them would have said no, for whatever reason, and I’m sure they would have yelled quite loudly that HTML is evil.
I would point out that there’s low hanging fruit in that direction. As a researcher, my own personal bête noire with the arXiv is the exceedingly poor integration between arXiv and peer reviewed versions of arXiv papers, which drives me up the wall. My students, when they’re submitting a conference paper in computer vision, will cite arXiv papers. And then the night before the submission deadline, I go through and Google the name of an arXiv paper, and I discover it appeared in the proceedings of a big conference, and I replace the arXiv citation with that. Because I want the reviewers of our paper to know that the papers that we’re citing was peer reviewed. This seems like an easy thing to fix, but it’s tied up in arXiv’s metadata system, which definitely needs work. We’ve tried hard to avoid touching it, because it needs a fairly complete rethinking. It is more likely that we would begin by fixing the metadata system, and this is probably a few years out. Once you fix the metadata system, you can do some kind of straightforward linkage between peer reviewed and unreviewed papers. Some of the peer reviewed papers themselves, depending on where they happen to be located, might have support for this, so it might be that you know the paper is in a conference like ICML or NeurIPS. And then you can point to the OpenReview paper site for that paper. So that’s not quite the full thing you’re talking about, but it’s a path towards it that I think could proceed at a reasonable speed.
MW:
How is arXiv dealing with research integrity in the age of AI?
RZ:
It’s definitely on our radar screen. Knock on wood, it hasn’t hit us in force yet. There are various arXiv internal tools that screen for bad content, such as plagiarism, in various ways. We don’t talk about those tests in detail, for obvious reasons. One would imagine an arms race between people who generate bad scientific papers and people who write code to detect bad scientific papers. It hasn’t been an issue for us, or we’re just not aware of it.
We interact with the ChatGPT world in a variety of ways that are interesting. We’re a big source of training data for these models. No one in the commercial industry will tell you what they use, for obvious reasons. But there’s very strong reason to believe that arXiv is a significant piece of training data for them. We are the place where AI research happens—DeepSeek’s tech report, guess where that is? Where else would it be? We’ve had authors ask whether they may use ChatGPT to write their papers. We don’t allow nonhuman authors, of course. But suppose it’s an aid to an author? There are situations where it can actually be super-useful, for example, for people whose native language is not English. Why shouldn’t they be able to use AI tools for things such as improving spelling or grammar?
MW:
Do you have anything else you would like to say?
RZ:
To give you a hint of the most exciting direction—there are ongoing discussions about whether arXiv might be better off existing as a freestanding nonprofit. Perhaps a little like the World Wide Web Consortium, which was an MIT project for years. I think they spun themselves out 15 or 20 years ago. So there’s ongoing discussion that maybe some other model for arXiv would be would be useful. But everyone involved in that discussion is really devoted to arXiv. It would in no sense be a bad thing for arXiv. I think it could be the next stage in arXiv’s evolution.
