Memorial Tribute
In Memoriam: Thomas W. Hawkins (1938–2024)
Jeremy Gray
David E. Rowe
Communicated by
Notices
Associate Editor Adrian C. Rice
Historians of mathematics worldwide lost one of the true giants in the field with the death of Thomas Hawkins last December at age 86. The previous summer he was still in good health, mountain biking in the Pacific Northwest while working on his fourth book. Hawkins’ published books dealt with three substantial and yet nearly independent subjects: Lebesgue’s theory of integration
1
, the evolution of Lie groups from Sophus Lie to Hermann Weyl
12
, and the mathematics of Georg Frobenius
14
. Equally impressive were his numerous lengthy articles in journals, particularly those published in
Archive for History of Exact Sciences
and in
Historia Mathematica
. In glancing through his bibliography, one is immediately struck by the fact that in the early stages of his career he had already planted the seeds for a great deal of his later work. His second and third books are among the most demanding historical studies in the modern literature, reflecting the fact that he wrote them for an audience that included readers such as Jean-Pierre Serre and Armand Borel, both of whom he thanked for commenting on his work in
12
. Tom Hawkins was an historian whose work truly had great appeal for research mathematicians. In 2001 the AMS named him as its first recipient of the Albert Leon Whiteman Memorial Prize for outstanding scholarship in the history of mathematics. Four years earlier, he won the MAA’s Chauvenet Prize, and in 2012 he was elected as one of the inaugural Fellows of the AMS.
Early Works
Thomas W. Hawkins, Jr. was born on January 10, 1938 in Flushing, New York. After receiving his doctorate from the University of Wisconsin-Madison in 1968, he taught briefly at Swarthmore College before joining the faculty at Boston University (BU) in 1972. He remained at BU up until his retirement in 2009. A conscientious, but demanding teacher, Tom is fondly remembered by his two PhD students, Dan Alexander and Jeff Suzuki. Both found in him a thoughtful mentor who aspired to the highest scholarly standards, just as he inspired many younger historians of mathematics whose work he supported. The inspiration he imparted to Dan and Jeff surely reflected Tom’s own personal experiences dating back to his graduate school days. During his years at Wisconsin, Tom had the opportunity to study history of science alongside mathematics. His mentors in these two fields were the eminent historian of physics, Erwin Hiebert, and the analyst R. Creighton Buck, who was also interested in history of mathematics. The Hieberts, Erwin and his wife Elfrieda, exerted a strong influence on the graduate students from that era, several of whom went on to enjoy stellar academic careers. Roger H. Stuewer and Mary Jo Nye both recalled the special atmosphere that reigned in their home, the meeting place for Hiebert’s advanced seminar, which focused on the writings of leading scientists and philosophers. Tom Hawkins thus gained much more than a solid background in mathematics during those years at Wisconsin.

His first book,
Lebesgue’s Theory of Integration: Its Origin and Development
1
was essentially identical to his doctoral thesis. In six short, insightful chapters it covered: Riemann’s theory of integration; the development of Riemann’s ideas (1870–1880)—notably, trigonometric series; set theory and the theory of integration—looking at such topics as nowhere dense sets and the theory of content; the end of the century—the introduction of the concept of measure and Borel’s work; the creation of modern integration theory by Lebesgue, the work of Vitali and Young, and Fubini’s theorem; together with pioneering applications of the Lebesgue integral.
In 1972, William C. Waterhouse reviewed this book along with three others in the
Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society
, commenting that all four broke new ground for the history of modern mathematics
15
. Singling out Hawkins’ book for praise, he wrote that “Lebesgue integration is one of the great success stories of modern mathematics, and Hawkins tells it very well” (p. 386). After summarizing some of the topics treated in the book, Waterhouse added: “Hawkins’s style is straightforward and clear. Consistent notation is used as much as possible throughout the book, which makes for easy reading” (p. 387). Waterhouse’s closing comments pointed to a newfound interest in historical studies among members of the American mathematical community.
Tom Hawkins’ subsequent work dealt with topics in the history of modern algebra. A trio of papers—

,
3
,
4
—already presaged his third book on the work of Frobenius. In the early 1980s, Tom published two remarkable papers on the largely forgotten work of Wilhelm Killing, beginning with Killing’s novel investigations of non-Euclidean space forms. The second paper dealt with Killing’s classification of complex finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras, a scheme later refined by Élie Cartan in his dissertation. Besides rediscovering Killing’s work and placing it in context, Hawkins also put the Berlin algebraic tradition up in lights. One usually associates this with the work of Ernst Eduard Kummer and Leopold Kronecker, but Hawkins rightly emphasized the contributions of Karl Weierstrass, in particular his theory of elementary divisors. This special focus helped to restore the importance of Berlin mathematics during an era when Göttingen became the far more dynamic research center.
Tracing the History of Lie Theory
In the course of his researches on Killing, Hawkins inevitably had to deal with the work of Felix Klein, a rival figure who later published Killing’s major papers in
Mathematische Annalen
, much to the chagrin of Sophus Lie. Recognizing that Klein was of marginal importance for the evolution of Lie’s theory of continuous groups, Hawkins broke that news with his paper reassessing the place of Klein’s “Erlangen Program” in the history of mathematics
7
. This novel essay represented the first serious attempt to study the reception history of Klein’s most famous work, and Hawkins emphasized that its fame was slow to arrive. When Lie spoke with Poincaré in Paris, a full decade after the “Erlangen Program” came out, the Frenchman had never heard of it. Hawkins’ subsequent investigations touched on topics from the period when Klein collaborated with Lie, but he was keenly aware that the first and foremost challenge he faced was to trace the sources of Lie’s original inspirations.
Our generation of historians was particularly fortunate to learn from one another during three fabulous conferences that took place within a short period of time. Tom took part in all three. The first of these was held in 1984 at the University of Siena’s conference center at the Certosa di Pontignano, originally a fourteenth century monastery located just north of the city. Two years later, in 1986, Tom Hawkins was one of the three featured speakers (alongside Jesper Lützen and Ivor Grattan-Guinness) at the international conference held at Sandbjerg Manor, an 18th-century mansion later acquired by Aarhus University. Tom gave several challenging lectures, one dealing with the role of Hesse’s transfer principle in higher geometry. Soon thereafter, he elaborated on this complex story in
8
. The third notable conference was held at Vassar College in 1988, when for the first time a large entourage of Europeans took part in a meeting on the history of modern mathematics held on American shores. It benefited greatly from the presence of several East Germans, as travel to the West had recently opened under the general influence of the Gorbachev reforms. Tom’s contribution to the Vassar conference volumes was his first major study on Lie’s early mathematical work
9
.
Tom and his new partner Diane Meuser, a number theorist at BU, spent 1988/89 together at the Institute for Advanced Study. This gave him the chance to mingle with some of the leading experts on modern Lie theory. A few years later, he published a deep analysis of Lie’s early work on differential equations, stressing the importance of Jacobi’s theory on Lie’s thinking
10
. Hawkins was especially keen to discover when and how Lie arrived at his “idée fixe,” namely, the goal of developing a Galois theory of differential equations. At the same time, he began to flesh out an overall picture of how Lie’s ideas first evolved and then later intersected with Killing’s work beginning in 1886, when Lie left Norway to assume Klein’s chair in Leipzig. A first sketch of this story appeared in “The birth of Lie’s theory of groups”
11
, the essay for which he won the Chauvenet Prize.
Tom had to overturn many large stones in order to pursue this story further. Killing’s classification of simple Lie algebras was notably improved by Élie Cartan, who went on to become the great exponent of modern Lie theory. Hawkins had to trace the sources that led Hermann Weyl to develop the global theory of Lie groups in the mid-1920s. He also wanted to elucidate the contributions of those in Lie’s Leipzig school alongside the work of others outside it. The result was his monumental
12
, his most important single work. He gave this challenging book a four-part structure based on the contributions of the four principal architects of the theory: Lie, Killing, Cartan, and Weyl. But he also included detailed accounts of works by a host of secondary players as well. This impressive study will long occupy a central place in the historical literature devoted to modern mathematics.
Recovering the Berlin Algebraic Tradition
Tom’s third book,
The Mathematics of Frobenius in Context
14
, represented a return to some of his earlier interests in the history of linear algebra and its rigorization by three leading Berlin figures: Weierstrass, Kronecker, and Frobenius. But it also reflected Tom’s growing recognition of just how much Frobenius contributed to modern mathematics and his central place within Berlin’s great algebraic tradition. Probably no figure of similar stature had been so overlooked as Frobenius, and Hawkins was uniquely suited to fill that lacuna. In doing so, he drew heavily on the still unpublished Dedekind-Frobenius correspondence in order to trace the trajectory of his accomplishments.
After an account of Frobenius’s early years, much of the first part of the book—subtitled
Berlin-Style Linear Algebra
—surveys the theory of matrices and linear transformations in the 1850s, 1860s, and 1870s. The theories of elementary divisors and canonical forms are described with full attention to the context in which these algebraic matters arose. Then the focus broadens, to include chapters on, among other things, matrix algebra, linear algebra, and group theory and group representations, a central theme in the correspondence with Dedekind. There are two chapters on problems raised by the work of Hermite and Kronecker on abelian functions and Frobenius’s generalized theory of theta functions, after which we return to Frobenius’s work on representation theory and the theory of characters. The reception of Frobenius’s work is considered carefully and at length. The introduction and conclusion both seek to reaffirm for a modern audience the importance of one of the more difficult and influential mathematicians of his time.
After his retirement, Tom continued to work on what he imagined to be his last book on the history of mathematics, which aimed to address an altogether different audience. He spent his final years studying the works of those who first developed the calculus, writing about such things as how Leibniz and l’Hôpital tackled area problems and the rectification of curves. He kept a journal of notes on the cover of which he recorded his advice for good living: Eat—Sleep—Bike—Repeat. When Tom wasn’t working on mathematics he was usually exercising, with his true passion being bicycling. He and his partner, Diane Meuser, bonded over their shared love of bicycling, often followed by good food and wine. They undertook many bike rides together, including leading day trips with undergraduates at BU as well as making several long tours in many of the countries in Europe. They took a long bike trip through Tuscany after the Siena conference, and before this they went biking through the Dolomites in Italy. After Tom’s retirement they married and moved to Tucson, Arizona. There Tom became devoted to mountain biking and in his later years acquired an e-bike. Until just a few months before his death he was able to continue biking on trails that were challenging to bikers decades younger, and he enjoyed being an inspiration to them. Tom’s favorite motto was: Never underestimate an old man on an e-bike.
Tom Hawkins greatly appreciated the interest and support his work received from the American mathematical community. In his response to the announcement that he had been awarded the Whiteman Prize, he wrote:
After leaving Wisconsin I was encouraged by the growing interest a number of distinguished mathematics departments showed in my work through their unsolicited invitations to speak about it. Among the many such departments, I want to mention in particular those at the University of Chicago and Yale University, where I have been invited back many times to talk about my latest discoveries. In addition, over the years many first-rate mathematicians have respectfully encouraged me in my work or have assisted me by reading over preliminary drafts of papers and by sharing their expertise, thereby helping me to avoid countless pitfalls as well as to explore connections I would have otherwise missed. To all the mathematicians who in one or more of the above mentioned ways have supported and assisted my work, I hereby extend my heartfelt thanks.
