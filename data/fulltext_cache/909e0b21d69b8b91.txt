This is the edited transcript of a Zoom discussion moderated by Mark C. Wilson in April 2025. The other participants are prominent award-winning authors and presenters of mathematics to general audiences.
MW:
What are good reasons for doing public outreach
?
KD:
Well, of course, historically, it was said that if you have a good public image, it improves the chance of funding from Congress—that may no longer be true!
SH:
We don’t just want to be talking to ourselves, right? It’s really important to make the case that mathematics is a beautiful, wonderful, exciting thing. It’s not just for doing your taxes. It’s not just for measuring rooms or whatever. It’s a delightful exploration. We have to share that with people, because otherwise how are we going to get more people to do mathematics?
KD:
I would not have been in mathematics if it wasn’t for popular books on mathematics. I came from a small school, no academic background, working-class family, no exposure to that sort of thing. But for some reason I came across these books and started reading them, and it didn’t resemble the stuff I was doing in school (which bored me). I read some popular books, and that’s what excited me. That was the only form of science outreach back then, and there were not many books. Lancelot Hogben’s
Mathematics for the Million
, and a couple of other things, and then W.W. Sawyer. They turned me on, and that changed my life, and I cannot be the only person for whom that kind of outreach was the case. Now there is much more outreach, including on YouTube. It must be bringing in people. Because even with good teaching, schools are still trying to get you to pass exams. So the relaxation, the fun part, has to come from somewhere else in a different context. The more of it there is, the more chance we have of catching people in the net.
SH:
Exactly so. I had a book by Raymond Smullyan (
To Mock a Mockingbird
) that blew my mind, and Roger Penrose
The Emperor’s New Mind
, where I learned about Turing machines. They absolutely changed my understanding of what mathematics could be, whereas in school you have to learn techniques—which can be fun, too, but there wasn’t the joy. Part of it was that some of the teachers you have in high school are perhaps not confident mathematicians. In the UK there’s a massive shortage of mathematics teachers. People are persuaded to teach mathematics, who may not necessarily themselves love mathematics. So you get rote learning—here’s an algorithm for solving this kind of problem. But not “this is so interesting because, oh my God, we can do this thing with triangles, and you can measure the circumference of the earth with a stick. You know this is genius, right?” You don’t get that. So these books are how people come to mathematics quite often.
SS:
First, since we’re talking about childhood memories, I want to throw in a pitch for the four-volume series,
The World of Mathematics
by Newman. I used to sit in my high school library for hours reading essays by Poincaré and Hardy’s reminiscence of when he got the letter from Ramanujan.
Now let me mention a different aspect of this. In addition to all the good we can do for our audiences and for the profession, I think we can also do a lot of good for ourselves. You shouldn’t underestimate how much you learn, how much clearer your own thinking gets—as we all know—by teaching something or explaining it. Also all of us love math, which is why we’re doing it. And you get to revel in the things that you love when you write for the public. So this is not really the officially sanctioned reason, because it sounds selfish to say “I’m doing it because it makes me happy to do it.” But honestly, I think that’s a big part of why many of us do it, in addition to all these altruistic reasons. I don’t think you have to make the choice.
I realize we’re talking here about outreach to the public. But mathematicians also communicate with each other, not very well, typically. Also mathematicians communicate with scientists in adjacent fields. I see that as all part of what we’re trying to do in our books and articles.
It doesn’t have to be for the most uninformed reader. For readers of the
Notices
who are thinking “Is this something I could jump into? What if I can’t write like Jordan or Sarah?”, you can still write clearly for other mathematicians, or for Martin Gardner’s readers, who already like math. You don’t have to convince them they like it. They just want to be spoken to understandably. There’s a whole spectrum. I happen to like the readers who are the most math-phobic, the really lost causes. I’m interested in reaching out to them. I find that an exciting challenge, and I don’t know how much I accomplished in trying it, but I feel like there’s this whole gamut from the most math-anxious to the people who were potential mathematicians, but chose to become doctors, or whatever.
You can write books that show plenty of equations. There is the Stephen Hawking claim that every equation cuts the sales in half. If you talk to the editors at Princeton University Press about the books that they’ve published, like
An Imaginary Tale: the story of
$\sqrt {-1}$
, or
:
$e$
The Story of a Number
, those books sell in the many tens of thousands of copies. You can make significant money writing a book that shows plenty of what we would consider math. They won’t be
New York Times
bestsellers, but they could be very successful books by any measure.
MdS:
One of the surprisingly good reasons was to understand my own subject better. I was quite surprised by that. I thought it was going to be quite a one-way process, inspiring the next generation, making politicians realize the importance of mathematics to society, blah, blah. I didn’t think I was going to get a huge amount of mathematical benefit out of it, but especially writing that very first book,
The Music of the Primes
, I did. I’d grown up knowing that the Riemann Hypothesis was the greatest unsolved problem in mathematics, but it was only writing that book that I realized why, which is kind of crazy, because I do number theory. Yet I needed to write a book about it to truly understand the significance and where it came from, and why it seemed this important. There is also that thing that Hilbert said⁠

MW:
What’s the greatest positive surprise you had when you became known for public outreach?
JE:
The size of the audience and the level of public enthusiasm. All of us who teach in universities spend a lot of time in front of students who, frankly, have not chosen to be there learning math from us. They come to us in good faith, and they do a good job while they’re there. But it wasn’t their choice—somebody made them take our class. That’s a lot of what we do. And so this concept, which I think people in publishing certainly know, that there are lots of people out there who will voluntarily walk into a bookstore and pay their own money to buy a book about math, and will email you and ask questions, that was very heartening. I have, over the years, become slightly radicalized, as somebody who works for a public university, about this idea that our mission cannot just be for the 18- to 22-year olds who are enrolled in our courses.
KD:
The first article I wrote for the
Guardian
(actually the second; they didn’t publish the first but they asked me to try again) was a big hit. BBC television picked it up. They ran a news show for children, and got me to go down to London to do a segment about it. I thought, “I’m speaking to kids all over the UK. This is huge. I’m speaking to them as real mathematician. Even if I come across as an idiot, or at least a bit weird, it’s a positive thing.” I don’t think I did come across like that, by the way. But suddenly I was a math popularizer. I saw it as a positive thing, because I was reaching a huge audience. It did bring negative comments from my colleagues. “Appearing on children’s television!” I was out of step. But things changed within maybe a decade.
It was just me and Ian Stewart back then. Interestingly, I’ve never met Ian in person. We collaborated on the Millennium Problems book. But we’ve never physically met.
SS:
For me a lot of it has to do with elementary school and high school teachers. Similar to Jordan’s answer that there’s a lot of hunger out there. We think of reaching students and drawing young people in. But Sarah made the point that a lot of the teachers have gone through a somewhat sterile test-driven system, and they are very hungry to find all the juicy stuff that we can offer. They will usually write to you. Likewise parents—you could see them as generalized teachers. So it’s not just the kids. It’s the people who are working with the kids, and we can help them do their jobs and just enrich their lives too. I didn’t realize there was such an enormous population of those people who could benefit from this kind of work.
MdS:
I was buying a book in Blackwell’s last week, and a guy overheard me giving my name to the cashier. He said, “Oh, my God! I don’t believe it. I read your
Number Mysteries
book when I was 10 or something, and I just fell in love with the idea of the shape of the universe and trying to understand it mathematically. And now I’m doing a DPhil in Oxford on differential geometry.” He was in tears by the end of telling me this and said “I didn’t realize I was going to be so emotional meeting the person who inspired me.” One of my DPhil students said “I saw you give a talk in New Zealand when I was 12, and I decided I wanted to come and work with you.” He knocked on my door in Oxford and told me this story and said “I want to do a doctorate with you.” Now we’re writing papers together. That for me, is just the most extraordinary thing.
I was taught as a kid by Christopher Zeeman, who gave the Christmas Lectures⁠

JAP:
I’ve received over the years, and still receive, flattering letters from people who say they majored in mathematics because of me, or they really love a particular article. Once I was in Singapore, at Nanyang University, and didn’t know a single soul. A young man ran up to me and said “I loved your books. I was thrilled to see you were going to come.” That is gratifying. I always respond very thankfully. I remember when I was much younger I wrote to Martin Gardner, and was thrilled when he responded to me, and we had a long correspondence, believe it or not, about dirty jokes. So that is a positive aspect of becoming well known—for the right reasons, I mean.
SH:
I agree with what people are saying. If someone comes up and says “you’ve had an effect on me,” that’s very gratifying. Also sometimes, because the nature of my book is about literature and mathematics, I get invited to literary festivals. There are people there who absolutely don’t think that they like mathematics. And then, if you get someone coming up to you at the end, and say “I never liked maths at school, but this is so interesting, and maybe I’m going to be more open to it,” that’s a real positive.
We’ve all had these experiences of people who’ve influenced us, and for me, it was David Singmaster. He happened to be a friend of my parents. My mother had taken evening classes in computing at what’s now London South Bank University. He taught them, and he’d shown her a flexagon, and she was super-interested in this flexagon. They became friends, and he would come to our house every so often. My parents held a summer party, and I thought he was like the summertime Father Christmas. An old man with a big bushy beard, he would come with his pockets full of puzzles and games, and he would talk to you like you were a worthy person to speak with. We’ve all had interactions with people who can’t believe they’re talking to a real professor, and this was my experience as a kid and a teenager—here’s a mathematician, and he’s talking to me, as if we’re equals, about puzzles and games. That is such a valuable thing to experience. So to anyone who’s involved in outreach: you may not realize how you appear to others. You’re an authority figure and expert. People can be really excited to have that conversation, that opportunity. This is a real positive delight of doing outreach.
MW:
What are some pitfalls that people interested in getting into public outreach should know about?
SH:
I think we have to mention this at some point. I don’t know how it is for the rest of you. I, as a woman, sometimes get unwelcome attention. People will send me not nice emails, or on social media. I don’t know if this happens to men as well. Maybe it does. Or maybe you might get people who are super eager to talk with you and meet with you. People will send you their proofs of the Four Color Theorem. That happens to everybody. If you’re a mathematician in any way in the public eye, you will have to think about whether and how to engage. Because if you do engage, they send you their proof of the Four Color Theorem, and you say “your first mistake is on line three,” and then they come back and say, “I’ve fixed that now” …. You have to be aware, especially if you’re a woman, how contactable you choose to be. I think we need to know that if you don’t want to reply to someone’s email, you don’t have to. We’re all very polite, but actually, sometimes it’s better not to reply.
JAP:
I write occasionally about politics, tangentially at least, the mathematical aspects of it. I often get emails from people saying “Stay in your lane, you pointy-headed mathematician. What do you know about that?” Especially from so-called MAGA people. It doesn’t bother me. In fact, I’m kind of flattered that they don’t like me.
KD:
Back in my
Guardian
writing days, I got in a conversation with someone. I think he was a retired school teacher, and there was something about him. I thought “Well, he’s not an idiot. He’s asking some interesting questions. He has some knowledge.” And I engaged in a back-and-forth that went on for several months. Then there was a pause, and then his widow sent me a letter saying “You consoled my husband who was dying of cancer for the last seven or eight months.” I was taken aback. Basically I was his life for those last months. I had no idea. I could have easily dismissed him, but I just kept patiently going back. I’m glad I did.
JE:
I do think Sarah’s point is a very good one. There are certain ways in which being in the public eye is mostly costless for me in a way that it might not be for a woman. And, Sarah, I wonder if you feel like it’s different if you are writing as opposed to making public appearances.
SH:
It probably is less if you are just writing. I have been doing Gresham College lectures. They go on YouTube and get lots of views. It’s well known that women speakers get comments on their YouTube videos that are different from the ones men receive. What I’ve learned from that is: never read the comments. That’s made me a lot happier. That would be my advice. With the written word, certainly you get a lot less interaction. If you’re making videos, or doing TV or radio, you might get some negative stuff. But you don’t have to engage with it. Obviously, I try to if someone emails me with a question or something genuine. I enjoy those conversations.
JE:
I want to complain that, like Sarah, and like probably everybody here, I do get people’s proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis all the time. But now, in the last year or so, people are sending me ChatGPT’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Come on, show some respect! In the old days people would do the wrong proof by themselves—how are people so lazy nowadays?
MdS:
I also advise people not to read below the line because if somebody’s got something interesting, they’ll probably contact you directly. But I’ve always said when I do talks about popularization that you are going to have to get a thick skin. You’re putting your head above the parapet. People will shoot at it. My first article I wrote in the
Times
, I was very excited to get two letters the next day. But they were both abusive, and I had to learn very quickly. I went into college lunch that day, and the historian, who also wrote for the
Times
, said “Oh, I loved your article yesterday. Have you got a letter from Miss Quick yet?” I said “How did you know that?” He said “She writes to me every time I write an article, so you’re just going to have to get used to that.” I remember writing an article and I read comments below the line. It was a massive anti-Semitic diatribe: “Why did you marry a Jew? Why are your children being brought up Jewish?” I was thinking “What has that got to do with this article?” You do have to get a thick skin. They’ll find a way to get to you.
MW:
How do you have to write differently when seeking a larger audience?
SS:
Our profession cares so much about exactitude. Don’t say anything wrong at all. It’s very difficult to write for the public or speak to the public, or do YouTube for the public without saying anything wrong. As a counter to that, I guess I would want to bring up the great probability theorist, Mark Kac, who said “Tell the truth, and nothing but the truth, but not the whole truth.” I think that works pretty well. Don’t lie. But you can leave some things out, and they can be in the notes at the end. I just worry that our training predisposes us to be really tight when it comes to writing for the public, because you do have to make little mistakes. Some people call it dumbing down. I wouldn’t, though, but it takes skill. If you really try to put in all the carping and the caveats that we’re used to, that’s going to be bad writing. It’s really tough to do this well.
JE:
One thing I’ve been doing is trying to figure out how to train academics to do outward-facing writing. I ran a kind of seminar for grad students about this, and now I’m teaching a freshman course. So they’re not academics, but to the extent they’ve learned to write, they’ve learned to write papers for class, and they’ve learned to write in their academic style. One thing I’ve started to understand is that there’s, of course, a process of learning how to do it. But there’s also an unlearning process. One of the things scientists like us have to do is unlearn certain habits of writing which are not bad. They’re effective and correct for the kind of writing that we do professionally, but they’re not appropriate or correct for writing for the public.
Another thing that we value a lot in academic math writing is novelty, and that’s something else I’ve had to learn to let go of. If I never wrote about anything that anybody else had already written about in an expository way, I would never write anything. Because in some sense the ground is well covered. One thing I’ve had to learn is, if I have a new spin, or what I think is an appealing way of expressing something, I’m not going to worry about “did Martin Gardner already do this?” It may have been written before, but the people we’re writing for have not read it before. Every day, there are new people who don’t know mathematics. There’s always a new audience. We wouldn’t write a paper that’s already been written. People may not have read it, but they can go read the original paper that proved that theorem. We’re not going to write that same proof again. I do think that this project of exposition is fundamentally different.
SS:
I tend to think of it more like music. Ella Fitzgerald has her cover, Sarah Vaughan has hers, and I want to hear them both. If Sarah Hart hasn’t done her thing yet on
Moby Dick
, I want to read it.
SH:
This point about precision is a crucial one. It’s enough to say that you’re not giving the details, and we know, if you’re a mathematician reading this, that the theorem has some caveats or places where it doesn’t apply. You can say it’s subject to some criteria, and you don’t need to see what they are.
MdS:
When writing
Once Upon a Prime
, it was the first time I’d had an editor. We had a process where I would put in a lot of detail. And then she’d say “Maybe this could be a footnote,” and I’d agree. And then about two months later, she’d say “Maybe we don’t need this footnote”—sort of a gentle progression.
MW:
How do you avoid becoming the story yourself and overshadowing the content, and should you avoid it?
SS:
I put myself into my first book.
Sync
was about the science and math of how things in the world get in sync spontaneously, and also the math we use to describe that. But because that’s the field that I worked in and still work in, I thought it was appropriate to be part of the story. Not to compare myself to Norbert Wiener—I tried to make it very explicit that I’m just a working mathematician in this field, and maybe the reader would like to see what it’s like in the trenches where you don’t have to be Roger Penrose or Stephen Hawking. You could just be a practicing mathematician. And so it was written partly that way. I did worry that it might be self-aggrandizing. So it’s tricky if you insert yourself. But I actually think it can warm up a story, and it’s appropriate sometimes.
For instance, I was advising a new person to our math exposition world. She was the first head of cryptography at the National Security Agency in the US. Being a woman cryptographer of that stature has got to be part of the story in addition to the math of cryptography. She was very reluctant to put herself in, but I said I really think it would make it a deeper and more interesting story for readers. If you’re comfortable, and if you have a good editor, you can do it in a way where you won’t embarrass yourself. I don’t know whether she’s decided to do that.
I like when people are personal. For instance, John, when you write I always know it’s you writing because you have such a personal voice, and I like it. I like knowing this is a John Allen Paulos piece, and I’m going to get scolded. And you have a really fun idiosyncratic sound that’s delightful. I don’t think you should tone it down, and of course you’re not going to, because that’s who you are and that’s how you write.
JAP:
Well, I appreciate those kind words, and in general I think a certain amount of exposing yourself in your piece helps. I don’t do it for that reason, but I think it does. You could write a great piece and say lemma, theorem, corollary, and no one’s going to read it. But you can go too far. I like him, but I think Neil deGrasse Tyson, for example, sometimes goes too far. He can be too much a part of the story, but that’s my own opinion. In general, I think showing that you’re a person, and you’re writing about mathematics or something tangential to mathematics is helpful. In the same way that video is more engaging than text. You can come across as an unbearable narcissist and self-aggrandizing and monomaniacal. We have to avoid that.
SS:
Keith, could you tell us about your Fibonacci book?
KD:
That was the first time I put myself in a book, and that was Vickie Kearn’s idea at Princeton, because I’d written a book,
The Man of Numbers
, which was a straightforward third-person account of Fibonacci and the introduction of
Liber Abaci
. I was talking about what I did to write that book—going to Italy and talking to the scholars, finding books in the archives, reading the Latin, and really becoming a bit of a historian. And she said “That’s a great story. You were on a quest to understand someone you admired.” I found it very difficult to write and put myself in, and she helped a lot. It got way more readers than the original history of Fibonacci. It was one of my most popular books, and it still is, so putting myself in increased sales dramatically. I felt really embarrassed writing it.
SS:
Aside from the success, are you embarrassed if you look at it now?
KD:
No, I’m not, because I’ve got over that. Part of what was going on was my long-running column, for the MAA, now a blog,
Devlin’s Angle
. That began in 1996, and for the first many years it was third person. Over the years it morphed into a conversation between me and the readers. If you look at the last few years, it really is just me chatting to people. I don’t think people take it as self-aggrandizement or anything. I think it’s just regarded as a conversation. People seem to like it, and it gets posted on school noticeboards. So there is a time, I think, when putting yourself in does make a difference. If you’re using mass media and social media, you have to, to some extent.
SS:
Marcus, I felt like you did that in
The Story of Maths
, the BBC show.
MdS:
For television, if you’re a presenter, then—this word is overused—the journey is very much part of how you generate the narrative. One of the challenges I think, for us communicating a subject is creating a good narrative strand through these topics. Maybe it’s a historical one. That’s a very strong narrative strand. That’s what I use in the
Music of the Primes
. But the
The Story of Maths
uses a geographical journey, literally. Telling the stories of the maths in those locations gave it a really strong narrative backbone. I certainly know that editors love it if you can put some of yourself in a book. It really just brings the reader in, and I totally agree with Keith. It’s not natural to us, I think, to do that, because our articles written for journals are written third person. I think it’s a balance. You don’t want to put yourself too much into it. In my second book,
Finding Moonshine
, I wanted to give people a backstage pass to what it was like to be a mathematician through the story of symmetry. I was very careful just to give people small glimpses, and then to use the big story, but I think getting that balance right is quite tricky, and where an editor certainly can help.
KD:
The most common response when I used to do
The Math Guy
on NPR with Scott Simon was “I love your accent.” I am under no illusion that the reason that show was so successful is you’ve got a guy on American radio with a Yorkshire accent. There was the chemistry between me and Scott, and it sounded like two guys chatting, even though we were in studios thousands of miles away and had never met (we did eventually become friends). It worked for a couple of reasons. One was we were just chatting, and of course it was all recorded and then they would cut out Scott’s really wild jokes. If you heard the outtakes from Scott Simon, he would never be on NPR again. That really worked because the chemistry was good. Scott Simon is brilliant at what he does, and the foil has a Yorkshire accent, so insofar as my accent is me, that was what it was about. If I just had a standard US or English accent, I don’t think that would have worked as well.
SS:
I was just thinking of Cedric Villani’s book
Birth of a Theorem
, where a top-flight mathematician, a Fields Medalist, is taking you, as Marcus said, backstage. He was very much in the book, he and his collaborator, but I felt it appropriate for that story. So I think it’s just a question of taste. If a person has good taste, they can be in the book or the movie or the series as much as they should be.
MdS:
I’m just going to add Ed Frenkel’s book as well. I appreciated hearing some of his backstory in reading about the Langlands program.
KD:
The Double Helix
was a great read, because you could see the competition, the ambition, the whole story about Crick and Watson.
SS:
The nice thing is that Watson shows you what a jerk he is throughout that book. There’s no pretending he’s any better than he is. But it makes a great story.
MW:
How do you tailor your approach to different media?
MdS:
I cut my teeth writing articles for the newspaper and I had an editor who was very patient, who explained to me that I didn’t know how to write for newspapers, and that I put the best story at the end in the last paragraph, and nobody reads that. She taught me the pyramid shape. Then I started writing a book, and my editor said “Oh my God, you’re shouting on every page. Everyone’s in this for the long haul, please just calm down.” One does have to learn different things for different media. Radio and television are very different. Radio is a richer environment because it’s so much more abstract. It’s perfect for mathematics, in a way, because you can talk about things which aren’t visualized. Television is a team sport, and so you have to be much more open to many people’s inputs. That’s exciting, but also challenging as well. You have to learn that you have to say things three times: “I’m about to tell you, I’m telling you, I have just told you.” I got very tired of television as an incredibly tedious medium. But it’s a very important medium, because it reaches so many people.
Live performances have a different quality to them. Maths isn’t a spectator sport, as somebody said. Try to find ways to involve your audience so they’re not passive. One of the things I’m most proud of is doing an event at the Royal Opera House about the
Magic Flute
. It was an interactive operatic event, where we explained the mathematics that was hiding behind the
Magic Flute
. It involved people coming up and doing things, creating a chaotic kind of dynamic exercise talking about parity. I still get people coming up to me years later, saying “I was at that
Magic Flute
event, and I still remember it, and it was because the audience were involved.”
JAP:
One platform I haven’t mentioned is BlueSky and Twitter (X). There I think it’s clear that you have to be succinct and punchy, and maybe link to more substantial things. But the initial thing has to be succinct, be punchy, inviting or enraging.

The Clickbait Mathematician
—that’s your next book.

MW:
You’ve talked about editors and how important they are. Can you expand on that?
SS:
It might be something our colleagues don’t have a lot of experience with, because a journal editor usually is just reacting to referee reports. But an editor of a book that you write, or even an expository article in a good outlet, will really shape things. You asked earlier about common pitfalls. Here’s one: if you have a math book in you for the public, don’t write it and then show it to an editor. That’s real amateur stuff. What you do is give them the pitch, and then they buy the book, the idea, and then you write the book. They will shape it so much in terms of “this goes here” or “no, that’s too much—don’t put that in.” Maybe it’s vanity on their part, but it’s not just vanity. They really have good ideas about what will work or not work, at least in my experience.
KD:
All my editors I regard as coauthors. With David Tranah, who did my book
Logic and Information
, I actually said “David, why don’t we just put you as coauthor?” “No,” he said, “I’m the editor,” but it was absolutely a joint work. I find that really exciting, because they bring a very different perspective. I’m working on a book now where my editor is a historian. That’s going to be tricky, because I know he’s going to have really important things to say that will take time to drill into me.
MdS:
All my editors have been historians. For
The Music of the Primes
, when I brought the first draft, my editor said “You may think this makes sense, but you’ve used a scientific narrative line to this, and I don’t know where you are. You’re jumping from the twentieth century to the eighteenth century, back and forth. And I’m seasick from reading this. Most people really understand a historical narrative. Can we write this in a different way, where you use the history, and then allow the mathematics to appear from that?” It transformed the book into something completely different. I was writing for an audience like him. I wasn’t writing for a scientific audience or a mathematical one. Like Steve, I’m really after those people who are maths-phobic, who are terrified of maths, and this editor was absolutely right, that the right way was to follow a narrative that people feel secure with, and then treat the things which they’re much less happy with through that spine.
SS:
I’m curious, because the book seems like it couldn’t have been written any other way. But what did you start with?
MdS:
It made total sense to me. It was a scientific narrative, which very often doesn’t need to be historical. Mathematicians very often start at the end. I think science is taught in quite a historical way, because you see things theories being introduced and overturned, and that’s very helpful way of learning science.
SS:
I remember taking quantum mechanics and suffering through the first few weeks of all the history. I thought “Just tell me what quantum mechanics is! I don’t want all this confusion.”
SH:
One of the things my editor helped me most with is my academic-minded tendency towards completism, in the sense of “here are all the examples of this thing.” There’s a feeling that I need to include every single instance to make my academic case as strong as possible. She said “The people reading this book, they know that you know stuff. You’re not trying to convince them that you know things. You’re trying to tell them an interesting story. Only have an example if it’s different enough from something else, if it’s showing a new facet. You do not need all the examples of the fourth dimension being used as a metaphor in Russian literature. One example of that is fine.” That was a very useful lesson to me. I’ve got all my voluminous notes if I need them. But in the book itself, I’m telling a story, and only things that are useful to that story get to stay in.
JAP:
There’s a quote from Bertrand Russell—actually, a lot of people have made the point. There’s a trade-off between clarity and precision. If you’re writing for a popular audience, clarity is probably more important than precision. With regard to editors, I don’t know. Maybe I’m just pigheaded or given to scolding, as Steve said, but I don’t like to listen to editors, and I haven’t. Maybe that’s a problem, I don’t know.
SS:
That’s interesting to hear from you. It doesn’t surprise me. You have a very strong vision of what you want to do. Maybe you don’t want to hear the editors. I’m always more open to that stuff.
MW:
What do you say to mathematicians who are worried about straying away from their own expertise when communicating with the wider public?
MdS:
I think you start with the area you’re happy with, and find your voice talking about it. If you are going to be involved in this long-term, you have to go outside of your comfort zone, and, in fact, that is often quite rewarding as well. I’m absolutely rubbish at probability. I remember Ian Stewart going on the radio and explaining the Monty Hall problem, but obviously not well enough, because there were so many letters into Radio 4. Ian had gone off to some conference on an island in Southeast Asia and was unobtainable. The next person on the BBC’s list of mathematicians was me, and I was invited on to talk about the Monty Hall problem. I had never understood that problem. But after preparing to go on the radio and talk about it, I now understand it and have a way to talk about it. It’s gone into a TV program I did with Alan Davies. Because I’m the professor for the public understanding of science in Oxford, everyone expects that I must know the whole of science. When I took over this job from Richard Dawkins I used to get people phoning up saying “The Nobel Prize for telomeres has just been announced. Could you explain what a telomere is?” If I’m bad at probability, then biology is really my weak point, but I started by opening the Wikipedia page, and as a scientist you can understand somebody else’s field enough to know what is the way to communicate that to a journalist. I began to realize that the best thing to do is to know the other scientists in my university who are much better placed to answer that question and move the journalist onto that person. It’s a real balance. I think you have to take some risks, but also to realize your own limits.
SH:
There is, of course, the impostor syndrome. I experienced this when Maryam Mirzakhani won the Fields Medal. The BBC clearly thought “the closest female math professor to broadcasting house in London is Sarah, so let’s get her in to quickly explain what Mirzakhani’s work is about.” Even though I’m a pure mathematician, that’s pretty tricky, and I sort of managed to get a rough grasp of what I thought it was. But then to explain it in 12 seconds to a Radio 4 audience, it’s impossible. I almost thought, “No, I can’t do this because I’m not an expert in this stuff.” But then I thought that I don’t need to be the world’s leading expert to be able to give some sort of overview. If you only talk about the thing you really are an expert in, you will have a very short career in outreach.
JAP:
Well, whenever you write or explain, you always have to take into account the people you’re trying to reach, and if what you’re providing enlightens them in one way or another, it doesn’t make any difference that you’re not a world-historical expert in X, Y, or Z, as long as you know more than those people, and can actually help illuminate things for them.
SH:
It’s not that you pass off other people’s work as your own. You give credit to them. One of the pitfalls to avoid is to let people think that you’re passing yourself off as some sort of world expert. You’re there to explain, and you can explain things without being the top person in the field.
SS:
If, as mathematicians, we only confine ourselves to the work that we have done personally, then compare that to the vast ocean of mathematical knowledge and progress! Talk about impostor syndrome. But by being an expositor you give yourself permission to talk about that whole ocean, and so it’s automatically better material than what you could produce on your own. I always find that a great benefit, that I don’t have to talk about my own puny stuff. I can talk about work by the greats. It’s probably worth also saying that exposition doesn’t have to be just about the greats, because there are a lot more mathematicians who aren’t, so to speak, great. And yet they’re doing work that’s very fulfilling to themselves that might be contributing to our subject and to society. I wonder if sometimes we focus too much on the top of the top—the Maryam Mirzakhanis of the world. There is also plenty of room to do exposition of work a notch or two below that, which could also be very beautiful and interesting and powerful.
JAP:
I think we should replace impostor syndrome with expositor syndrome.
SH:
I agree with what Steve said—it doesn’t have to just be the greats. For students it’s very, very important that they hear about people who are mathematicians who are kind of ordinary. We can all contribute. You might hear about the top handful of people, but those people are within a large community. I find it quite useful, when writing or teaching undergraduates, to mention some of the people who are contributing in this way, and who might have done small things. That allows you to represent a much greater diversity of people as well, which is very important.
SS:
This comment might be too far out. I think, speaking of ChatGPT and all that, the day is coming pretty quickly when all of us will be those small people, including the greatest human mathematicians. The best theorems will not be coming from human beings, soon. In other words, if we only confine ourselves to the best of the best, I think we may never be talking about human work at all. So we might as well get used to the idea right now that there’s still value in all kinds of mathematical contributions, including things that might be dismissed as recreational. There’s a whole spectrum of ways that we can be doing math that are fulfilling. It’s not just a matter of churning out the best theorems.
JAP:
I think we should recognize the dispiriting aspect of chatbots and AI in in general. All of the mathematics you learn in high school and college is in a way useless now. I at least find it very dispiriting. Why take pride in knowing something?
SH:
We don’t really need to be able to do long division, right? But somehow, it almost doesn’t matter what techniques you learn because you’re learning how to think. That’s what I console myself with—this idea that the actual choice of things that you learn in a mathematics course is less important than learning how to learn and learning how to think. That’s always going to be important. And also ChatGPT is terrible at mathematics. So for now we’re all right.
MW:
What reaction have you had from colleagues and the wider math community to your outreach work?
KD:
When I started out, there was a lot of negativity about it from my colleagues. I was at the University of Lancaster, and there was still a sense of “you’re sullying yourself.” I used to get all sorts of snide comments and remarks: “Here’s the guy who doesn’t know what a polynomial is.” It was just endless, chipping away. I remember one of my colleagues when I wrote for
New Scientist
saying “You’re writing for
Comic Cuts
again.”
SS:
Have you found it in the years since then? Do you think it’s still true for you today?
KD:
Oh, no, no, no! Stephen Hawking’s book
A Brief History of Time
probably was what changed it. When you’ve got a guy of that stature writing what’s pitched as a popular book (actually, many people didn’t read it) …. But when Hawking came out with his book and really became a big deal, I think that added imprimatur to it. Then Roger Penrose bringing out books. You got the big guns bringing them out, and that legitimized the whole thing.
SH:
There was still some of that, in the 1990s and 2000s, when people would say “don’t get sucked into doing outreach because you won’t have time for your research.” There’s a slightly disparaging phrase in the UK, a “media don.” If you do things for the public, then there’s a suggestion that you’re doing that because you’re no longer able to do research. And this is the long shadow of G.H. Hardy’s
Mathematician’s Apology
. He says he’s writing the book as an admission of defeat, because he’s no longer able to do brilliant research. There’s the horrible phrase that “mathematics is a young man’s game.” Much as I love that book, he’s got a lot to answer for: “if you can’t do the work any longer then now might be the time to do exposition.”
SS:
I’m very glad to hear you say you think he has a lot to answer for, because, as an applied mathematician, I’ve always felt that way. But also I just think he’s wrongheaded about it. There’s nothing even historically disreputable about doing exposition. Einstein did it, Poincaré, von Neumann. If anything, he’s just an anomaly. Of course, he’s very much influenced by World War One, and the evil that comes from scientists cooperating with military people to kill better. So we could maybe make allowances for his attitude. But to give a contemporary example, think of the effort that Terry Tao is putting into exposition on his blog. He really does a nice job trying to communicate well, about all kinds of things, and at many different levels, too.
JE:
It was something that I worried about a little. That’s why my blog has a somewhat obscure name.⁠
Footnote

It doesn’t say Jordan Ellenberg anywhere on it, precisely because when I started doing it blogging was a weird hobby, and I mixed stuff about math with stuff not about math. I worried “is this going to seem not academically serious?” But I never for a second felt that public writing was a problem for me with my colleagues or with my university. If anything, I think they are extremely pleased at the university level whenever anybody on the inside is doing anything that’s visible in a good way. It seems similar to somebody who truly cares about issues of undergraduate curriculum design. I think most of us are not interested in it, and we’re all grateful to the people who do want to put a shoulder into that, because there’s recognition that it’s important. As a caveat, I will say that I didn’t start doing it seriously until I was a little farther in my career and already had a tenure track job. I think I was writing for
Slate
when I was a postdoc, but that was pretty minor. It wasn’t a major commitment of my time. I was writing a few articles a year. I certainly didn’t write popular books until I already was tenured.

MdS:
I suppose I had a worry when I first started doing this that I was going to get a backlash. Partly, as Sarah pointed out, because we’re living under the Hardy ethos. I was quite amazed by the positive response from my Research Council, and also the Royal Society. I was funded by a University Research Fellowship from the Royal Society, and they have very personal contact with their research fellows. Someone said to me “You know that article you wrote in the
Times
….” I thought they were going to say, “Please stop doing that because you should be doing your research.” And they said completely the opposite: “We want more scientists to step up. We realize that there’s actually a gap here.” In fact, I was invited onto the first Royal Society Committee for Science and Society, which was formed after the Jenkins Report in the House of Lords said that there’s a real crisis because scientists are not taking responsibility to communicate to society about the impact that science is having on politics.
For me one of the most important things is to explain to politicians the importance of science in society, and that has a ripple effect on parents, on education, and then the kids as well. I was very happy that the Royal Society realized this about the same time that I was starting to want to play a role in that, and I’ve had support from them ever since.
There was a point Jordan mentioned about universities. Oxford realizes that it’s a win-win situation, because every time I talk, Oxford gets mentioned and mathematics gets associated with Oxford. I have a position created in 1995 especially to relieve the holder of all their teaching and administration, to enable them to concentrate on outreach. It was one of the first of its kind, but now more and more are being created (I just met the current holder (Kate Biberdorf) of the new position in Notre Dame last week—Steve and I did an event with her online.) Universities are realizing that such positions are quite useful, because, we get some publicity. It probably pays for itself—in the amount of money we generated, for example, to build our new maths department in Oxford, because hedge funds had heard of maths in Oxford, because of stuff I did on television. The university realizes that this is actually worth supporting.
SS:
That’s why it was so interesting to me to hear Keith’s negative experiences. I just don’t feel like it’s happening anymore. You used to hear about it in connection with Carl Sagan, that he didn’t get into the National Academy of Sciences, even though by objective measures, given his work on planetary atmospheres and other things, you would have expected that he would. But he didn’t. He was so successful in terms of being on TV all the time and big movies being made from his work. Also his personality—he did seem to love looking at himself in the camera. I think there was a bit of a backlash toward him.
My department chair at MIT told me “don’t write a textbook as an assistant professor—that shows misplaced priorities. You should be focusing on your research.” So there are a number of signals to say that we shouldn’t be doing this at an early stage.
MW:
Any parting shots?
SH
: One thing I’d say is there’s no need to try and do things that you’re not at home with. You don’t have to do whatever the latest thing is. Start off with something you’re comfortable with, and then you can branch out. I don’t make TikTok videos, and I think if I tried it would be very obvious that I was not relaxed. So I give public lectures, and I write, and I used to be on Twitter before it became a hellscape. Some things come more naturally to people. I’m not going to be a TikToker or an Insta influencer, but Hannah Fry does amazing things on Instagram.
JAP:
You might be more comfortable with something small. It doesn’t have to be a book or writing articles for a magazine or newspaper.
SS:
Even an expository article in the
Notices
. Do it if you want to, but otherwise, do your math.
JAP:
I would also suggest a more expansive view of what mathematics is—some topics can be called philosophy, physics, or public policy. It shouldn’t be conceived in a very narrow way. My first book was
Math and Humor
, and the second
I think, therefore, I laugh
, which is about Wittgenstein’s comment that a good and serious work in philosophy could consist entirely of jokes. If you get the joke, you get the relevant point. You could ease into mathematics. There are lots of areas, and in fact, all of knowledge is in a sense tangential to mathematics. I don’t want to be imperialistic about it.
KD:
There’s a news story within what we’re talking about. The latest thing I wrote for
Devlin’s Angle
got picked up by John Naughton, who writes for the
Observer
. He linked to my article, which was simply about why I started writing for a popular audience in mathematics. He saw that that was an interesting story.
MdS:
I didn’t have a plan to be where I am. I sat next to the features editor of the
Times
at a dinner in Oxford, and he said “Oh, what you do sounds so sexy” after I told him. He said “Write me an article,” and I didn’t, but he came back to the same dinner three years later. There’s an old adage in Oxford that the guests remain the same, but the fellows change. However I was still there. He said “you never wrote me that article,” and I was so amazed that three years later the features editor of the
Times
had remembered what I talked about. I thought “Actually, we do have a responsibility to explain to the public, especially if we’re publicly funded, what we do and why it’s important.” And that evening a lot of journalists said “I think you should write that article because you receive money from the public. You need to tell us what you’re doing with that.” So I felt I needed to do it. That was the slippery slope that started the whole thing.
