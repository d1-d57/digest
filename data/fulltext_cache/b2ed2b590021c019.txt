Citation: Arnav Tripathy
The 2026 Mohammad K. Azarian Prize for Mathematical Reviews Reviewers is awarded to Arnav Tripathy. Tripathy received his PhD in 2016 from Stanford University. Since 2018, he has written 53 reviews for Math Reviews, across an impressive number of areas, including algebraic geometry, symplectic geometry, and mathematical physics. The reviewed articles themselves span all tiers of journals, books, and conference proceedings, including many highly cited works.
Tripathy’s reviews aptly reflect the insight of an early career mathematician with wide reaching interests. His writing style is dynamic, engaging, and often tinged with humor. More than mere summaries, Tripathy’s reviews supply the reader with context, often summarizing a broad research area before drilling down to the contributions of a particular article.
For example, after a technical paragraph introducing a volume on field theory and topology, Tripathy writes, “There are a great many technical adjectives in the above and one of the achievements of this relatively slim text is to fully explain and motivate each and every one in a manner entirely suitable for consumption by early-stage graduate students. One of the most appreciated achievements, however, is the extremely clear articulation of what it means for a mathematician to prove results in mathematical physics.”
Reviewing a paper on mirror symmetry, he writes, “This is a wonderful paper. I could easily imagine a sufficiently abstract thinker disparaging this paper as revisiting ground already well-trodden and understood for decades […] But such a reaction would be missing the point, namely that you don’t really have a feel for a subject until and unless you do serious computations in it, and that is exactly the purpose this paper serves in the case of an excellently chosen example.”
Tripathy’s reviews radiate the positivity of someone who is excited about mathematics, and his appreciation for the work he reviews rubs off on even the nonexpert. In addition, experts will appreciate that Tripathy balances his praise and context with deep technical insight into the papers he reviews. They are a model for communicating the pleasure of mathematics alongside its content.

The Ivo and Renata Babuška Thesis Prize
Established in 2022 by Ivo Babuška, the Ivo and Renata Babuška Thesis Prize is awarded annually to the author of an outstanding PhD thesis in mathematics, interdisciplinary in nature, possibly with applications to other fields.
Ivo Babuška was a Czech-American mathematician whose honors include five doctorates
honoris causa
, the Czechoslovak State prize for Mathematics, the Leroy P. Steele Prize, the Birkhoff Prize, the Humboldt Award of Federal Republic of Germany, the John von Neumann Medal, the Neuron Prize Czech Republic, the ICAM Congress Medal (Newton Gauss), the Bolzano Medal, and the Honorary
Medal De Scientia Et Humanitate Optime Meritis
. Asteroid 36060 Babuška was named in his honor by the International Astronomical Union.
Renata Babuška (née Mikulášek) was Ivo’s wife and partner for 63 years. Renata grew up in Prague, Czechoslovakia, and graduated from Charles University in 1953 with a degree in Mathematical Statistical Engineering. After a career in Czechoslovakia as an educational administrator, Renata worked for different government agencies in Washington, DC as a data and computing management consultant. She liked to point out that behind every successful man is a strong woman and he often said that without Renata, he would not have accomplished all that he did.
Babuška’s work spanned the fields of theoretical and applied mathematics with emphasis on numerical methods, finite element methods, and computational mechanics. His interest in fostering collaboration among mathematicians, engineers, and physicists led him to establish this prize to encourage and recognize interdisciplinary work with practical applications.
Citation: Tianyun Tang
The 2026 Ivo and Renata Babuška Thesis Prize is awarded to Dr. Tianyun Tang of the National University of Singapore in recognition of the outstanding contributions in his PhD thesis titled “Feasible Methods for Solving Low-Rank SDP Problems.”
Dr. Tang works in optimization, with connections to machine learning and data science. His thesis focuses on both theoretical questions and computational algorithms for solving large scale semidefinite programming (SDP) problems. Semidefinite programming (SDP) is a major development in optimization since the 1990s, comparable in importance to the development of linear programming (LP) in the 1950s. SDP extends LP in that the decision variable is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix instead of a nonnegative vector. SDP includes LP as a special case, but the modelling capability and applications of SDP are far wider, encompassing a broad array of problems in science, engineering, and economics. This fundamental and general class of optimization problems has been at the forefront of optimization research in the last two decades with diverse applications in fields ranging from combinational optimization problems in theoretical computer science to wireless communication problems, as well as in quantum computing and polynomial optimization.
Dr. Tang’s thesis addresses the challenges of solving large-scale SDP problems that are beyond the reach of current solvers. The new approach proposed by Dr. Tang is motivated by the desire to exploit possible low-rank property of the optimal solutions to substantially cut down the memory requirement and computational cost when solving large scale problems. As a result, the original convex problem is reformulated as a nonconvex problem by expressing the original positive semidefinite matrix variable as the factorization of a low-rank matrix and its transpose. The resulting feasible set is now an algebraic variety which may contain singular points that must be handled carefully in theoretical analysis and in numerical computation. Moreover, how to obtain the globally optimal solutions of the nonconvex problem is another challenging issue to address. In addition, establishing a tight theoretical bound on the valid rank for the factorized variable is also an interesting theoretical problem to investigate.
Dr. Tang’s thesis addresses the aforementioned challenges through several highly innovative works including Riemannian-based algorithms complete with comprehensive convergence analysis and extremely effective numerical techniques for computing the global solutions of the factorized nonconvex problem.

Chevalley Prize in Lie Theory
The Chevalley Prize was established in 2014 by George Lusztig to honor Claude Chevalley (1909–1984). It is awarded for notable work in Lie Theory published during the preceding six years; a recipient should be at most twenty-five years past the PhD. The prize is awarded in even-numbered years, without restriction on society membership, citizenship, or venue of publication.

Citation: Zhiwei Yun
The award is based on several recent publications: “Minimal reduction type and the Kazhdan-Lusztig map,” published in
Indagationes Mathematicae
; “Endoscopy for Hecke categories, character sheaves and representations,” joint with George Lusztig, in
Forum of Mathematics Pi
; “Functions on the commuting stack via Langlands duality,” joint with Penghui Li and David Nadler, in
Annals of Mathematics
; and “Higher Siegel-Weil formula for unitary groups,” joint with Tony Feng and Wei Zhang, in
Inventiones Mathematicae
.
The cotangent bundle of the flag variety provides a resolution of singularities for the variety of nilpotent elements in the Lie algebra. The fibers of this Springer resolution map are called Springer fibers. The Weyl group does not act on a Springer fiber, but it does act on its cohomology, a fact important in geometric representation theory. In particular, representations of the finite Chevalley group are related to the geometry of Springer fibers via Green functions and the theory of character sheaves. There are three levels of this theory: standard Springer fibers, which are subvarieties in the flag variety of a reductive group; affine Springer fibers related to the geometry of the loop group; and (following Ngô) Hitchin fibers for the automorphic theory. The latter originated in Hitchin’s work on Higgs bundles and were only later connected to Springer theory in Ngô’s proof of the fundamental lemma.
Springer theory is a major topic in geometric representation theory, and Zhiwei Yun is a leading expert. The cited works include outstanding contributions at all three levels of the theory: finite, local, and automorphic.
Kazhdan and Lusztig had defined a map from unipotent classes in the group to Weyl group conjugacy classes, and Lusztig had defined a map in the other direction and stated some conjectures about these maps. In “Minimal reduction type and the Kazhdan-Lusztig map,” Yun introduces new notions of minimal reduction type and skeleta for affine Springer fibers, settling these conjectures in a very satisfactory way.
Irreducible representations of finite groups of Lie-type can be partitioned as a union of subsets or “blocks,” referred to as Lusztig series. The blocks are labelled by semisimple conjugacy classes in the dual group. Moreover, each block can be studied via unipotent representations of endoscopic groups. In their seminal work “Endoscopy for Hecke categories, character sheaves and representation,” Lusztig and Yun gave a categorical upgrade to these phenomena, using a new monodromic version of Soergel bimodules to produce an equivalence between the mixed Hecke category for
$G$
with prescribed monodromy, and the Hecke category for an endoscopic group. This enhances the connection between character sheaves of groups and their endoscopy groups, offering a structural bridge that enriches our understanding of non-unipotent characters and their categorifications.
For a
-
$p$
adic group
,
$G$
cocenters are dual to the spaces of
-
$G$
invariant distributions. Likewise, decomposing cocenters is a dual problem to decomposing the category of representations of G. The cited work of Li, Nadler and Yun on the universal affine Hecke category has reshaped our understanding of its categorical co center. Their fundamental contribution lies in establishing a semi-orthogonal decomposition of this cocenter, effectively categorifying the Newton decomposition of cocenters of affine Hecke algebras. This framework yielded two striking applications. First, they resolved a longstanding conjecture in commutative algebra, proving that the ring of invariant functions on the commuting scheme of a reductive group is reduced. Second, they uncovered a profound connection between the cocenter and the genus-one automorphic category in Betti Geometric Langlands, providing an elliptic realization of affine character sheaves.
In the last cited work, Yun introduces a geometric representation theoretic framework to study the theory of automorphic forms. With Feng and Zhang, they establish a higher Siegel-Weil formula for unitary groups. The Siegel-Weil formula originated with Siegel’s work on quadratic forms, and Weil’s interpretation of that work as a relationship between Eisenstein series and theta functions on the metaplectic group. The Siegel-Weil formula was greatly developed by Kudla and Rallis, with arithmetic versions developed by Kudla and Rapoport, Chao Li and Wei Zhang, and others. The cited work proves a function _eld version of the Kudla-Rapoport conjecture, connecting all derivatives of Eisenstein series to the degrees of special cycles on the moduli stack of hermitian shtukas. This is accomplished by connecting both sides of the formula to perverse sheaves arising from a Hermitian variant of the Springer theory. A key observation was that the special cycles are governed by a version of the Hitchin fibration whose geometry can be described via Springer theory.

Citation: Pablo Raúl Stinga
The 2026 Levi L. Conant Prize is awarded to Pablo Raúl Stinga for his article “Fractional Derivatives: Fourier, Elephants, Memory Effects, Viscoelastic Materials, and Anomalous Diffusions,” published in the
Notices of the American Mathematical Society
70 (2023), no. 4, 576–587. The article traces an arc from the origins of calculus to the modern day applications of fractional calculus, and does so in a way that is inviting and appealing to a wide audience. The story of the article starts with a letter to l’Hôpital from Leibniz, who muses about the idea of a fractional derivative because he “had some extra space left to write” (contrary to Fermat!). While this may have served mainly as a thought exercise at the time, Leibniz was prescient enough to hope that “one day we will come to some very useful consequences of these paradoxes.” Stinga then whets the reader’s appetite by gesturing towards the applications that are hinted at in the title of the article: “Real-world phenomena exhibiting long-range interactions, memory effects, anomalous diffusions, and avalanche-like behaviors are very well described by fractional derivative models.” The article then moves to the work of Joseph Fourier, who formalized the fractional derivative in his work on the heat equation. Once framed in the language of the Fourier transform, the proper definition reveals itself. The article then turns to basic questions: how do we compute the fractional derivative? is it a limit of quotients? which functions have fractional derivatives? Thus begins the technical heart of the article, where the author masterfully explains the nuts and bolts of the theory, highlighting the method of semigroups. At this point the author’s work with Torrea plays a starring role. The author conveys the key idea that—thinking of the independent variable as time—fractional derivatives are “one-sided,” in the sense that they look into either the past or the future. The article culminates in an array of applications of partial derivatives, exemplifying the theme that fractional derivatives have memory. The first application is to population growth. As the author writes: “Populations are influenced by a multitude of factors such as pandemics, migration, cultural changes, natural disasters, social media, etc. In other words, populations have memory.” Another application is to viscoelastic materials, such as pizza dough, rubber bands, and human skin. The article ends with a discussion of anomalous diffusion, which describes the diffusion of lipids and receptors in cell membranes, the travel strategies of wild animals, the sleep-wake transitions during sleep, and the fluctuations of the stock market, to name a few. Stinga’s article is beautifully written and full of insights for mathematicians of all backgrounds. Nominators describe the article as “cleverly written,” “entertaining,” “timely,” and “illuminating.” As one nominator writes: “The theory of non-local operators and its applications is experiencing a justified boom given the many uses for it, and this expository paper presents an outstanding exposition of this.”

Citation: Semyon Dyatlov and Maciej Zworski
Dyatlov and Zworski’s 2019 AMS book
Mathematical Theory of Scattering Resonances
has established itself as the key text on contemporary spectral and scattering theory, magisterially unifying decades of advances into a cohesive, rigorous framework. Resonances, which are defined as complex poles of the scattering matrix’s meromorphic continuation, arise ubiquitously in the mathematics and physics of waves. The spectral interpretation of resonances generalizes discrete eigenvalues to both dissipative systems and to noncompact conservative ones; it has a wide range of application in the physical sciences. On the other hand, resonances are also of central importance in core areas of pure mathematics. In fact, the zeros of the Riemann zeta function appear as the resonances of the Laplacian on the modular surface. Prior to this book, the mathematical literature lacked a comprehensive treatment of modern developments in this important field: the celebrated 1967 book of Lax-Phillips played such a role in its day, but the next installment in the saga was long overdue. Dyatlov and Zworski’s remarkable monograph has filled the void with exceptional clarity, depth, and geometric insight, firmly establishing itself as an indispensable reference for researchers and graduate students in PDEs, microlocal analysis, and mathematical physics.
The book’s core achievement lies in its systematic development of resonance theory across a wide range of settings, from Euclidean scattering to asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds. Dyatlov and Zworski expertly navigate between abstract functional analytic principles and concrete asymptotic analysis. They divide their book into three parts, which form a natural succession but present different perspectives, each of fundamental importance in its own right. The first part presents potential scattering, the second geometric scattering, and the third resonances in the semi-classical limit.
Part 1 establishes foundational tools. Starting with the more explicit and accessible one-dimensional theory, the authors guide the reader through the main notions that are prevalent through the theory, such as outgoing solutions, the meromorphic continuation of the resolvent, the relation between resonances and the scattering matrix, trace formulae, complex scaling, and resonance expansions. They then cover scattering by compactly supported potentials in odd dimensions, basing their analysis on expansions of the resolvent and its meromorphic continuation. Distorted plane waves, trace formulae due to BirmanKrein and Melrose, which play a key role in many settings, Schrödinger parametrices, and scattering asymptotics conclude Part 1 of the text.
Part 2 begins with so-called black-box scattering, which leads to a more unified theory, encompassing a significant class of different operators ranging from Laplacians on surfaces with constant curvature cuspidal ends to obstacle scattering in Euclidean space. This is accomplished by an axiomatic definition of a family of semi-classical unbounded self-adjoint operators on a complex Hilbert space, allowing for a wide class of local modifications of the Euclidean Laplacian. Building on the notions of Part 1, but going far beyond perturbations by potentials, Chapter 4 develops techniques applicable to metric perturbations and scattering by obstacles. In these settings, trapped classical trajectories can appear, leading to a much richer and more delicate scattering theory. The more challenging Chapter 5 presents a major recent advance in geometric scattering theory, Vasy’s approach to the meromorphic continuation of resolvents for even asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds. The methods described in Chapter 5 form the basis of the celebrated proof by Hintz-Vasy ’16 of global nonlinear stability of slowly rotating Kerr-de Sitter black holes under Einstein’s equations. This material, like much of the material on trace formulae and existence of resonances in Part 1, has never previously appeared in the textbook literature.
Part 3 deals with the asymptotic properties of resonances in the semiclassical limit, where a small parameter, which might be Planck’s constant or just the reciprocal of energy, tends toward zero. Near the real axis, the distribution of resonances in this limit turns out to be governed by properties of the underlying classical Hamiltonian flow. Of central importance is the notion of the trapped set of that flow, which refers to initial positions in phase space whose dynamics remain in a compact set. Chapter 6 focuses on the case of mild trapping, especially the case of normally hyperbolic trapped sets. In the final Chapter 7, the authors allow for larger trapped sets which lead to worse resolvent bounds and many more resonances near the real axis.
Dyatlov and Zworski’s exposition combines technical precision with intuition. Complex analysis, spectral theory, and dynamical systems are presented and used naturally, assuming some familiarity with Sobolev spaces, pseudodifferential operators, and symplectic geometry. The writing remains accessible, however, through well-placed heuristics and well-structured exercises that help the reader master advanced concepts. The authors wisely make the text approachable by initially restricting themselves to compactly supported potentials, referring as they go to the more general results to come. Extensive notes at the end of each chapter summarize the material, but also develop it further and place it in a wider context. In these concluding notes, the authors point the reader to numerous other topics with extensive references. Of great pedagogical value are the five appendices. Apart from setting notation and covering basic notions such as distributions, they cover essential techniques in spectral and Fredholm theory, as well as complex and semi-classical analysis. Taken together, the opening chapters of the book and the appendices are almost a text unto themselves, offering a gentle entry into the field for beginning graduate students or well-prepared undergraduates.
For the mathematical community, this book has helped to delineate the frontier in spectral and scattering theory, especially from the semi-classical point of view. It consolidates key elements of the theory, such as the classical Lax-Phillips approach to scattering, Sjöstrand’s microlocal methods, and Vasy’s resolvent expansions on Kerr-de Sitter spaces, while introducing novel perspectives. The numerous applications given throughout the text underscore resonances as universal spectral signatures. Although the authors necessarily excluded topics of importance, they present a large body of material in a unified and elegant package.
In conclusion, Dyatlov and Zworski’s AMS text on Scattering Resonances is a transformative work that elevates the field. Its geometric language and physical motivation, coupled with notable analytical depth, render it essential for spectral theorists, mathematical physicists, and analysts working at the interface of PDEs and dynamics. The text not only captures the state of the art but also maps out the road ahead for future research.

Leonard Eisenbud Prize for Mathematics and Physics
This prize was established in 2006 in memory of the mathematical physicist Leonard Eisenbud (1913–2004) by his son and daughter-in-law, David and Monika Eisenbud. Leonard Eisenbud was a student of Eugene Wigner. He was particularly known for the book
Nuclear Structure
(1958), which he coauthored with Wigner. A friend of Paul Erdős, he once threatened to write a dictionary of English to Erdős and Erdős to English. The prize is awarded every three years for a work or group of works, published in the preceding six years, that brings mathematics and physics closer together. Citation: Simion Filip
The 2026 Gordin Prize is awarded to Simion Filip for his work, which provided completely unexpected solutions to several major conjectures in Teichmüller, complex and smooth dynamics. In his thesis, he solved what was at the time, one of the major open problems in Teichmüller dynamics. Eskin, Mirzakhani and Mohammadi had proved that all of the orbit closures of the SL(2,R) action on the moduli space of surfaces with a holomorphic 1-formare affine manifolds (i.e. given by linear equations in period coordinates). Filip then proved that affine manifolds are in fact quasi-projective varieties, a highly nontrivial statement since the period coordinates are themselves transcendental. This result was important enough to get a very prominent place in Curtis McMullen laudation of Mirzakhani’s work at the International Congress of Mathematicians, where the Fields Medal was awarded.
Moreover, during the course of the proof, which cleverly combines Hodge theory and dynamics, Filip gave a complete algebraic-geometric characterization of an arbitrary orbit closure, which became key to a lot of subsequent work in the area. Filip’s proof of his algebraicity result is spread over two papers (one in the
Annals
, and one in
Inventiones
. In another paper, Filip solves, through a very ingenious proof with highly nontrivial components both from dynamics and from Hodge theory, a conjecture of Forni, Matheus and Zorich about zero Lyapunov exponents in Teichmüller dynamics, that looked completely out of reach when it was first made. Another paper which deserves mention is the joint work with Eskin and Wright on algebraic hulls and finiteness theorems, which appeared in the
Annals
. This computes the Zimmer algebraic hull of the Kontsevich-Zorich cocycle, and as a consequence gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of infinitely many “nontrivial” orbit closures in fixed genus.

This should be the foundation of a lot of further work in the area. In recent years Filip has continued to do outstanding work. Citation: Vadim Gorin
The 2026 Gordin Prize is awarded to Vadim Gorin for his elegant use of algebraic techniques to prove universality results for scaling limits of probabilistic and statistical physics models.
Much of Gorin’s work pertains to probability measures on interlacing systems of particles such as occur in random 2d tiling models, random matrix theory, characters of classical Lie groups, and vertex models. In this context, Gorin, has developed sophisticated generalizations of the Fourier/characteristic function and Schwinger-Dyson loop equation approaches to prove law of large numbers (i.e., limit shapes), central limit theorems (i.e., Gaussian free field fluctuations), and other important fluctuation results.
Gorin proved universality of local correlations for random lozenge tilings of a broad class of polygonal domains, and partial universality for any polygonal domain. This was the first significant progress in a well-known conjecture that originated from the work of Cohn, Kenyon, and Propp over 20 years ago. Subsequently, with Petrov, Gorin proved universality of local statistics for nonintersecting random walks on short time scales. This was a crucial ingredient in Aggarwal’s resolution of the Cohn-Kenyon-Propp conjecture.
Recently, Gorin, along with Xu and Zhang, used his generalized Fourier approach via Dunkl differential-difference operators to construct the long-sought-after edge scaling limit of general Beta Dyson Brownian motion, generalizing the celebrated Airy 2 line ensemble.
In work with Borodin and Guionnet, Gorin introduced the “Nekrasov equations,” a generalization of the Schwinger-Dyson loop equations, and applied them to study asymptotics of a wide class of discrete general beta ensembles. Gorin and Huang, and related work of Dimitrov and Knizel, subsequently introduced a dynamical version of these Nekrasov equations that enabled their study of very general 2d interlacing systems of particles in which these discrete beta ensembles embed as 1d slices.
Gorin has several other influential works. With Borodin and Corwin, he proved Gwa and Spohn’s 1993 conjecture that the stochastic six vertex model has fluctuations determined by the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class, and with Bykhovskaya he applied his expertise in random matrix theory to solve several statistical problems previously posed by leading economists.

Leroy P. Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research
The Leroy P. Steele Prizes were established in 1970 in honor of George David Birkhoff, William Fogg Osgood, and William Caspar Graustein and are endowed under the terms of a bequest from Leroy P. Steele. Prizes are awarded in up to three categories.
The Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research is awarded for a paper, whether recent or not, that has proved to be of fundamental or lasting importance in its field, or a model of important research. The prize is awarded according to the following six-year rotation of subject areas: Open, Analysis/Probability, Algebra/Number Theory, Applied Mathematics, Geometry/Topology, and Discrete Mathematics/Logic.
Citation: László Erdös, Benjamin Schlein, and Horng-Tzer Yau
The 2026 Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research is awarded to László Erdös, Benjamin Schlein, and Horng-Tzer Yau for their series of three papers (i) “Semicircle law on short scales and delocalization of eigenvectors for Wigner random matrices” (
Annals of Probability 2009
), (ii) “Local semicircle law and complete delocalization for Wigner random matrices” (
Comm. Math. Physics
2009), and (iii) “Universality of random matrices and local relaxation flow” (
Invent. Math
. 2011). These papers develop a beautiful dynamical approach to the universality of the spectrum of random matrices at the local scale. The first two papers give an inductive improvement of resolvent estimates as the imaginary part of the argument shrinks. The third paper, which marks the culmination of their argument, uses these resolvent estimates as the starting point for the dynamical consideration of mixing of local statistics in a variant of Dyson’s Brownian motion. In this way, Erdös, Schlein and Yau succeeded in showing that the limit distributions of spacings between eigenvalues of random symmetric matrices, with entries chosen independently from rather general distributions, are identical to those computed classically for the Gaussian case (namely the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble GOE). (A related universality theorem was established by Tao and Vu drawing on ideas and results from the first two papers of Erdös, Schlein and Yau, and then proceeding by a different method.) The techniques introduced in the three papers of Erdös, Schlein, and Yau have been very fruitful and have led to a full understanding of fine spectral properties for many other ensembles of random matrices, such as those derived from sparse random graphs and related objects.
Biographical note
László Erdös
László Erdös
received his PhD from Princeton University in 1994 under the supervision of Elliott Lieb. After postdoctoral positions at ETH Zürich and Courant Institute, he went to GeorgiaTech, where he obtained tenure in 2001.
In 2003 he accepted a chair professorship at the Ludwig Maximilian University in Munich. Since 2013, he is a professor at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria. His work has been recognised by several awards; he received, jointly with Horng-Tzer Yau, the Leonard Eisenbud Prize of the AMS and the Erwin Schrödinger Prize of the Austrian Academy of Sciences. He was awarded two Advanced Grants of the European Research Council in 2014 and 2021.
László Erdös works in probability and mathematical physics. Prior to his research on random matrices, he worked on the mathematical theory of large atoms in magnetic fields, on the rigorous derivation of quantum diffusion, as well as on many-body quantum dynamics.
Response from László Erdös
I feel deeply honoured to receive this prestigious prize, I thank the Prize Committee and the American Mathematical Society.
My first inspiration for probability stems from Domokos Szász in Budapest, while I learned hard analysis and quantum mechanics from Elliott Lieb. However, it was Horng-Tzer Yau, my postdoctoral advisor, who has introduced me to quantum dynamics and interacting particle systems. Somewhat surprisingly, their combination turned out to be the right approach for the universality question in random matrix theory. With Benjamin and Yau we were extremely fortunate to look into the right question at the right time.
In retrospect, it looks natural to push resolvent methods to their limit to obtain precise a priori bounds on the eigenvalue distribution and then switch to exploit a local equilibration mechanism. While various pieces of these ideas have been around before our work, at the beginning it looked technically almost impossible to understand a single eigenvalue deeply inside the spectrum so accurately as the universality conjecture demands. We gradually realized the extreme power of randomness that helped us overcome all difficulties.
I am extremely grateful to Benjamin and Yau for relentless exchanges of ideas, motivations and support over many years we spent on random matrices and other projects as well.

Benjamin Schlein
Benjamin Schlein
received his PhD from ETH Zurich in 2002, with a thesis written under the supervision of Jürg Fröhlich. After graduating, he held postdoctoral positions at NYU, Stanford and Harvard University, partly funded by an NSF postdoctoral fellowship. Benjamin Schlein works in mathematical physics. Apart from the work on random matrix theory, he is best known for his research in many-body quantum mechanics, in particular towards the derivation of effective equations and the mathematical justification of Bogoliubov theory.
Response from Benjamin Schlein
It is a great honor for me to accept the Leroy P. Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research from the American Mathematical Society. I started working on random matrix theory with László Erdös and Horng-Tzer Yau in 2002, immediately after arriving as a postdoc at the Courant Institute. At the beginning, however, we did not make much progress and we soon switched to a different subject. In 2007, we came back to random matrix theory, working intensively on the spectral properties of Wigner ensembles. This led to the series of works that is being recognized with the Leroy P. Steele Prize, first establishing local laws and delocalization of eigenvectors and then proving universality of local correlations.
I would like to thank all mathematicians that nominated our work and the committee that selected us for this prestigious award. Moreover, I am very much indebted to my collaborators László Erdös and Horng-Tzer Yau, for everything I learned from them, on random matrix theory and on all other subjects we worked on.
Biographical note
Horng-Tzer Yau
Horng-Tzer Yau
is the Merton Professor of Mathematics at Harvard University. He received his BS from Taiwan University in 1981 and his PhD from Princeton University in 1987 under the supervision of Elliott Lieb. After postdoctoral work with Michael Aizenman and S.R.S. Varadhan at New York University, he held faculty positions at NYU and Stanford before joining Harvard.
Yau’s research covers mathematical physics and probability theory, including quantum dynamics, partial differential equations, interacting particle systems, and random matrices. He delivered the Morse Lectures at the Institute for Advanced Study in 2022 and will be a plenary speaker at the 2026 International Congress of Mathematicians. His honors include the Packard Fellowship (1991), MacArthur Fellowship (2000), Poincare Prize (2000), Simons Investigator Award (2012), and Eisenbud Prize of the American Mathematical Society (2017). He is a member of Academia Sinica and the US National Academy of Sciences.
Response from Horng-Tzer Yau
I am deeply grateful to my coworkers, the prize committee, and the AMS for this prestigious honor. My research began with Lieb and Varadhan, studying quantum mechanics and probability. For years, we struggled with the delocalization conjecture for random Schrödinger operators. In 2002, Spencer’s work on band matrices drew our attention to the connection between random Schrödinger operators and random matrices.
In 2007, when the three of us were at Harvard and feeling stuck in every direction, we asked a playful question: if we wanted to show that random Schrödinger operators behave like random matrices, why not start by truly understanding random matrices first? That turned out to be the best question we ever asked. At the time, the excitement at Harvard surrounding the flow methods used in the Poincaré conjecture was palpable. We asked ourselves whether the idea of “flowing to universality” could make sense in random matrices. This led us to study Dyson’s Brownian motion via the logarithmic Sobolev inequality—an important tool in probability theory and Poincaré conjecture.
I am profoundly thankful to the many mathematicians who have advanced the interests of this field—for example, Ben Arous, Guionnet, Johansson, and Sarnak—and to my students and postdocs, especially Bourgade, Huang, Landon, and Yin.
Leroy P. Steele Prize for Lifetime Achievement
The Leroy P. Steele Prizes were established in 1970 in honor of George David Birkhoff, William Fogg Osgood, and William Caspar Graustein and are endowed under the terms of a bequest from Leroy P. Steele. Prizes are awarded in up to three categories.
Presented annually, the AMS Leroy P. Steele Prize for Lifetime Achievement is awarded for the cumulative influence of the total mathematical work of the recipient, high level of research over a period of time, particular influence on the development of a field, and influence on mathematics through PhD students.
Citation: H. Blaine Lawson, Jr.
The Steele Prize for Lifetime Achievement is awarded to H. Blaine Lawson, Jr. for his groundbreaking contributions to differential geometry, topology, and analysis. Lawson’s fundamental research and expository monographs have been a boon to the mathematical community, launching new directions of research and illuminating large areas of mathematics. His work on minimal varieties included his brilliant construction of compact minimal surfaces of every topological type (other than the projective plane) in the 3-sphere. His construction of codimension-one foliations on spheres brought tremendous excitement and changes to the field. His paper with F. R. Harvey on boundaries of complex analytic varieties was a major result in several complex variables. His longtime collaboration with F. R. Harvey on the theory of
calibrations
inspired profound developments in Riemannian holonomy and generalizations of potential theory for fully non-linear partial differential equations. His work with M. Gromov on the Dirac equation and positive scalar curvature was a milestone in Riemannian geometry that has inspired much subsequent development. His work on the topology of algebraic cycles and cocycles, particularly the development of Lawson homology, has been crucial in deepening our understanding of subvarieties and Chow varieties and is the focus of continuing research. His 1990 book
Spin Geometry
, written with M. L. Michelsohn, has been an invaluable reference for a generation of geometers, as have his other expository works on such subjects as minimal submanifolds, gauge theory, and foliations. Throughout his career, Lawson has been generous in sharing his knowledge and insights with students and colleagues at all levels. His mentorship of his many graduate students and postdoctoral fellows has been of enormous value to the current generation of geometers.
Biographical note
H. Blaine Lawson, Jr.
H. Blaine Lawson, Jr.
received his PhD from Stanford University in 1968 as a student of Robert Osserman. His work in differential geometry covers a wide range of topics in geometry, topology, and analysis. He is a Distinguished Professor of Mathematics at Stony Brook University. He has been a Sloan Fellow, a Guggenheim Fellow, a Fellow of the Japan Society for Promotion of Sciences, a Hardy Lecturer of the London Mathematical Society, an Aisenstadt Chair at the Centre de Recherches Mathématiques of Montréal, and was several times a visiting member at the Institute des Hautes Études Scientifiques. Foliations
in the Bulletin of the AMS was awarded a Steele Prize. He is a member of the National Academy of Sciences, the Brazilian Academy of Sciences, and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. Journal of the AMS
as well as many other leading journals in mathematics.
Response from H. Blaine Lawson, Jr
I was completely amazed and thrilled upon receiving notice of this prize, and I want to express my deep and humble gratitude to the AMS. My journey in mathematics has been a wonderful personal experience: rapture, woes, absolute involvement—but most of all, the people.
Already in my second year as a post-doc at Berkeley I met two amazing graduate students: Shing-Tung Yau and Bill Thurston—it was exciting! Yau wrote his thesis that year and a paper with me, and I enticed Bill into foliations. Bill’s work quickly subsumed my construction of foliations of spheres, which itself had gotten some notice (the Brittanica Yearbook). I found Bill’s work wonderful, and writing about it earned me the Steele Prize.
My longest collaboration (nearly 60 papers) has been with Reese Harvey. Our results on Calibrations became, to our joy and surprise, of importance in many areas, including modern physics. Over time we have considered many, many different things in analysis and geometry. One of the earliest was a result on boundaries of complex varieties, which I have always liked.
Coming to Stony Brook began exhilarating mathematical journeys with Misha Gromov and with Marie-Louise Michelsohn. Michelsohn and I also wrote a book, whose typed drafts we carried around in a suitcase for years. I proved a result on algebraic cycles which led to much subsequent collaboration with Michelsohn, Friedlander, Lima-Filho, dos Santos, and many others.
My having this award depends so much on these people. I am profoundly grateful to them.
