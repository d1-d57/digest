Background

Typical high school curricula consist of courses called Algebra I, Geometry, Algebra II, and Precalculus or Math I, II, III, and IV, a series of courses that integrate mathematical content areas within and across the courses. Approximately 
$24\%$
 of students in grade 8 are enrolled in a middle school course called Algebra I 
26
, and in states with standards similar to the Common Core State Standards in Mathematics 
4
, key algebraic concepts are included in eighth grade mathematics. More than eighty percent of US high school students take a course called Algebra II sometime before they graduate 
16
; however, such courses often have different names (e.g., Advanced Algebra, Algebra II, Honors Algebra II), which often signal a different emphasis on content depending on the background of the students. At the spring 2025 National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM) conference where display copies of commonly used high school textbooks were available, I browsed and took notes on textbooks to get an idea of how different ideas were treated. I also reviewed online texts. Based on my observations, most first-year algebra courses cover foundational algebra content such as equivalent expressions, linear equations, inequalities, introduction to functions, systems of equations, exponents, quadratic polynomials, radicals, and rational expressions. Approximately one fourth (and in some cases more) of the second-year algebra curriculum is focused on reviewing linear and quadratic equations and inequalities, multiplying polynomials, solving systems of equations, radical and rational expressions, with some extensions such as factoring cubic polynomials or solving a system of three equations in three variables. The remaining content varies widely but usually has different emphases related to exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions with a few texts attending to sequences or statistics and probability in the last chapters.

While several texts develop both conceptual and procedural understanding with carefully chosen exercises, some focus primarily on practicing skills (e.g., “simplify” 
$\frac{2x^{-7/4}}{4x^{4/3}}$
;
 “factor” 
$-90x^3+100x^2+60x$
,
 taken from a supplemental first-year algebra worksheet). It seems the dominant conception is of mathematics as 
products
, a body of static knowledge and skills that need to be learned and acquired 
14
. For too many students, mathematics appears unrelated to their world and often boring 
8
22
, and, despite an emphasis on preparing students for high stakes tests, scores declined on the NAEP 
15
, ACT 
1
, and SAT 
5
. Failure rates in second year algebra are often high 
24
.

Throughout history, mathematics grew as a tool to make sense of the world, from numbers to serve commerce, geometry to capture characteristics of the spaces in which people lived, algebra as a language for generalizing solution paths, calculus to deal with change and motion, coordinate geometry connecting geometry and algebra to analyze the heavens. And because these ideas were important for dealing with the world in which people lived, the basic concepts underpinning the ideas became part of the mathematics taught in schools and, in the US, under the guise of consistency and ease of calculation, led to stances such as all answers much be reduced (e.g., 
$4/8$
 had to be written as 
$1/2$
),
 “simplified” 
$(3(x+2)-4$
 had to be written as 
$3x+2$
)
 or without a radical in a denominator.

A World of Data

Today we literally live in a world of data, a world that is essentially missing from the school curriculum. Advances in technology have opened doors to collecting, organizing, and analyzing vast amounts of data, which are then used to drive decisions framing both policies and actions in every field from economics to health to entertainment to government. Students need to see that collecting and analyzing data can be used to solve real problems such as those involving climate change, bias in policy decisions, health care, and the use of resources. They need to understand that modeling is the process of using mathematical and statistical concepts, equations, and data to create representations of real-world phenomena. These models help us describe, understand, predict, and control various systems, from the physical and biological to the social and economic 
3
. Yet while modeling has been promoted in standards documents (e.g., Common Core State Standards Mathematics) and in curricular documents (e.g., 
17
18
), and while resources to support teachers and professional developers in using and learning modeling are available,⁠
Footnote
2
 modeling remains largely absent from mainstream high school mathematics courses.
2
For example, see the QUBES hub resources page: 
https://qubeshub.org/community/groups/mmhub/resources_page
.
✖

Students in today’s world need to know essential concepts from traditional mathematics 
17
 and how to work with data, but they need to know more. They need to understand risk, what a false negative might mean for their health, that percentages do not make sense unless you know the base, the difference between absolute and relative frequency, that the average (mean) should never be given without a measure of variability (an increase in the mean with an increase in standard deviation indicates an increasing gap), and that correlation does not imply causation. They should have experiences with randomness, understanding that probabilities stabilize over the long run, and recognize sampling variability, the impact of sample size and the role of chance in making inferences. To be informed citizens, they should know the questions to ask when given statistical results and how to make evidence-based decisions. Yet only 
$17\%$
 of high school students take a statistics course 
16
. While some statistical ideas might be part of the traditional courses, they are most often at the end of the course and most likely to be omitted.

Perhaps it is time to broaden the focus of high school mathematics 
18
, harnessing technology and contemporary applications. Key mathematical content should not be discarded. But the curriculum, the structure whereby content is presented to the students, must be (in the words of 
18
) “reimagined” to include content that is relevant to preparing students to live and work in a world of data. This article argues that 1) core mathematical concepts are still important for all students to learn; 2) but we should also be teaching what students need to understand and to make sense of a data driven world; 3) and we can capitalize on the affordances of technology in the teaching and learning of mathematics to accomplish these goals. To do so, the article first discusses how technology, from interactive software to artificial intelligence, should change the kinds of questions on which mathematics courses focus. Then, using three example activities, the article illustrates how it may be possible to integrate concepts from mathematics, statistics, data literacy, and data science. As Schoenfeld and Daro write in 
21
, “A forced choice between algebra II and data science serves no one: Ideas and content from both areas are likely to be needed in future curricula” (p. 1298). Note that while how instruction is carried out is critical in developing students’ mathematical understandings, the focus here is not on the pedagogy (how the content is taught) but the content itself (what is being taught).

School Mathematics in a Technological World

The expanding power of technology raises the question: Are we teaching what is important to teach?

Consider how interactive dynamic technology can support student learning. Sliders can help students investigate holes, asymptotes, and end behavior of a graph. Tables that interact with graphical representations can help students conceptualize what it means to say the rate of change is decreasing or to discover that the rate of change of a cubic polynomial is a quadratic. Exploring graphs of functions can help students connect symbolic representations to their graphical representations. Dynamic geometry software can engage students in exploring shapes and their relationships. Mathematical action technology allows students to connect to the world by investigating problems involving large, often messy data sets related to real issues in society (see examples below). In addition, the technology can open doors for students, offering opportunities to build positive student thinking about mathematics 
2
.

Humanity has a long history of creating devices to “do the work” in mathematics, from an abacus, to Napier’s Bones, to slide rules, to calculating machines, to today’s computers. In 1685, describing the value to astronomers of the hand-cranked calculating machine he had invented, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz is quoted as saying, “It is unworthy of excellent men to lose hours like slaves in the labour of calculation which could safely be relegated to anyone else if machines were used” 
23
. Advancing technologies have not only opened the world to data but can carry out complicated algorithms with the push of a button, perform complex mathematical operations, create unique graphical representations, represent the results of a computation graphically, prove (and disprove) theorems 
9
25
. Yet, most high school mathematics involves only minimal use of mathematical action technology, that is, technology that can “perform mathematical tasks and/or respond to the user’s actions in mathematically defined ways” 
7
. Aside from graphing equations and using sliders to investigate parameters, mathematical action technology has been mostly used to do what we did by hand and after we learn to do it by hand 
19
.

There have been a few tweaks; characteristics and mantissas are no longer taught as ways to calculate with logarithms, nor is a procedure for finding square roots by hand. On the other hand, consider expanding and factoring quadratic polynomials. Since the 1960s, this topic has taken up at least one chapter in typical first- and second-year algebra textbooks, and those books contain hundreds of expressions contrived to be factorable. But to what end? Factoring as a skill is emblematic of the changes that are needed. Factoring is a key mathematical concept that allows us to decompose functions into polynomials of a lesser degree, identify roots, and describe the behavior of graphs. But the emphasis in textbooks, at least since the 1960s to the present day, is on quadratic polynomials. I suspect that we incorporated factoring quadratics into the curriculum because they were all we could do by hand. The Greeks factored quadratics using geometry; Indian mathematicians used numerical approximation methods. We can factor selected quadratic polynomials (and higher-degree polynomials that have certain characteristics—such as a common factor—by using some rules and tricks, and thus was born the need to teach factoring quadratics. It is worthwhile for students to do some factoring of quadratics or carefully selected higher-degree polynomials by hand to understand how the ideas work. But today we have the machines Leibniz called for that can factor a polynomial of any degree at the push of a button. So, there is no reason in our current context to focus only on factoring contrived polynomials by hand. Instead of spending time factoring expressions in functions such as 
$g(x)=4x^3+12x^2-x-3$
,
 students can graph 
$g$
,
 identify the zeros, and explain how they know that 
$(x+3)$
 is a factor, but 
$(x-3)$
 is not. They can answer questions involving factors such as what do each of the following equations tell you in terms of the number of items sold, 
$x$
,
 and the profit earned, 
$y$
 for a particular company? a) 
$y = 2x^2-8x-24$
;
 b) 
$y=2(x+2)(x-6)$
;
 c) 
$y=2(x-2)^2-32$

In the area of rational functions, my observation is that high school textbooks tend to emphasize steps to manipulate equations into two forms: 1) 
$r(x)= \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$
 and 2) 
$r(x)=f(x)+ \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$
,
 where the degree of 
$p(x)$
 is less than the degree of 
$q(x)$
.
 But a focus on steps obscures a potentially more useful instructional focus: interpreting and using these forms rather than focusing on how to get expressions into those forms. Students can use interactive dynamic technology to divide polynomials or combine complicated rational polynomials without the need for manipulations involving synthetic division or searching for least common polynomial denominators.

Even solving equations can be examined from this perspective: do approaches that give students a set of steps to follow, or have them “undo” operations, or “isolate the variable” generalize to a third-degree polynomial (consider the function g above)? Can “isolate the variable” be used to solve a quadratic or an exponential equation? And rather than promoting a step-by-step approach to solving an equation, helping students first understand that finding a solution involves a search for replacements for the variables that make the equation a true statement can engage them in thinking about the mathematics. For instance, such a shift could encourage students to solve equations by examining structure (e.g., 
$\sqrt {3x+5}-4=1$
),
 analyzing a graph (e.g., 
$\sqrt {2x-3}=x-3$
),
 or reasoning about mathematical relationships (e.g., 
$((x^2-5x+5)^{(x^2-9x+20)}=1$
.
 (Note that not all tasks have to be context related. Tasks that engage students in thinking and reasoning about mathematics can be interesting and motivating; for example, consider Glenn Stevens’s PROMYS program at Boston University.)

Examining the content in typical mathematics courses from a technology lens—what can be better done by technology—can free students to think about the mathematics rather than the mechanics (e.g., “What happened to the negative sign?”). It is also important to think about what “by hand” skills all students should have, for example with beginning algebra skills. Students need some degree of fluency with topics such as solving linear equations or simple systems of equations, and using slope to graph a line 
17
. The new face of mathematics should address both questions: What mathematical understandings are important given that a machine can do almost any algebraic procedure? How adept should students be with pencil and paper manipulations?

Any discussion of using technology in the teaching and learning of mathematics in today’s world would be incomplete without acknowledging the existence of artificial intelligence (AI). Algorithmic software such as 
Symbolab
$^{\mathrm{TM}}$
 or Photo 
Math
$^{\mathrm{TM}}$
 can do many of the problems on a typical homework assignment or state assessment without making an error: e.g., simplify 
$\frac{x}{x^2-9}-\frac{3}{3-x}$
 (Symbolab used 
$(x-3)(x+3)(3-x)$
 as a common denominator and reduced) or solve 
$f(x) = \sin (2x)-\cos (x)$
 for 
$f(x)=0$
,
 in this case giving the solution, the mathematical process that leads to the solution, and a graph of 
$f$
 with the solutions marked. From one perspective, the software can serve as a way for students to check their work and verify their thinking, which can be important in building student confidence and understanding. However, to ensure that students are engaging in thinking about the mathematics rather than giving answers provided by software, many of the tasks provided in typical curricular resources need to be reframed. The emphasis should be on probing students to think about why procedures work, concepts, underlying structures, and explanations rather than on manipulating symbols. Such problems may involve critiquing an AI solution (Is there an alternative approach to the solution?), making comparisons (Which hang glider will go farther and why?), investigating structure (What values of 
$a,b,c$
,
 and 
$d$
 in 
$\frac{(x-a)(x-b)}{(x-c)(x-d)}$
 will produce a rational function with an asymptote?), identifying and explaining connections (How are the triangular numbers related to Pythagorean triples?), or using multiple strategies for finding solutions.

The free versions of Large Language Models (LLM) such as 
ChatGPT
$^{\mathrm{TM}}$
 or 
Claude
$^{\mathrm{TM}}$
 are often error prone but are rapidly improving and becoming increasingly better at replicating mathematical reasoning 
12
. Students will not ignore these as potential “answer getters” nor should teachers. Generative AI can serve as a valuable learning tool, providing opportunities for students to pose questions and to carefully read the answers to ensure they are correct. Often a first question does not elicit an answer, which means students have to rephrase the question or be more explicit in the wording they use. The ways in which ChatGPT responds can provoke useful and meaningful discussions about the vocabulary it uses and the approaches it takes. These can also engage students in thinking about overarching concepts: how does ChatGPT describe the role of the symbol 
“
$p$
”
 in statistics or what are the advantages of writing a quadratic equation in different forms. Mathematics educators should recognize that AI will continue to evolve, forcing us to think deeply about which mathematical ideas are important and how students should come to know them.

Changing the Face of High School Mathematics

NCTM’s 
Catalyzing Change in High School Mathematics

17
 called for rethinking the high school curriculum and for examining who has access to which mathematics courses. Since then, others have taken up the call. Some support increased use of technology 
18
 and doing away with “anachronistic, computation-heavy topics that are no longer relevant in the computer age” 
11
 to free a year that could be dedicated to data literacy, statistics, or courses such as financial literacy. Dana Center’s Launch Year Initiative is focused on multiple pathways through the last two years of mathematics and providing support for the transition from high school to college 
6
. Others argue for a complete reframing of the high school curriculum (e.g., 
18
21
). A common theme across these calls is that high school mathematics should maximize outcomes and opportunities for all students. No matter how the high school sequence may be changed, “Deciding when and how specialization occurs in high school is dense with trade-offs, risks, and benefits” 
21
, 
p. 1298
.

While rethinking the entire high school sequence is ultimately my goal, one way is to begin with second-year algebra. Such a course could consist of four major themes:

•

functions (linear, quadratic, polynomial, exponential, trigonometric), including rate of change and graphical representations, building vocabulary and notational fluency;

•

quantitative reasoning with an emphasis on “grown up” contexts for percentages, ratio, fractions, and number;

•

statistics including using the statistical investigative process, working with new types of data, innovative graphical representations, sampling, and informal inference;

•

modeling, both mathematical and statistical, using real data to answer real questions.

These themes should not be isolated but connected, where modeling with functions (i.e., algebra) is integrated with data, and the mathematics would include work with data and computation 
21
. The course would not just add data to traditional content but would involve working with spreadsheets to manage data and making use of simple programs to facilitate investigations with explicit attention to the data investigative process: frame the problem, consider/collect data, process data, analyze data, explore and visualize data, consider models, communicate and propose action. Analyzing and visualizing data would include core statistical concepts related to distribution, sampling, variability, and statistical modeling. Three examples of activities in such a course are described below (see Mathematical Modeling 
https://education.ti.com
).

Example 1: Median Incomes
.

An open-ended activity engages students in investigating the median income for workers 15 and older given data on gender, race, occupation, education, and location from 1960 to 2020. First-year high school students can answer a question such as “Will men’s and women’s median income ever be equal?” by looking at the differences in income or ratios of women’s income to men’s income over time or by plotting both men’s and women’s income with respect to time, fitting trend lines and looking for the intersection. When comparing the situation where there is no difference 
(
$y=x$
 line) to the actual data, they are working with linear inequalities. When asked to investigate a question that they care about, some students look at the slopes of regression lines to find the impact of race or education. Advanced students experiment with nonlinear models, examining residuals and, depending on their statistical background, some create confidence intervals for the slope of their regression equations 
2
.

Example 2: Modeling 
$CO_2$
 Levels
.

In this activity students use data on 
$CO_2$
 levels collected from the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii from 
$1958-2023$
.
 The yearly mean levels seem approximately linear, but the residuals show a pattern indicating a linear model is not appropriate. An exponential model, while a better fit, still has a pattern in the residuals. The monthly mean 
$CO_2$
 levels show a cyclic pattern, and a sinusoidal regression fits the data for each year fairly well. For example, the mean 2006 levels could be modeled using 
$2.812\sin (0.613x - 1.211) + 382.038$
,
 and 2022 levels could be modeled using 
$2.5888\sin (0.7172x - 2.0461) + 421$
,
 with residuals that suggested good fits. Students can analyze the period and amplitude for each year and observe that the constant term, which can represent the mean amount of 
$CO_2$
 in the atmosphere, is increasing. Because two patterns of change are evident in the data, one reflecting cyclic variation across the seasons of the year, and another reflecting an overall general increase in the yearly mean 
$CO_2$
 levels, a challenge for students is to create a model involving both changes. One such model is 
$f(x)=57.0155e^{0.01624(t-1958)}-2.8426\sin (2\pi \cdot 1.0005(x+23.339)) +256.625$

10
, 
§3.1 & §4.3
. Investigating how to use this model for predicting (assuming the trend continues) when the 
$CO_2$
 level will be greater than 1,000 ppm, the danger level for humans, can involve solving complicated equations.

Example 3: Exploring Medical Test Results
.

The pandemic raised public awareness of ideas related to risk with terms such as false positives and false negatives in the media. An activity to build student understanding of such terms and how they are connected to conditional probability involves using simulation to investigate the difference between the efficacy and the effectiveness of a flu vaccine. Students move from acting out the situation to generating random numbers to represent those likely to catch the flu based on historical data and those likely to have positive and negative outcomes from the vaccine based on the information provided by the manufacturer of the vaccine. A two-way contingency table is used to organize the results, and students are asked to consider the probability of a false positive or negative and to find the probability they have the flu given they test positive. Using an app, they can run the simulation many times to estimate confidence intervals for the probabilities and also change the efficacy rate and the initial probability of having a given disease as well as the number of people given a vaccine.

Understanding and interpreting these ideas are critical in health care in deciding whether to take steps to treat a condition based on the results of a screening test, a decision that is problematic even with medical professionals 
13
. In addition to learning about probability, students learn about variability, rates, and percentages and to think about the impact of having tests with high false positive or false negative rates, both in their own lives and for the public. (Is it better to have many people who have a disease but don’t know it or many people who don’t have the disease but think they do?)

Teaching with Data

Engaging students with real data involving many variables (not just 
$x$
 and 
$y$
)
 from relevant contexts requires them to think conceptually about how variables interact, what patterns emerge, and how to interpret and communicate the results. Students can have the freedom to explore their own questions related to the data or work on tasks that are often open ended with no exact solution. Thus, teachers and students have to deal with uncertainty and variability by approximations and statistical reasoning, making it impossible to post a list of homework solutions for students to check. Class time is not typically spent watching someone do the work; “lectures” are carefully staged to help students see where new thinking might take them and to bring ideas together. Students and teachers learn to value discussion and sharing within a community of learners who attend to each other’s ideas as valuable resources for their own thinking 
2
. Handling many variables by hand is not possible, so students and teachers must have access to and feel comfortable with spreadsheets, statistical software, and using programs. Students may be working on different aspects of the same problem, which means the teacher has to be flexible and ready to adapt to different ways of thinking and different mathematical approaches.

Conclusion

The paper argues for the integration of concepts from mathematics, statistics, data literacy, and data science to give all students the foundation they will need to move forward in any pathway they might choose in a data driven world; suggests a framework for Algebra II; and provides examples that illustrate how this could be done. The examples feature key aspects of data science, with students engaging in hands-on work with data, having to make choices about how to analyze the data, not just apply an algorithm and read out the results. They also highlight the importance of fundamental mathematical skills such as rates, ratios and percentages, solving equations, interpreting graphs and symbolic representations, and developing mathematical models as well as dealing with variability, statistical summary measures, sampling and informal inference, and probability.

The challenges in rethinking high school mathematics are many: helping students and teachers understand that, while theoretical mathematics involves deductive reasoning about numbers and shapes with problems often having precise answers, statistics and modeling deal with numbers in a context characterized by variability and uncertainty, but it is possible for the two to complement each other 
20
; supporting teachers who often lack preparation in statistics and are reluctant to teach in anything other than a tightly controlled context; providing reimagined curricular resources for teachers; overcoming the inertia of tradition, which can value processes and procedures that are no longer relevant; designing learning trajectories that move students from pencil-and-paper computations to utilizing technology; and ensuring that policies and high-stakes assessments align with an integrated curriculum. Incorporating concepts from statistics, data literacy, and data science would not undermine mathematics nor replace it but instead delineate and emphasize important mathematics all students need to learn. It would enable more students to see the value of mathematics, to utilize new mathematical learning technologies that can create new ways of seeing and doing mathematics, and to work with relevant and engaging mathematics. The bottom line is we need to let go of the past and look to the future, or we will continue to prepare students for yesterday’s world and not for tomorrow.
