Memorial Tribute
In Memoriam, Vicki Powers (1958–2025)
Bruce Reznick
Vicki Powers
Victoria Ann Powers was born at the Emory University Hospital in Atlanta on July 28, 1958, almost across the street from the Emory department which hired her three decades later. She grew up in Georgia, Florida, and New Jersey, and graduated with honors from the University of Chicago in 1980, where she was a math major and a track star: at the time of this writing, she is still listed in the UC Top Ten all-time for the 400 meter hurdles. Vicki’s 1985 PhD dissertation, “Finite Constructable Spaces of Signatures,” was written under Alex FTW Rosenberg at Cornell University.
There she met Colm Mulcahy, another student of Rosenberg’s, whom she later married. In time, they had two daughters, Ann Powers and Molly Mulcahy. In 1985, Vicki obtained a tenure-track position at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, but in 1987 gave it up for a similar position back at Emory University, where she was based for the rest of her career.
Vicki was awarded tenure in 1993 and became a full professor in 2006. She was an Alexander von Humboldt Research Professor at the University of Regensburg from 1991 to 1992, a visiting professor at Universidad Complutense in Madrid in 2003, and later served as an NSF Program Officer in Algebra in Ballston, Virginia from 2013 to 2015. In 2014, she completed DC’s Marine Corps Marathon. Vicki served as an AMS Council Member at Large from 2013 to 2016.

.
Vicki Powers, 2018.
She wrote more than thirty refereed research publications, as well as the 2021 Springer monograph
Certificates of positivity for real polynomials–theory, practice, and applications
6
. Vicki supervised four PhD students at Emory: Kathleen Krzastek, Dionne Bailey, Mari Castle (deceased) and Ha Nguyen.
In March 2024, Vicki was diagnosed with ALS, and she died at home on February 2, 2025.
In 2021
6
,
p.vii
, Vicki gave this description of her joyful approach to mathematics:
My obsession with the computational and algorithmic aspects of sums of squares has its roots in two events that happened a decade after receiving my PhD. In early 1995, I read a preprint of the seminal paper
4
by M.D. Choi, T.Y. Lam, and Bruce Reznick that set out the theory of Gram matrices. I was fascinated by the theory laid out by the authors and realized that it led to an algorithm for writing a polynomial as a sum of squares. I decided to compute a small example of a Gram matrix. It took me most of an afternoon to find an example that was simple enough to work out by hand, and then to do the actual computation. When I finally finished my example, and discovered that there were nonobvious ways to write the simple polynomial I had chosen as a sum of squares, I danced around my office with joy. With that, I was hooked on certificates of positivity.
A few months after reading
4
I visited Eberhard Becker at Dortmund University, and he told me about Konrad Schmüdgen’s solution
12
to the multidimensional moment problem in the compact case, and the corollary relating polynomials positive on compact semialgebraic sets to preorders in the real polynomial ring. I found this result, which became known as Schmüdgen’s Positivstellensatz, absolutely beautiful and incredibly mysterious, and I set out on a quest to really understand it; a quest which continues to this day.
From Vicki’s Colleagues
By
Bruce Reznick
Vicki Powers was my best friend in the mathematical world and a wonderful professional collaborator. We always had a great time when we saw each other. I first met Vicki and Colm at a quadratic forms meeting at McMaster University in July 1983, while they were still graduate students. I thought they were fun people and hoped I’d meet them again. Over the years, Vicki and I met at dozens of conferences, as well as visiting each other’s home institutions. In addition to being a highly inventive host and cook, she always knew the interesting restaurants wherever we found ourselves. I had the pleasure of collaborating with Vicki on nine research papers. Working together was delightful: we knew different mathematics and never argued about punctuation (or anything else). I will especially miss her when I am at a conference and she is not there. (Where the hell should I eat?) I miss Vicki and remember our times together with great warmth.
Here is commentary from some of her many friends and collaborators. Parimala Raman is also preparing a memorial article for AWM.
By
Eberhard Becker
In the academic year 1984–1985, I had the pleasure of welcoming Vicki and Colm and their doctoral supervisor Alex Rosenberg to Dortmund University. I was impressed by how intensively Vicki and Colm familiarized themselves with new areas of research and how quickly this resulted in scientific publications. All in all, we had a productive research period.
Vicki’s willingness and ability to adapt quickly to other personal and scientific environments also characterized her later scientific career. She regarded scientific work as an international collaboration, which she actively and very successfully sought out.

By
Marie-Francoise Roy
Vicki’s mathematical style, and the enthusiasm and the clarity of her talks, were admirable and her laugh is truly unforgettable.
Of particular note is her result with Bruce Reznick on Bernstein’s theorem on the representation of positive polynomials
8
. Bernstein’s theorem (1915)
2
is a striking result stating that a univariate polynomial
$P$
of degree
$d$
which is strictly positive on an open interval is visibly positive in the Bernstein basis for a large enough degree: there exists a degree
$D$
(bigger than
)
$d$
such that all the coefficients of
$P$
expressed in the Bernstein basis of degree
$D$
are strictly positive. A contribution of Vicki and Bruce was to study Bernstein’s theorem from a quantitative point of view and to find an explicit value for
.
$D$
Rather naturally, the minimum of
$P$
on the interval plays a role, and since this minimum value can be very small, the certificate of positivity they obtain is of exponential size in some cases. They even provide an explicit example of the situation where
$D$
has to be exponentially big.
The quest for effective certificates of positivity with quantitative bounds remains an active research topic for real algebraic geometers, 25 years after the seminal contribution of Vicki and Bruce. Vicki was one of the most active people in the area, writing a series of papers with distinguished coauthors, and
6
in 2021. Thank you, Vicki for your beautiful contributions to the mathematical edifice. Thinking of what you did and who you were, I feel admiration and gratitude, in spite of the sorrow of your too-early death.
By
Frank Sottile
Vicki and I first met at the 1994 Joint Mathematics Meetings in Cincinnati as I was finishing my PhD, we connected through our interest in real algebraic geometry, and met regularly over the years. She was a great friend whom I will miss.
Of all my collaborations, the one with Vicki was the most genial, exemplifying the joy of friendship and sharing of mathematical ideas (in this case over memorable meals). It concerned David Hilbert’s 1888 paper in which he showed that a homogeneous quartic polynomial
$q$
in three variables (ternary quartic) that is positive
(
$q(x)>0$
for nonzero
)
$x\in {\mathbb{R}}^3$
is a sum of squares of three homogeneous quadrics. That paper led to Hilbert’s 17th problem, and eventually to much of modern real algebraic geometry.
A few years after we met, I saw a talk by Vicki at an AMS Special Session in which she mentioned a computation she did with Bruce Reznick. In every instance they computed for
$q$
positive,
7
, they found 63 inequivalent representations of
$q = p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2$
with
$p_i$
complex polynomials, fifteen that were sums or differences of squares of real polynomials and of those, eight were sums of squares. Intrigued by the numerology
,
$(2^6 - 1, 2^4 - 1, 2^3)$
I reproduced their results on my computer that night. Vicki, Bruce, and I discussed this and Coble’s 1929 AMS Colloquium lectures where he discussed expressing a ternary quartic as a quadratic form of quadrics.
Claus Scheiderer was also interested, and Vicki, Bruce, Claus, and I began collaborating at a meeting in Rennes in June 2001. The weather, food, company, and mathematics were all lovely and we made significant progress, including a crash course on real Jacobians of quartics on the excursion to Mont St. Michel.
By the end of the week, the story was clear: By Coble, such complex representations up to equivalence correspond to the nontrivial 2-torsion points on the Jacobian
$J$
of the quartic plane curve
$C$
defined by
.
$q$
This is a 3-dimensional complex abelian variety. As a group
,
$J\simeq (S^1)^6$
where
$S^1={\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}$
is the circle group. Thus
$J$
has
$2^6-1=63$
such 2-torsion points. Those that are sums or differences of real quadrics come from the real points of the Jacobian (its points fixed under the induced complex conjugation). As
$q$
is positive,
$C$
has no real points, but plenty of divisors, some with interesting real structure. In fact,
—it
$J({\mathbb{R}})\simeq (S^1)^3\times {\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}$
has two components. In this way, we find
$2\cdot 2^3-1$
sums or differences of squares of real quadrics, and the eight sums of squares should correspond to the 2-torsion points on the nonidentity component of
.
$J({\mathbb{R}})$
Later, Claus furnished an exact sequence using Galois cohomology to complete the proof. We eventually published this in
Comptes Rendus Mathématique
9
. It was a magical collaboration with good friends: While sharing meals in a beautiful setting, we found something new to say about a foundational result of Hilbert. Over the ensuing 20+ years, I saw Vicki many times, rekindling our friendship over meals while discussing our families and mathematics. In this time, we collaborated on organizing mathematical events, including a Semester at MSRI, a BIRS workshop, and in June 2017 a two-week CIMPA Research School at the University of Ibadan in Nigeria.

By
Bernd Sturmfels
Polynomial equations and inequalities in several real variables play a fundamental role in mathematics and its numerous applications, especially in optimization and data science. The systematic study of real polynomials is the field of real algebraic geometry. Vicki Powers held a leadership role in real algebraic geometry in the United States for three decades. Her research, mentorship, and service to the community have been a great inspiration for many of us, and they have been hugely beneficial to my own research.
Vicki and I first met in the early 1990s. At the time I was on the faculty of Cornell University, where Vicki had obtained her PhD a few years earlier. She maintained close connections to the real algebraic geometry centers in Europe, especially in Germany and in Spain. When polynomial optimization became popular, through advances in sum-of-squares representations and semidefinite programming, Vicki was at the forefront. A fine example is her 2007 article with Jim Demmel and Jiawang Nie
5
on the representation of positive polynomials on semialgebraic sets.
Vicki was enthusiastic about mathematics, about the social aspects thereof, about sharing her wonderful insights, about collaborations, and about German beer. One collaboration that I found fascinating is the one that led to
9
. A wonderful journey from Vicki’s computations to the joys of abelian varieties.
Recently, I have been to collaborating with particle physicists. My new friends love Feynman integrals. The question whether such an integral converges leads to deciding and certifying positivity of polynomials. When I heard the very sad news of Vicki’s passing, I was in the middle of a project on this topic. That very day, I was studying Vicki’s wonderful article
3
, which, in my view, is very important for convergence of Feynman integrals. There is no doubt in my mind that Vicki’s mathematical legacy will live on and thrive.
By
Claus Scheiderer
Vicki and I first met in 1989 in an Oberwolfach conference on quadratic forms. She was enthusiastic about math in a way that was truly inspiring to others. She kept asking questions that were seemingly innocent, but whose answer turned out not to be clear at all. And she would suggest ideas for how to attack such problems, which often resulted in joint papers with others. About her own work she was always very modest, and tended to underrate it. Vicki was a very positive person throughout, with a welcoming attitude towards everybody.
By
Daniel Plaumann
Vicki’s paper on the moment problem with Claus Scheiderer
10
was my introduction to research mathematics. Claus soon became my advisor. When I finally met Vicki, she was not the professorial senior figure I had perhaps imagined. She was one of the most joyful, warm, and friendly mathematicians I have ever encountered. We met many more times at conferences and later in Atlanta.
She would cheerfully proclaim “I just love polynomials.” That was an understatement, of course, but it reflected her preference for concrete and accessible mathematics. She connected people working in algebraic geometry and optimization, and later made a foray into the mathematics of elections. She wrote a wonderful book on positive polynomials
6
that gives a detailed but highly readable account of the development of the subject, much of which happened during her own career.
By
Ha Nguyen
As a new graduate student at Emory many years ago, I struggled with the imposter phenomenon, often doubting my math ability. Vicki not only welcomed me and made me feel at ease but also believed in me before I fully believed in myself. She encouraged me to present at international conferences in Germany and France, where she enthusiastically introduced me to the positive polynomials research community.
Vicki was exceptional at making complex math more accessible to general audiences. She was also extraordinarily generous. She shared her written research narratives to help me develop my own research writing skills and insisted that I take full credit for my presentations on our joint work.
Inspired by her Math and Politics freshman seminar at Emory University, I later incorporated mathematics for social justice topics into my own courses, showing students how they can use mathematics to make sense of the world. Vicki shaped the way I teach and mentor my own students today. Even as ALS took its toll on her, Vicki remained the incredibly warm, positive, and courageous person I had always known, admired, and respected.

By
Tomek Bartoszynski, Matt Douglass, Joanna Kania-Bartoszynska, Tie Luo, Jennifer Pearl, and Andy Pollington
Vicki Powers served as a program director with us in the Division of Mathematical Sciences at the US National Science Foundation from August 2013 through August 2015. She served the mathematics community as part of the Algebra and Number Theory program and also helped to manage the REU Site, the Mathematical Science Research Institutes, and the CAREER programs. Working with colleagues across the directorate, Vicki was also a member of the organization committee for the MPS Distinguished Lectures. She was a wonderful colleague, and we most remember her telling stories, laughing, and impressing us with the distances she could run. It was a treat to get to spend time with both her and her husband Colm in DC, and to enjoy their taste of international food. We miss Vicki and remember both her personal and mathematical contributions fondly.
