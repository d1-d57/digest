LUNA’S SLICE THEOREM AND APPLICATIONS
MICHEL BRION, HANSPETER KRAFT, AND GERALD W. SCHWARZ

Abstract. The aim of this article is to give an introduction to Luna’s Slice
Theorem for non-specialists. In the first section we give an idea of its significance without being too precise. It is followed by an introduction to compact
transformation groups in section 2. Then we show by examples how classification problems lead to the study of orbits of algebraic groups (section 3).
In order to explain and formulate Luna’s Slice Theorem we need some basics
from algebraic transformation groups and algebraic geometry. This is done in
sections 4 and 5. The paper ends with some striking applications in the last
section 6.

1. Introduction
Luna’s Slice Theorem is an algebraic version of many more classical slice theorems, for example, the one for smooth actions of compact Lie groups on manifolds.
But the statement of Luna’s theorem is rather advanced, and it takes some acquaintance with the theory to appreciate its full depth and scope. In this short
introduction we try to give an idea of its significance without being too precise.
Precise statements are in the body of the paper; see the end of this introduction
for an outline.
1.1. A philosophical Slice Theorem. Let G be a reductive complex algebraic
group (see 4.2). Examples are the classical groups, e.g., GLm (C), SLm (C) or SOm (C),
or any finite group. Let V be a finite dimensional complex vector space on which
G acts linearly. We say that V is a G-module.
Unless otherwise specified, we are using the Zariski topology on V ≃ Cn (see
section 3). The Zariski-closed subsets of Cn are exactly the sets of common zeroes
of polynomial functions in the coordinates z1 , . . . , zn . The usual topology on Cn is
much finer. We call it the strong topology or the C-topology, and we will talk about
C-open and C-closed subsets, etc.
An orbit Gv := {gv | g ∈ G} ⊂ V is always Zariski-open in its Zariski-closure
which equals the C-closure (see section 4.1) and thus carries a natural structure of
an algebraic variety. Unless the image of G in GL(V ) is finite there are always orbits
which are not closed. For example, if G = C∗ = GL1 (C) acts by multiplication on
C, then the orbits are C \ {0} and {0}, and only the latter is closed.
Luna’s Slice Theorem describes what is happening near closed orbits. Let v ∈ V
such that Gv is closed. Then the isotropy group Gv := {g ∈ G | gv = v} is also

Theorem A (Philosophical Slice Theorem). The structure of the G-action in an
open neighborhood of the closed orbit Gv ⊂ V is determined by the action of the
isotropy group Gv in a neighborhood of 0 ∈ Nv .

1.3. Étale morphisms. What can we say in the algebraic category? First of all
there are far fewer open sets and the inverse function theorem does not hold. This
leads to the concept of étale morphisms (see section 4.5). Let f : X → Y be a
morphism between complex algebraic varieties. If X is smooth at x and Y smooth
at f (x), then f is étale at x if the differential dfx : Tx X → Tf (x) Y is an isomorphism. Thus f is C-locally biholomorphic at x, i.e., posesses, C-locally at f (x), a
holomorphic inverse. (For possibly singular varieties, we refer to section 4.5 for a
definition.) We say that f is étale if it is étale at all points.

1.7. First applications. Luna’s Slice Theorem has many applications as we shall
see in section 6. Here we list two. Luna was interested in the question of classifying
actions of reductive complex groups on complex affine spaces Cn . An impetus for
proving the slice theorem was the following consequence, see Example 6.1(4).
Theorem. Consider an action of G on Cn and suppose that there is only one
closed orbit. Then, after an algebraic change of coordinates on V , the action of G
becomes linear.
It took a long while (and more ideas of Luna’s) to see that this Linearization
Theorem does not hold in general if there are infinitely many closed orbits ([36],
[21]).
Probably the first application of Luna’s Slice Theorem (aside from those in his
original paper) was by Kac, Popov and Vinberg in 1976 ([16], see also [34] and
[35]). They determined all irreducible G-modules V , where G is a simple complex
algebraic group (e.g., SLn (C) or SOn (C)) and V //G is smooth; equivalently, the
algebra of G-invariant polynomial functions on V is a polynomial ring. Critical was
using the following immediate consequence of the slice theorem: if V //G is smooth,
then so is the quotient of every slice representation.

1.8. Guidelines for the reader. Here is an outline of the paper. Section 2 gives
an introduction to the theory of compact transformation groups, in particular to
the topological slice theorem. A reader familiar with these concepts can skip this
section.
In section 3 we show how classification problems in algebra lead to the study
of orbits of algebraic group actions and of orbit spaces and quotients, and we give
several examples which can also be skipped in a first reading.
The next section 4 contains a short introduction to algebraic transformation
groups and to étale morphisms. Once these concepts are clear, we can formulate
and prove Luna’s Slice Theorem in section 5, and then give a number of striking
applications in the final section 6.
Let us mention here that the book Geometric Invariant Theory of Mumford,
Fogarty and Kirwan contains a survey on Luna’s Slice Theorem with some
applications, see [31]. Also, there is the article Luna’s slice theorem and applications
by Drézet in [10] with a proof of the Slice Theorem.
Luna’s slice theorem has many applications to the local structure of moduli
spaces (see for example [24], Section II). The theorem has been generalized to positive characteristic by Bardsley and Richardson; see [4], and [3] (Theorem 5.1)
for a further generalization that allows non-reduced isotropy groups. We mention
finally recent developments on the structure of algebraic stacks related to Luna’s
slice theorem, see [1] and also [33].

2. Compact Transformation Groups
We start with some generalities and some lack of precision. Let X be a topological space acted upon by a topological group G. A slice theorem enables one to
determine the properties of the G-action near an orbit Gx in terms of the action
of the isotropy group Gx on a slice S. There are many slice theorems depending
upon which category of spaces, groups and actions one chooses. In this first section
we concentrate on the case of compact groups where the main concepts are more
elementary.
Our basic reference here is Bredon’s book [8].
2.1. Group actions and orbit spaces. Let G be a finite or, more generally, a
compact group G acting continuously on a topological space X. This means that
we have a homomorphism ρ of G into the group of homeomorphisms of X such
that the “action map” G × X → X, (g, x) 7→ gx := ρ(g)x, is continuous. We call X
a G-space. We will always assume that our topological spaces are locally compact
and Hausdorff and thus completely regular1 ([15, Theorem 2-56]).
The orbit space X/G, i.e., the set of orbits Gx := {gx | g ∈ G}, comes with a
canonical map π : X → X/G, called the quotient map, which sends a point x ∈ X
to its orbit Gx. For a subset Y ⊆ X we will use the notation GY := {gy | g ∈
G, y ∈ Y }.

The orbit space X/G has a canonical topology, the quotient topology, where a
subset U ⊆ X/G is open if and only if π −1 (U ) ⊆ X is open. Then π is an open
mapping. It is a nice exercise in topology to prove the following result (see [8, Ch.
1, Theorem 3.1]).
Proposition 2.1. The orbit space X/G is locally compact and Hausdorff and the
quotient map π = πG : X → X/G is proper, i.e., the inverse image of every compact
set is compact.
Another observation is the following. Let Y be a topological space, let C(X, Y )
denote the space of continuous functions from X to Y , and let C(X, Y )G denote
the subset of G-invariant functions, i.e., those functions which are constant on the
G-orbits in X. If f ∈ C(X/G, Y ), then the pull-back π ∗ f := f ◦ π is G-invariant.
The converse also holds.
Proposition 2.2. The pull-back map π ∗ induces an isomorphism of C(X/G, Y )
with C(X, Y )G .
Thus we see that there is a strong relation between orbit spaces and invariant
functions. We will see later in section 2.10 how invariant functions can be used to
construct orbit spaces.
Example 2.3. Consider the group G := {±1} acting on the sphere S n ⊂ Rn+1
by sending x to −x. It is well-known that the orbit space S n /G can be identified
with the n-dimensional real projective space Pn (R) and that the quotient map
π : S n → Pn (R) is a 2-fold covering.
Example 2.4. Consider the compact group S 1 := SO2 (R) acting on R2 by rotation.

Definition 2.5. Let Y be a G-space. A continuous map f : X → Y is G-equivariant
if f (gx) = gf (x) for every g ∈ G and x ∈ X. Then f sends G-orbits in X into Gorbits and induces a continuous map X/G → Y /G which we denote by f /G.
For some results we will need the additional assumption that G is a compact Lie

and the inverse are smooth maps. It is known that a compact group is a Lie group
if and only if it is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of the orthogonal group On (R)
for some n, cf. [8, Chap. 0.5, Theorem 5.1].
