Teichmüller Theory
 is the title of a three-volume work by John Hamal Hubbard. So as not to leave the reader in suspense, let me begin by saying that this is one of the best math books I have ever read. Reading this book is like carrying on a long illuminating conversation with an infinitely patient ideal graduate advisor. The author (Hubbard) is always drawing your attention to small but pivotal details, and generously explaining how he always found X confusing until he realized Y, and incidentally while we’re on the subject, did you know Z, and W?

Teichmüller Theory
 is about Teichmüller theory—but if you already know what Teichmüller theory is you probably also know who John Hubbard is, and were going to buy and read this book anyway. So for everyone else I will spend the first part of this article giving an informal sense of what the subject is about, and only delve into the specific delights of Hubbard’s book toward the end.

Teichmüller theory is the branch of complex analysis that deals with 
marked Riemann surfaces
. A Riemann surface is a manifold with an atlas of charts modeled on open subsets of 
$\mathbb{C}$
 with holomorphic transition functions between charts on their overlaps; a 
marking
 of a Riemann surface is a homotopy class of homeomorphisms to some fixed reference surface. A marking gives labels to the topological features of a Riemann surface. To put it more poetically: the miscellaneous item of clothing your crazy uncle knits you for your birthday is a Riemann surface; it becomes a 
marked
 Riemann surface when you decide which limb—neck, arm, leg, tail, etc.—goes in which aperture. Riemann surfaces often arise in finite-dimensional families, whose continuous invariants (
moduli
) such as the lattice of periods and the spectrum of the Laplacian are parameterized by 
moduli spaces
. A 
marking
 on a Riemann surface is a further discrete invariant, and the marked Riemann surfaces of a fixed topological type are parameterized by a 
Teichmüller space
. Forgetting the marking gives a projection from Teichmüller space to moduli space that is essentially the universal covering map.

The simplest example of a Teichmüller space is the space of marked elliptic curves over the complex numbers. An elliptic curve 
$E$
 is a closed Riemann surface homeomorphic to a torus, with a marked point 
.
$p$
 The universal cover of such a surface is isomorphic as a Riemann surface to 
,
$\mathbb{C}$
 and the deck group of the cover, i.e., the fundamental group 
,
$\pi _1(E,p)$
 is a doubly periodic and discrete group of translations. As an abstract group the deck group is isomorphic to 
,
$\mathbb{Z}^2$
 but there is no 
canonical
 choice of such an isomorphism. Such an isomorphism is determined by a choice of oriented basis, and a 
marking
 in this context is equivalent to such a choice. After choosing free abelian generators 
$m,l$
 for the deck group, one may choose holomorphic coordinates on the universal cover of 
$E$
 for which 
$m$
 acts as translation by 
$1$
 and 
$l$
 acts as translation by 
,
$\tau$
 a complex number with positive imaginary part. Conversely, for any such 
$\tau$
 the additive subgroup 
$\langle 1,\tau \rangle$
 of 
$\mathbb{C}$
 acts discretely and cocompactly on 
$\mathbb{C}$
 with quotient an elliptic curve. Thus the Teichmüller space 
$\mathcal{T}$
 of marked elliptic curves is isomorphic to the upper half space 
$\mathbb{H}$
 consisting of complex numbers with positive imaginary part, and the moduli space 
$\mathcal{M}$
 is obtained from 
$\mathcal{T}$
 by quotienting out by the discrete group 
,
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$
 which freely permutes the different choices of marking—i.e., the different choices of oriented bases for the deck group. This action is properly discontinuous on 
$\mathbb{H}$
 but not quite free, since finitely many conjugacy classes of torsion elements in 
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$
 have fixed points in 
$\mathbb{H}$
 that project to nonsmooth “orbifold points” in moduli space.

Even in this example one may see certain advantages of Teichmüller space over moduli space, including:

(1)

Teichmüller spaces are easy to understand topologically; they are diffeomorphic to finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, whereas moduli spaces are orbifolds that can have complicated topology, even locally;

(2)

Teichmüller spaces have many automorphisms: the 
mapping class group
 of a surface 
$S$
 is the group of homotopy classes of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms, and this group acts as a discrete group of symmetries of the Teichmüller space of 
$S$
 by permuting the markings.

In fact, and this is actually the key point, Teichmüller spaces admit many interesting self-maps beyond these automorphisms. Morally speaking, any “interesting” topological relationship between a surface and itself gives rise to a holomorphic endomorphism of a Teichmüller space. We give two elementary examples.

(1)

Let 
$A$
 be a 
$2\times 2$
 integer matrix with determinant a squarefree integer 
.
$d>1$
 Let 
$E=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$
 be an elliptic curve marked by an isomorphism 
.
$\mathbb{Z}^2 \to \Lambda$
 Then the image of 
$A\mathbb{Z}^2$

$\Lambda '$

$\Lambda$

,
$d$

$d$

.

 Thus the map sending 
$E$
 to 
$E'$
 induced by 
$A$
 gives an endomorphism (actually, an isomorphism) of the Teichmüller space of elliptic curves. A fixed point for this endomorphism, that may or may not exist depending on 
,
$A$
 is called an elliptic curve with complex multiplication.

(2)

A quadratic polynomial of the form 
$f:z \to z^2 + c$

)
$f'$

.
$0$
 If 
$c$

,
$0$

,
$f(0)$

$f(f(0))$

$n$

.
$f^n(0)$
 For certain values of

Suppose we want to find a real number 
$c<0$

$$\begin{equation*} f(0) < 0 = f^3(0) < f^2(0). \end{equation*}$$
In other words, we are looking for a real root

$c < 0 < c^2+c$
 If we had such a

.
$\infty ,c,0,c^2+c$

To bring this down to earth: what this amounts to numerically is making some initial guess

$$\begin{equation*} c_{j+1} = -\sqrt {\sqrt {-c_j}-c_j} \end{equation*}$$
where

$c_0=-1$

,

,
$-\sqrt {2}$

,
$-1.61351$

,
$-1.69816$

,
$-1.73242$

,
$-1.74603$

In each of these examples we start with a topological description of some dynamics and try to realize the dynamics geometrically, which is to say holomorphically. In the first case we are looking for a holomorphic endomorphism of an elliptic curve 
$E$
 realizing a particular matrix as the induced map on 
;
$\pi _1(E)$
 in the second case we are looking for a degree 2 polynomial endomorphism 
$f$
 of the Riemann sphere for which the orbit of the unique root of 
$f'$
 realizes a specific topological itinerary. These are examples of so-called 
geometrization problems
. One of the key technical innovations in the celebrated work of William Thurston in the 1970s and 80s was to formulate the solution of certain deep geometrization problems in terms of the existence of fixed points for endomorphisms of Teichmüller spaces, and it is the goal of Hubbard’s book to present full and detailed proofs of these theorems of Thurston together with all the background necessary to understand and appreciate them.

Before going into more detail, it is worth discussing why one should expect any given endomorphism of a Teichmüller space to have a fixed point. The reason is that endomorphisms of Teichmüller spaces are typically distance decreasing for a certain metric, and are therefore subject to the contraction mapping principle.

Let me explain in more detail. Moduli spaces have natural complex analytic structures, and the same is therefore true of Teichmüller spaces. On 
any
 complex analytic manifold there is a canonical (Finsler) pseudometric, called the 
Kobayashi metric
, and whenever this metric is nondegenerate, any holomorphic endomorphism is distance nonincreasing. The simplest example is the Poincaré (hyperbolic) metric 
$d_\mathbb{D}$
 on the unit disk 
.
$\mathbb{D}\subset \mathbb{C}$
 This is given infinitesimally by the quadratic form

$$\begin{equation*} ds^2 = \frac{4 dz d\bar{z}}{(1-|z|^2)^2} \end{equation*}$$

and its geodesics are straight lines or arcs of round circles that meet 
$\partial \mathbb{D}$
 orthogonally. The fact that every analytic map 
$\mathbb{D}\to \mathbb{D}$
 is distance nonincreasing in the Poincaré metric is equivalent to the Schwarz lemma. For an arbitrary complex analytic manifold 
,
$X$
 the Kobayashi “metric” is the largest pseudometric 
$d_X$
 for which 
$d_X(f(x),f(y)) \le d_\mathbb{D}(x,y)$
 for all holomorphic maps 
.
$f:\mathbb{D}\to X$
 From this it follows that any holomorphic map between complex analytic manifolds is nonincreasing in their respective Kobayashi pseudometrics; in particular, any endomorphism 
$f:X \to X$
 is distance nonincreasing.

To apply this observation to a given example, one must show that the Kobayashi metric is nondegenerate, i.e., that it is an actual metric. Royden did precisely this: he showed that the Kobayashi metric on Teichmüller space is nondegenerate, and in fact he identified it with the so-called 
Teichmüller metric
, which can be defined as follows. A conformal map between Riemann surfaces takes infinitesimal round circles to infinitesimal round circles; a 
quasiconformal map
 takes infinitesimal round circles to infinitesimal ellipses with 
bounded aspect ratio
. The data of this distortion is represented by a (measurable) field of infinitesimal ellipses on the surface called a 
Beltrami differential
, and the quasiconformal map can be recovered up to isomorphism from this differential. If 
$f:R \to S$
 is a quasiconformal homeomorphism between Riemann surfaces, let’s denote by 
$K(f)$
 the maximal aspect ratio of such infinitesimal ellipses. The logarithm of 
$K(f)$
 is called the 
dilatation
 of 
;
$f$
 it is zero if and only if 
$f$
 is conformal. Let 
$R$
 and 
$S$
 be marked Riemann surfaces in some Teichmüller space 
.
$\mathcal{T}$
 Then the marking determines a homotopy class of homeomorphism between them, and the 
Teichmüller distance
 from 
$R$
 to 
$S$
 is the infimum of the dilatation 
$\log K(f)$
 over all quasiconformal maps in this distinguished homotopy class.

It turns out that for any marked Riemann surfaces 
$R$
 and 
,
$S$
 there always exists a unique map realizing the infimal dilatation, and indeed this map has a rather special form. Such maps can be described in terms of 
quadratic holomorphic differentials
. A quadratic holomorphic differential 
$q$
 on a marked Riemann surface 
$R$
 is something that can be expressed in terms of any local holomorphic coordinate 
$z$
 in the form 
,
$q = q(z) dz^2$
 where 
$q(z)$
 is a holomorphic function of 
.
$z$
 Away from the zeros of 
,
$q$
 a quadratic holomorphic differential admits a square root unique up to sign, which is just a holomorphic 1-form that may be paired with (real) tangent vectors to give complex numbers. By abuse of notation we denote this equivalence class of 1-forms by 
,
$\sqrt {q}$
 though as we stress it is only well defined up to multiplication by 
.
$\pm 1$
 Thus, away from the zeros of 
,
$q$
 the tangent space of 
$R$
 admits a canonical real splitting into tangent directions that pair with the 1-form 
$\sqrt {q}$
 to give real numbers, and tangent directions that pair with 
$\sqrt {q}$
 to give purely imaginary numbers; and these real subbundles may be integrated to give a pair of singular foliations on 
,
$R$
 transverse to each other like a coordinate grid. For any real 
$\lambda > 1$
 one may deform the complex structure on 
$R$
 by stretching the leaves of the real foliation by a factor of 
$\lambda$
 and compressing the leaves of the imaginary foliation by a factor of 
.
$1/\lambda$
 This gives a new complex structure on 
$R$
 that may be interpreted as a new marked Riemann surface 
,
$S$
 and thereby defines a so-called 
Teichmüller map
 from 
$R$
 to 
.
$S$
 By varying 
$\lambda$
 and multiplying 
$q$
 by a complex number of absolute value 
,
$1$
 one obtains a 1-complex-dimensional family of such Teichmüller maps whose images sweep out a totally geodesic 
$\mathbb{D}$
 in 
$\mathcal{T}$
 called a 
Teichmüller disk
 on which the Teichmüller metric and the intrinsic hyperbolic metric agree. Comparing metrics on arbitary holomorphic maps of 
$\mathbb{D}$
 into 
$\mathcal{T}$
 with these special “Teichmüller disks” one may show that holomorphic maps from 
$\mathbb{D}$
 into 
$\mathcal{T}$
 are distance nonincreasing, and Royden’s theorem follows.

The conclusion is that any holomorphic endomorphism 
$\varphi :\mathcal{T}\to \mathcal{T}$
 is distance nonincreasing, and in fact is strictly distance decreasing everywhere unless it is an automorphism induced by a mapping class. In particular, any endomorphism that is not an automorphism either has a unique fixed point, or the image of any point under iterates of 
$\varphi$
 must eventually leave every compact subset (i.e., 
$\varphi$
 is “proper”).

What are the specific geometrization problems of Thurston covered in Hubbard’s book? There are three of them:

(1)

the classification of homeomorphisms of surfaces;

(2)

the topological characterization of postcritically finite rational maps; and

(3)

the hyperbolization theorem for Haken 3-manifolds.

The last of these problems splits rather naturally into the fibered and the nonfibered cases. In order to emphasize the unity of the subject, we shall focus on the second problem and the nonfibered case of the third problem. We also note that the complete proof of the nonfibered case of the hyperbolization theorem is projected to appear in volume 4 of Hubbard’s book, which at the time of this review is not yet completed.

Let us first discuss the second problem, that of the topological characterization of postcritically finite rational maps. “Rational maps” here just means holomorphic endomorphisms of 
,
$\mathbb{CP}^1$
 i.e., maps of the form 
$f:z \to p(z)/q(z)$
 where 
$p$
 and 
$q$
 are polynomials. The 
critical points
 of a holomorphic map 
$f$
 between Riemann surfaces 
$f$
 are the points at which 
$df$
 vanishes. Geometrically these are the points near which 
$f$
 looks locally like a branched cover 
$z \to z^n$
 for some 
;
$n>1$
 in algebraic geometry one calls these the 
ramification points
. A rational map of degree 
$d$
 has 
$2d-2$
 critical points counted with multiplicity, and it turns out that the dynamics of 
$f$
 is largely governed by the orbits of the critical points under repeated application of 
.
$f$
 This is roughly because holomorphic maps of Riemann surfaces without critical points are covering maps, which tend to be dynamically uninteresting, and thus the “interesting” behavior can be localized to the orbits where some branching is observed. A rational map is said to be 
postcritically finite
 if the critical points are preperiodic; equivalently, if their forward orbits are all finite sets. This behavior may be imposed by 
$2d-2$
 equations, one for each critical point. On the other hand, a rational map 
$z \to p(z)/q(z)$
 is determined by 
$2d+1$
 coefficients if we normalize 
$q$
 to be monic; conjugation by the (3-complex-dimensional) group of Möbius transformations 
$z \to (az+b)/(cz+d)$
 therefore reduces the complex dimension of the space of isomorphism classes of degree 
$d$
 rational maps to 
.
$2d-2$
 Thus by a naive dimension count, one expects to be able to manufacture rational maps that are postcritically finite, i.e., maps whose critical points are all preperiodic. Furthermore, at least locally, one expects that postcritically finite maps should be holomorphically rigid up to conjugacy.

Now, suppose that 
$f:\mathbb{CP}^1 \to \mathbb{CP}^1$
 is a postcritically finite map. Let 
$C$
 denote the set of critical points of 
,
$f$
 and let 
$P$
 denote the (finite!) set of forward orbits of elements of 
.
$C$
 Then we obtain a topological invariant of 
$f$
 by taking the relative isotopy class of the map of pairs 
.
$g:(\mathbb{CP}^1,P) \to (\mathbb{CP}^1,P)$
 Of course 
$\mathbb{CP}^1$
 is topologically just the 2-sphere 
,
$S^2$
 and the relative isotopy class of 
$g:(\mathbb{CP}^1,P) \to (\mathbb{CP}^1,P)$
 only “sees” the topological structure of 
.
$\mathbb{CP}^1$
 So we may ask the converse question: given a (topological) oriented branched covering map 
$g:S^2 \to S^2$
 for which the forward orbit 
$P$
 of the set of branch points 
$C$
 is finite, one may form the relative isotopy class 
$g:(S^2,P) \to (S^2,P)$
 and ask whether there is a postcritically finite rational map 
$f$
 realizing this topological data; and if so whether it is unique up to conjugacy. This is a geometrization problem in the above sense.

Here is Thurston’s solution. A marked conformal structure on 
$S^2 - P$
 pulls back under 
$g^{-1}$
 to a marked conformal structure on 
$S^2 - g^{-1} P$
 that may be filled in over the points of 
$g^{-1}(P) - P$
 to give a new marked conformal structure on 
.
$S^2 - P$
 Thus 
$g$
 determines an endomorphism 
$\sigma _g$
 of 
,
$\mathcal{T}$
 the Teichmüller space of marked conformal structures on a sphere minus 
$|P|$
 points. A solution to the realization problem is equivalent to a fixed point for 
.
$\sigma _g$
 Under modest topological conditions, always satisfied for example if 
,
$|P|>4$
 the endomorphism 
$\sigma _g$
 turns out to be a 
strict contraction
 of the Teichmüller metric; and furthermore, the infinitesimal contraction factor at some marked conformal structure only depends on the image of that point in moduli space, i.e., the conformal structure obtained by ignoring the marking. It follows that either a fixed point exists and is unique, or the orbit of any marked conformal structure, respectively its unmarked image, is proper in Teichmüller space, respectively in moduli space. A sequence of conformal structures on a Riemann surface can only diverge in moduli space if the surface develops “thin necks”—i.e., if it contains essential embedded annuli whose moduli go to infinity. The cores of such annuli form a 
-
$g$
invariant system of essential simple closed curves—a 
multicurve
—that constitute a topological obstruction to existence of a rational map 
.
$f$

$\mathcal{T}(S_0)\times \mathcal{T}(S_1) \to \mathcal{GF}(N)$
 determines a map

$$\begin{equation*} \mathcal{T}(S_0)\times \mathcal{T}(S_1) \to \mathcal{GF}(N) \to \mathcal{T}(\bar{S_0}) \times \mathcal{T}(\bar{S_1}). \end{equation*}$$

On the other hand, the orientation reversing homeomorphism 
$\varphi :\partial N \to \partial N$
 acts on markings to induce isomorphisms 
$\mathcal{T}(\bar{S_0}) \to \mathcal{T}(S_1)$
 and 
.
$\mathcal{T}(\bar{S_1}) \to \mathcal{T}(S_0)$
 Composing these maps gives rise to Thurston’s 
skinning map

$$\begin{equation*} \sigma _N:\mathcal{T}(S_0)\times \mathcal{T}(S_1) \to \mathcal{T}(S_0)\times \mathcal{T}(S_1), \end{equation*}$$

and a fixed point for 
$\sigma _N$
 is a geometrically finite structure on 
$N$
 that can be glued up to obtain a hyperbolic structure on 
.
$M$

If 
$S$
 is not a fiber so that 
$N$
 is not a product, a theorem of McMullen implies that 
$\sigma _N$
 is strictly distance decreasing. Thus either a (unique) fixed point exists, or every orbit of 
$\sigma _N$
 must enter a specific region of Teichmüller space. Just as in the case of postcritically finite rational maps, this latter possibility certifies the existence of a topological obstruction to the solution of a geometrization problem: in this case, the existence of a hyperbolic structure on 
,
$M$
 namely a system of essential annuli connecting pairs of curves on 
$\partial N$
 that glue together to produce a nonempty family of essential tori in 
.
$M$

Previous steps in the gluing can be reduced to this last gluing step by a trick (the “orbifold trick”) and the base step of the induction is to realize the polyhedral balls into which 
$M$
 is ultimately decomposed by virtue of its Hakenness as right-angled hyperbolic polyhedra; this is accomplished via a result known as Andreev’s theorem.

I would now like to move on to a more detailed discussion of Hubbard’s book, its style, its focus, and its point of view. This discussion will necessarily become somewhat technical, and I apologize in advance to the nonspecialist who has read this far; hopefully you have already been persuaded of the beauty and utility of the subject and need no further inducement to buy the book.

Perhaps the first thing I should say is that the book is very long, though it is usefully divided into three volumes, the first of which contains most of the technical background, and the latter two which contain the proofs of Thurston’s theorems. The first time that I read the book, I read it cover to cover, all 1300 pages in serial order. I am convinced this is not the right order to read the book, and I strongly suspect that it was not the order in which the book was written, particularly the first volume.

This is how one should read the book. Suppose you want to know the proof of Royden’s theorem (discussed above) that the Teichmüller metric and the Kobayashi metrics on Teichmüller space coincide. This is Theorem 6.10.6 in Hubbard’s book. The proof given is 
not
 Royden’s proof that we sketched earlier, but an alternate proof due to Earle–Kra–Krushkal that we now describe. If 
$E$
 and 
$F$
 are 1-dimensional complex vector spaces, let 
$L^+_\mathbb{R}(E,F)$
 denote the space of orientation-preserving real linear isomorphisms from 
$E$
 to 
,
$F$
 and let 
$L_{\mathbb{C}}(E,F)$
 denote the space of complex linear isomorphisms. The coset space 
$L^+_\mathbb{R}/L_{\mathbb{C}}$
 measures the failure of a real linear map to be complex linear. It is a homogeneous space isomorphic to 
,
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})$
 and may naturally be identified with a copy of the hyperbolic plane, which is the same thing as the unit disk 
$\mathbb{D}$
 with the Poincaré metric. Thus the Beltrami differential 
$\mu$
 of a quasiconformal map 
$f:R \to S$
 may be thought of as a measurable section of a certain 
$\mathbb{D}$
 bundle over 
$R$
 which for concreteness we will denote by 
.
$\mathcal{D}$
 A conformal map corresponds to the zero section, and the quantity 
,
$\log K(f)$
 which one minimizes over all suitable maps 
$f$
 to compute the Teichmüller distance from 
$R$
 to 
,
$S$
 is the essential supremum over 
$p\in R$
 of the hyperbolic distance from 
$\mu (p)$
 to 
$0$
 in the fiber 
$\mathcal{D}_p$
 over 
.
$p$

Thus the “hard direction” of Royden’s theorem, i.e., that 
$d_\mathcal{T}(g(x),g(y)) \le d_\mathbb{D}(x,y)$
 for any holomorphic 
,
$g:\mathbb{D}\to \mathcal{T}$
 may be proved as follows. Suppose for any holomorphic 
$g:\mathbb{D}\to \mathcal{T}$
 taking 
$g(0)$
 to 
$R$
 there is a lift of 
$g$
 to a map 
$\tilde{g}$
 from 
$\mathbb{D}$
 to Beltrami differentials on 
$R$
 with a certain property. For each 
$x \in \mathbb{D}$
 and each 
$p\in R$
 the Beltrami differential 
$\tilde{g}(x)$
 may be identified as above with a measurable section of 
,
$\mathcal{D}$
 and the restriction of this section to 
$p$
 gives a point in 
;
$\mathcal{D}_p$
 hence 
$\tilde{g}$
 determines, for each 
,
$p\in R$
 a map 
$\tilde{g}_p:\mathbb{D}\to \mathcal{D}_p$
 taking 
$0$
 to 
.
$0$
 The property we want is that for each 
,
$p\in R$
 the map 
$\tilde{g}_p$
 should be holomorphic; for in that case, since 
$\mathcal{D}_p$
 is isomorphic to 
,
$\mathbb{D}$
 the desired inequality follows immediately from the Schwarz Lemma. Note that the “easy direction” of the theorem follows from the existence of Teichmüller disks as discussed earlier.

The existence of such a lift from 
$g$
 to 
$\tilde{g}$
 follows from the 
equivariant Slodkowski’s theorem
—Theorem 5.2.13 in Hubbard—as follows. By the Bers embedding theorem, a special case of the Ahlfors–Bers theorem discussed previously, a map 
$g:\mathbb{D}\to \mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(R)$
 determines a map

$$\begin{equation*} \psi :\mathbb{D}\to \mathcal{GF}(R\times I) = \mathcal{T}(R)\times \mathcal{T}(\bar{R}) \end{equation*}$$

which is constant and equal to 
$\bar{R}$
 on the second factor. If we normalize coordinates so that the limit circle for the representation 
$\psi (0)$
 is the round 
$\mathbb{RP}^1$
 in 
$\mathbb{CP}^1$
 and 
$\Omega ^-(\psi (0))$
 is the lower half plane 
,
$\bar{\mathbb{H}}$
 we get a holomorphic family of maps 
$\mathbb{D}\times \bar{\mathbb{H}} \to \mathbb{CP}^1$
 where 
$p\times \bar{\mathbb{H}}$
 maps conformally and 
-
$\pi _1(R)$
equivariantly to 
.
$\Omega ^-(\psi (p))$
 Slodkowski’s theorem says that such 
holomorphic motions
 can be (equivariantly) extended to 
$\mathbb{D}\times \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{CP}^1$
 which can then be restricted to a family of maps 
$\mathbb{D}\times \mathbb{H}\to \mathbb{CP}^1$
 taking 
$p\times \mathbb{H}\to \Omega ^+(\psi (p))$
 or, after taking the quotient by 
,
$\pi _1(R)$
 taking 
$R$
 to the surface 
.
$g(p)\in \mathcal{T}(R)$
 The (so-called) 
-
$\lambda$
lemma of Mañe–Sad–Sullivan
 says amongst other things that this holomorphic extension is quasiconformal, and its Beltrami differential gives the desired lift 
,
$\tilde{g}$
 proving Royden’s theorem.

The 
-
$\lambda$
lemma of Mañe–Sad–Sullivan is Theorem 5.2.3 in Hubbard and the proof given depends on a characterization of quasiconformal maps (Theorem 4.5.4) in terms of a property called 
skewness
. Given three distinct complex numbers 
$a,b,c\in \mathbb{C}$
 the quantity 
$\mathrm{skew}(a,b,c)$
 is the ratio of the longest to the shortest side of the triangle they determine. A homeomorphism 
$f:U \to V$
 between open domains in 
$\mathbb{C}$
 is 
quasisymmetric
 if the skewness of triples has uniformly controlled distortion under 
$f$
 and 
;
$f^{-1}$
 Theorem 4.5.4 is the fact that quasisymmetric maps are quasiconformal and vice versa. The direction we need is that quasisymmetric implies quasiconformal, and amounts to showing that if 
$f$
 is quasisymmetric, then on sufficiently small triangles 
$\Delta$
 in an equilateral mesh, the square of the diameter of 
$f(\Delta )$
 is bounded by a constant times the area of 
.
$f(\Delta )$
 After giving the proof Hubbard remarks that the proof of this theorem “explains” why in the definition of quasiconformal maps the distributional directional derivatives of a quasiconformal map are required to be locally in 
$L^2$
 rather than, e.g., in 
,
$L^1$
 because one needs to control the 
area
 of the image rather than the length. I will admit that at this point I went back to the analytic definition of quasiconformal maps (Definition 4.1.1) and the subsequent sections and tried to think through what could go wrong with derivatives that were merely locally 
.
$L^1$

OK, so if one really just wants to verify the proof of the equality of the Teichmüller and Kobayashi metrics this is hardly a direct route. Why go to all this bother? If you examine the contents of a mathematician’s head you will find several different categories of mathematial knowledge. Part of this knowledge consists of facts—theorems that a mathematician “knows,” or at least believes, to be true. For some of these theorems the mathematician will know a proof, or a sketch of a proof, or a sketch of a proof that appeals to some “standard” background material that belongs to another subdiscipline and is therefore someone else’s responsibility. And in some small subset of cases the mathematician will really be able to fill in all the details, or at least have been exposed to them at some point in their education. Much rarer is real 
understanding
, in which we perceive all the elements of a mathematical theory as part of an integrated sensible whole. If you are impatient to tick off a checklist of “big theorems,” read another book; for real understanding, stick with Hubbard. After reading this book once forward and then one more time (mostly) backward, I have begun to get some glimmers of this understanding; in particular I now feel like I “get” Royden’s theorem and a few others in a way that I never did before.

Perhaps the reader wants to know how this book compares to other well-known books on Teichmüller theory and related topics. So as not to be rude, I will only say that if Hubbard’s book had been available when I was a graduate student, I would probably now have a dozen fewer books in my math library. The book is at its best when it discusses analytic aspects of the theory in geometric terms; this is a perspective that harmonizes perfectly with my own, and I could not imagine a better source from which to learn the subject. There are literally hundreds of illustrations (Figure 2.1.3 in volume 1 is one of my favorites) and numerous beautiful exercises. I should emphasize that no important result used anywhere depends on a fact whose proof is left as an exercise. I suppose there might be mathematicians who do not care for this approach—to them I say: 
de gustibus non est disputandum
.

Some books transcend their specialty and become universal classics that every mathematician should read; for example, Serre’s 
A Course in Arithmetic
, Milnor’s 
Morse Theory
, and a few others. Hubbard’s 
Teichmüller Theory
 belongs in this category.
