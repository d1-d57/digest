 —  3. Further directions  — 

Will Sawin and Ofir Gorodetsky have obtained analogues of several of the above results for function field models or permutation models respectively; we briefly discuss these in the paper, although we do not plan to cover these models in depth. We also note another 
recent use of this technique
 to solve a separate Erdős problem (#858) relating to antichains in a variant of the divisibility poset.

The zeta process that we have discovered hints at an emerging theory of the “developmental anatomy of integers”, which differs from the existing topic of anatomy of integers in that it views a large integer 
 (and its prime factor “organs”) not as a static entity, but rather as an evolving 
process
 in which primes (or powers of primes, in the case of the von Mangoldt process) are added or removed to the integer over time. With this perspective, primitive sets can be viewed as singular moments in such a developmental process, which are only encountered at most once in the life cycle of a given integer. It seems of interest to study this developmental perspective further.

The paper is currently a work in progress; we have released an early version due to the public interest in this problem. We plan to explore some further applications, and also to formalize more of the above results in Lean (currently two of the six main theorems are formalized), before submitting the paper for publication. The situation here highlights a distinction I have 
recently made
 between three components of the problem solving process in mathematics, namely proof generation, proof verification, and proof digestion. In this particular case, the first two steps were extremely rapid due to modern AI tools; however, properly digesting the AI-generated proofs into a coherent exposition that places the arguments in context with both past literature and future directions remains a slower process that requires expert human attention.

4 May, 2026 at 6:58 am

Terence Tao

In order theory, a 
chain
 is a totally ordered set.  In the theory of Markov processes, a 
Markov chain
 is a stochastic process in which each element of the process transitions to the next in a memoryless fashion.  When one iterates a (discrete) Markov process, one gets a sequence of states, but in general there is no reason to expect any ordering on that states that would make that process a chain in the order-theoretic sense.  However, in this paper we restrict ourselves to two special types of Markov chains on posets – upwards Markov chains and downwards Markov chains – in which the sequences produced by the Markov chains are also order-theoretic chains.  Because of this, it is relatively safe to conflate the two (otherwise distinct) notions of “chain” for the purpose of this paper, though if one wished to consider Markov chains that were not upward or downward, or order-theoretic chains that were not generated by Markov processes, then it would be advisable to keep these two notions separate.

5 May, 2026 at 7:45 am

Terence Tao

1) Yes, one should be able to extend the constructions easily to any number field or function field with unique factorization; Will Sawin has already worked out all the analogues in the simplest function field case of irreducible polynomials over a finite field.  The question of what happens with non-trivial class group is less clear – one could presumably still build some analogues of these processes on ideals rather than individual elements of the ring of integers, but perhaps some additional complications arise.  (Also, for many of the results here, we needed quite precise numerical bounds on the Riemann zeta function; getting the analogous bounds for other zeta functions is in principle possible, but the numerical inequalities one needs to verify may end up being false in some settings.)

2) Certainly!  In one of our applications (the AKS theorem, in Section 8) we use a different upwards chain than either the von Mangoldt or Mertens processes, namely a multiplicative simple random walk where we multiply the current number by a random prime drawn from a zeta-type distribution to get the next number in the sequence, though it took some trial and error to land on this particular choice of process (which was hinted at *very* implicitly in the original AKS paper, but took a while to tease out, as they phrased their argument in terms of a double counting argument involving Sperner type cubes instead).  To prove Sperner’s lemma or the LYM inequality one can use a simple downwards process where one divides out every number by a prime factor chosen uniformly at random (rather than with von Mangoldt weighting); see Example 2.6.    For any given problem one may have to proceed by a trial-and-error process in which one experiments with one type of random chain, identify the places where it is not performing optimally, and tweak the chain as needed to try to address these problems.  For instance in several of our applications we had to modify the von Mangoldt chain to patch the issue that it would sometimes “jump over” the primitive sets we wanted to detect because it would multiply or divide by powers of primes rather than a single prime.

Knowing the anatomy of “typical” elements of the distribution of natural numbers one is trying to emulate can help.  The (normalized) prime factorization of a typical large number is known to follow the Poisson-Dirichlet process (by Billingsley’s theorem), which in turn is well-known to be generated by the 
stick-breaking process
.  In retrospect, this was already a large hint that the von Mangoldt chain (which corresponds to the first breaking of the stick) should be relevant for such problems.  But when working with numbers with more unusual anatomy, some other process may be better adapted to understand them.

Terence Tao

Well, as discussed in Remark 1.8 of the paper, the duality is basically reversible: any bound on primitive sets is equivalent to the existence of a random process obeying a certain hitting bound.  However, this process is not necessarily Markovian; the fact that Markov processes seem to be a particularly good choice for the dual process certificate is still slightly mysterious, although Markov processes are a natural type of process to consider in other problems and so it is perhaps not extremely surprising in retrospect that they also show up here.
