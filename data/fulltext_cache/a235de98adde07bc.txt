Neanderthal Bone Grease Factory
John Carlos Baez — Azimuth blog, 2025-07-11

John Carlos Baez's blog post explores how Neanderthals solved a nutritional survival challenge through sophisticated food processing.

The Problem: Protein Toxicity
When hunter-gatherers subsisted primarily on lean game during winter months, they faced a counterintuitive danger. Consuming sufficient lean meat to meet caloric needs could actually prove fatal due to excessive protein intake without adequate fats and carbohydrates — a condition known as "rabbit starvation" or protein toxicity.

The Solution: Fat Processing
Rather than consume muscle meat, these populations prioritized fatty portions, particularly bone marrow. More significantly, they developed bone grease production: crushing bones with stone tools, boiling them for hours, then skimming the rendered fat from the cooled liquid.

Archaeological Evidence
The post highlights a remarkable discovery: Neanderthals operated what researchers call a "bone grease factory" approximately 125,000 years ago near Neumark-Nord in Germany, during the Last Interglacial period. The site revealed "tens of thousands of small bone fragments" from systematically processed bison, horses, and deer — suggesting organized, large-scale fat extraction operations.

This archaeological finding demonstrates that Neanderthals possessed sophisticated understanding of nutrition and food processing technology far earlier than previously documented in other human populations.
