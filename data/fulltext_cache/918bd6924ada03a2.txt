The AMS Karl Menger Memorial Awards Committee is tasked with traveling to the Regeneron International Science and Engineering Fair⁠
Footnote
1
(ISEF) each year in May to judge mathematically oriented projects. ISEF, organized by the Society for Science, is the largest global science competition open to high school students. These high school students, who come from more than 75 countries, regions, and territories, and compete across 21 different STEM disciplines, earned the right to compete at ISEF by winning a top prize at a local, regional, state, or national science fair. The Menger Awards Committee members act as Special Award judges and give out first, second, and third place prizes, as well as honorable mention awards. This year, ISEF was held in Columbus, Ohio, and the AMS was one of 45 professional organizations and universities to participate in the Special Awards.

One thing I learned from serving on this committee several times is that the Menger committee serves a unique role at ISEF, in that it is the only group of judges which consists of trained mathematicians who can assess projects that use advanced math.

DS: I hadn’t heard about ISEF until last summer when the AMS invited me to serve on the Menger Awards Committee. This is my first year participating as a judge, and I will serve on this committee for two more years. I had an amazing time at ISEF meeting with students, listening to their oral presentations, learning about their projects, and asking questions to assess their understanding of the work. I was impressed by the variety and high quality of the projects and presentations, especially those using advanced topics.
Q:
What are moments that you will always remember about your experience judging at ISEF?
WH: I’m always impressed by the projects in which students can understand, and do nontrivial work, in very advanced areas of mathematics. More often though, the projects that stand out, and that I love hearing more about, are those by students genuinely passionate about what they’re working on; their love of the project really comes across, and it becomes clear they’ve been involved in genuine mathematical research.
MZ: What stays with me are the students who did their work completely on their own, just guided by their own interests. Often they don’t realize how impressive their projects are, and I really enjoy encouraging these students. In a different direction, I won’t soon forget the student this year who had such solid understanding of so much advanced math that I eventually asked him when he had learned algebraic geometry, to which he matter-of-factly replied, “in middle school.”
DS: In hindsight, I don’t think I was prepared for what I experienced at ISEF. These are high school students from around the world who are super driven, genuinely curious, extremely intelligent and capable of diving into difficult mathematics research. Many of the projects were completed in 6–12 months, and about a quarter of the project finalists that we interviewed didn’t work with a faculty mentor. It was truly mind-blowing to me. I’m so excited to go back next year!
Q:
What were some standout projects this year?
WH: Two projects that stood out to me, personally, were “Analyzing Etruscan Language Origins Using TDA [topological data analysis],” by Helena Welch, and “A Book About the Union-Closed Sets Conjecture,” by Serene Yi Feng.
The TDA project asked an interesting question about linguistics, characterizing the “structure” of languages using TDA and using cluster analysis to identify languages that were closest to each other. As a part of the analysis process Helena used large language models (LLM) in the linguistic processing, and compared the quality and likeness of translations based on the type and size of LLM model used. It was an awesome mathematical data science project that encapsulated getting creative about what objects should be analyzed statistically, incorporated a heavy computational component, and really impressed all three judges.
The other project, the book about an open conjecture, was actually a children’s book. Serene had written, illustrated, and self-published (with actual professional-looking physical copies at the booth) a book introducing the union-closed sets conjecture⁠
Footnote
7
through a story, with a “story terms to math terms” dictionary at the end. This conjecture states that for any nonempty, finite, union-closed family
$\mathcal{F}$
over a set of elements
$U$
,
there is some element
$x \in U$
such that
$x$
is an element of at least half the sets in
$\mathcal{F}$
.
The book was fun to read, integrated math into the story in a way that didn’t feel contrived, and the author had started testing the book as a classroom resource in several elementary school classes. We were all astounded and just so excited about the prospect of more resources introducing open mathematical questions to younger folks.

MZ: In addition to the projects Wesley mentioned, another impressive project was “General Theory of the Occurrence of Pi in the Mandelbrot Set and First Proof of Its Occurrence at the Parameter
$-\frac{3}{4}$
”
by Thies Brockmoeller, Oscar Scherz, and Nedim Srkalovic. The authors showed that if
$c$
is a parabolic point in the Mandelbrot set, meaning a complex number for which some iterate
$f(z)$
of
$z^2+c$
has a fixed point
$d$
for which
$f'(d)$
is a root of unity, then several natural sequences of numbers connected to how
$c$
resides in the Mandelbrot set all converge to
$\pi$
.
This was known previously only for the value
$c=\frac{1}{4}$
.
This would be very nice work if it had been done by a leading complex dynamicist; the fact that it was done by high school students is astounding.
DS: For me, the standout projects were the three mentioned by Wesley and Michael, as well as a project about a math card game, “Make24: Elegant Elementary Bounds for a Numbers Game” by Liqian Ying. In the classic version of Make24, players compete to be the fastest to get to
$n=24$
using
$k=4$
cards (integers between 1 and 10 inclusive) and addition, multiplication, subtraction, and/or division. Liqian grew up playing this game with his family in Singapore and noticed that there’s not always a solution. He was motivated to explore a generalized version of the game and studied the minimum
$k$
such that there is a valid solution for each fixed
$n$
.
He developed this research project on his own based on personal experience and genuine mathematical curiosity, and he was the most enthusiastic and interactive speaker we interviewed. In addition, Liqian was dressed in the colors of the Singaporean flag (red blazer, white pants) to further show pride in his upbringing. I was inspired by his passion for research and making math accessible to everyone, while also pushing the boundaries of what is known about the generalized version of this game.
I want to add that as Special Award judges, we focus on judging projects categorized as “math,” but we can consider mathematically oriented projects in other categories as well. The evening before students arrive for interviewing, judges may walk through the entire poster display. We did this, and as we were leaving, we discovered the children’s book on the union-closed sets conjecture by chance! This project was categorized as “behavioral science,” but it had significant math content. We all stopped to read the poster and the children’s book, and we were very impressed. We interviewed Serene the next day and she continued to impress us with her knowledge, drive, and ability to seamlessly integrate complicated math concepts into an accessible children’s book.
Q:
What do you want others to know about judging at ISEF and getting involved?
WH: Volunteering at ISEF, either as a Menger Award judge, or a Grand Award⁠
Footnote
8
judge through ISEF itself, is incredibly rewarding. You get to see some awesome research projects done by high school students from around the world, food is provided, and you get to work alongside other math enthusiasts. As for getting involved, ISEF changes its location each year, so I’d recommend keeping an eye on where it takes place each year in case it shows up at a location near you; the next year’s location is usually announced by the previous year’s fall. In the United States, many ISEF participants get selected through their regional (state or multi-state) fairs, so another opportunity to get involved would be through local-to-you fairs. For example, Massachusetts’ science fairs are coordinated by the Massachusetts Science and Engineering Fair (MSEF), and they maintain a list of regional fairs and ways to volunteer as a judge at them. Become a judge!⁠
