History

Toward a History of American Mathematics
Karen Hunger Parshall
Communicated by 
Notices
 Associate Editor Adrian C. Rice

In 1985, historians of American science Sally Kohlstedt and Margaret Rossiter brought together a group of their colleagues “to assess the current state of various specialties [in the history of American science] and to indicate what ‘needs and opportunities’ remained after more than a decade of significant activity” 
15
, 
p. 7
. In January of 2025, a group of historians of mathematics was asked to consider essentially the same question in the context of a semester-long research program on “Modern History of Mathematics” at the Isaac Newton Institute (INI) in Cambridge, England. Specifically, they were asked to identify “emerging themes in the modern history of mathematics” and to isolate “the main lacuna” in the discipline’s different subfields.⁠
Footnote
1
 The first initiative resulted in Kohlstedt and Rossiter’s book, 
Historical Writing on American Science: Perspectives and Prospects

15
, the first volume in a new series of 
Osiris
 that was brought out under the auspices of the History of Science Society (HSS). Indeed, Arnold Thackray, the then editor of the HSS’s journal 
Isis
 declared that it “was natural to make American science the theme for volume 1, since, as he saw it, at that moment “within the history of science, scholarship on American science [was] the leading sector” 
15
, 
p. 5
. This paper resulted from the second initiative. By tracing the historiography of the history of mathematics in the United States—that is, how that history has been treated and interpreted over time—this article makes the case that the study of the technical history of American mathematics after 1950 represents the major, although by no means the only, lacuna in the history of American mathematics.
1
A weeklong workshop devoted to that theme kicked things off. The program, as well as my stay in Cambridge, was supported by EPSRC grant EP/Z000580/1. I thank the Newton Institute for its support, the organizers for inviting me to speak, and my fellow participants for many fruitful conversations and discussions. This paper grew from the talk I gave there.

1
.

Historiographical Context

Historical Writing on American Science
 brought together fifteen scholars who looked at what they delineated as four different aspects of their field 
15
, 
pp. 9–16
. There were the so-called “classic themes” of American science, namely, institutional history as well as science in medicine, in religion, and in the Federal government. There were also the “newer areas” of native knowledge in the Americas, science and technology, science and war, and post-World War II science and public policy. And, there were issues of access to sources in addition to the matter of “science in the specialties.” The “specialties” covered represented a tour of the science departments at any major American research institution in the mid-1980s: astronomy, biology, chemistry, geology, and physics, but also the social sciences. One glaring omission? Mathematics.⁠
Footnote
2
 What were readers of this book to take way from this, if, indeed, they even noticed? Was there effectively no history of American mathematics to survey and assess in 1985? Were the historians of American science active within the HSS in the early 1980s simply oblivious to work that had been done in the history of American mathematics? Or was something else at play?
2
For a discussion of this point in broader historiographical context, see 
19
, 
pp. x–xiii
.
✖

Leaving aside possible answers to these questions, suffice it to say that a significant amount of ink has been devoted to what had evolved by the 1960s—thanks to the social and cultural turns in historical scholarship—as the fraught relationship between the history of science and the history of mathematics. Prior to that time, historians of science confidently followed the founder of their field, George Sarton, in the much-quoted belief that he expressed in his 1937 book, 
The Study of the History of Mathematics
, namely,“[t]ake the mathematical developments out of the history of science, and you suppress the skeleton that supported and kept together the rest” 
21
, 
p. 4
. It is mathematics, he held, that “gives to science its innermost unity and cohesion” 
21
, 
p. 4
. After the mid-twentieth century, however, with the increasing emphasis on so-called “external” as opposed to “internal” factors in the development of science, the strength of this position quickly eroded.⁠
Footnote
3
3
In the history of science, an internalist approach is one that focuses primarily on the technical development of a given science, whereas an externalist approach focuses more on the “external,” societal, philosophical, or other sorts of nontechnical factors.
✖

As recently as 2011, 
Isis
 carried a Focus Section entitled “The History of Science and the History of Mathematics” in which historian of mathematics Amir Alexander still felt the need to argue why the answer to the question “should historians of science care about the history of mathematics?” is, in his words, “ ‘yes’ and unequivocally so” 
1
, 
p. 478
. His cocontributors, Moritz Epple, Stephanie Dick, Joan Richards, Jeremy Gray, and Tony Mann, each in their own ways, concurred, even if Gray closed on the cautionary note that “[w]e have a lot to do” 
12
, 
p. 517
.

But in many regards, the relationship between mathematics and the history of mathematics is also fraught. That was brought out quite starkly by the ever-blunt mathematician, André Weil, in both his blistering review of Michael Mahoney’s biography of Pierre de Fermat in the 
Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society
 in 1973⁠
Footnote
4
 and then again when he took the podium at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Helsinki in 1978 
28
.⁠
Footnote
5
 Invited to give an hour-long plenary address, Weil opted to speak not on some aspect of the mathematics he had helped to develop, in, say, number theory or algebraic geometry, but rather on “History of Mathematics: Why and How?” Of course, this was another area in which he had done a certain amount of work, and, in his ICM lecture, he stated emphatically that “the craft of mathematical history can best be practiced by those of us who are or have been active mathematicians or at least who are in close contact with active mathematicians” 
28
, 
p. 234
. For Weil, such things as “[g]aining insights into a great mathematician’s character and into his weaknesses is an 
innocent
 pleasure that 
even serious
 historians need not deny themselves” 
28
, 
p. 234 (my emphases)
. There was thus only one valuable, one right history of mathematics for Weil, and he even used a different term for it: “mathematical history.”⁠
Footnote
6
 That was the type of history of mathematics done by the collective mathematician Nicolas Bourbaki—of whom Weil was a part—the type of history that, for example, “properly,” in Weil’s words, analyzed “the contents of Books V and VII of Euclid” using “the concept of group and even that of groups with operators, since the ratios of magnitudes are treated as a multiplicative group operating on the additive group of the magnitudes themselves” 
28
, 
p. 232
.⁠

The historian, however, would take issue with Weil’s premises. The kind of technical history of mathematics that Weil espoused, the historian would say, is ahistorical. The historical figures did not have the “advantages” of modern constructs, so they could not have understood things in those terms, even if those terms are congenial to the modern mathematician. How then, the historian would ask, did 
they
 understand things? And, who were 
they
? Are only those identified as “great,” either in their day or only subsequently, important in the development of a set of mathematical ideas? Or put another way, what, the historian would ask, are the dynamics of and who constitutes a given mathematical community?

Putting it polemically as could be his wont, the distinguished and much-lamented English historian of mathematics, Ivor Grattan-Guinness, summed things up this way in 1990. The history of mathematics is “a classic example of a ghetto subject: too mathematical for historians and too historical for mathematicians” 
11
, 
p. 158
. With this broad and complex historiographical framework in mind, consider the problem at hand historiographically, that is, let’s see where a glimpse at the historiography of mathematics in the United States leads us in our effort to answer the question: what is the main lacuna in the history of American mathematics, specifically?

2
.

The Historiography of Mathematics in the US to 1989

Regardless of the reason why 
Historical Writing on American Science
 lacked a chapter on the history of mathematics, Uta Merzbach filled that unfortunate gap just four years after the volume’s publication. In “The Study of the History of Mathematics in America: A Centennial Sketch” 
16
, published in the three-volume 
A Century of Mathematics in America

8
, she demonstrated that historical writing on mathematics went back at least to the late nineteenth century in America with the publication in 1890 of 
The Teaching and History of Mathematics in the United States
 by émigré Swiss mathematician, Florian Cajori 
5
. His book also marked, in a sense, the beginning of the study of the history of American mathematics per se. In it, Cajori presented what was effectively an institutional history of mathematics in the United States to 1890 as well as a snapshot of both the teaching and research activity then underway in the country.⁠
Footnote
8
 As he saw it, mathematical developments had thus far passed through two developmental stages. First, a period of British influence lasted until roughly 1820 and found American mathematics teaching dominated by British or British-inspired texts and the American mathematics curriculum following that of most British institutions. Second, an analogous period of French influence persisted, in his view, to “the present time,” that is, to the late 1880s 
5
, 
p. 293
. While subsequent scholarship has taken issue with his rather simplistic interpretation (see below), his study remains remarkable for the comparative data, flawed and incomplete though they may be, that he amassed on the state of mathematics at some 168 colleges and universities.
8
See also Karen Hunger Parshall, 
A Century-Old Snapshot of American Mathematics
, The Mathematical Intelligencer 
12
 (1990), no. 3, 7–11.
✖

A tradition of work in the history of mathematics in general, but also in the history of American mathematics per se, continued into and through the opening decades of the twentieth century. To abstract out the two major book-length examples of the latter from the many, many works that Merzbach highlighted in her essay, we find 
A History of Mathematics in America before 1900

22
 published in 1934 by historians of mathematics David Eugene Smith and Jekuthiel Ginsburg and 
A Semicentennial History of the American Mathematical Society
 produced four years later by mathematician Raymond C. Archibald 
2
. While the first of these two books covered much the same ground that Cajori’s had, it was a work more reflective of genuine historical research as opposed to a compilation of data. The second broke new ground in documenting the history of the country’s first professional mathematical research society.

Smith and Ginsburg, unlike Cajori, aimed at a connected narrative of the historical development of American mathematics from the earliest explorations of North America in the sixteenth century through 1900. They situated their story within both its cultural and institutional contexts through what Smith described as “a painstaking examination of material in several of our largest libraries” 
22
, 
p. v
. Like Cajori, though, they were interested in making their readers aware of those who had come before, and not just those with what they termed “prominent names” but also those whose names were less well-known 
22
, 
pp. 52, 91, and 118
. And, whereas Cajori focused primarily on the teaching side of the mathematical endeavor, Smith and Ginsburg, while not neglecting that, tried also to give a nontechnical, bibliographical sense of the “general trend of mathematics in America” as well as of the “trend of important branches” of American mathematical research, especially in the last quarter of the nineteenth century 
22
, 
pp. 154–164 and 164–197, resp.
.

From a methodological point of view, Archibald’s book was quite different. It represented a deep dive into the archival record as held both by longtime AMS secretary, his colleague at Brown University Roland Richardson, and by various of the Society’s past presidents. It documented the AMS’s founding, its incorporation, the vagaries of its funding, the different initiatives it championed, its publications, its political structure, and its various offices and officers. Relative to the latter, it also provided biographies of those who had served as secretary and as president as well as bibliographies of their works published to 1938. For these reasons, it remains an invaluable secondary source for historians of American mathematics almost ninety years after its publication.

Moreover, although Merzbach did not mention it, Archibald’s book was accompanied by a second volume of 
Semicentennial Addresses of the American Mathematical Society
, the aim of which was “to serve both to reveal what has been accomplished in America since the founding of the Society, and also to acquaint mathematicians with current problems and research in many fields” 
6
, 
p. i
. Eight of the leading mathematicians in the country at the time contextualized technical American contributions in their respective areas, while George Birkhoff then took on what he described as “the challenging task of tracing our mathematical development under the auspices of the Society during the years which have passed,” that is, the fifty years from 1888 to 1938 
3
, 
p. 270
. These expository essays represent a concerted effort on the part of mathematicians to establish the respective mathematical contexts of their topics, to address the mathematics itself in a historically sensitive way, and to highlight not “great men” necessarily but American contributors. In so doing, they provide meaningful looks into some of the technical aspects of American mathematics over the course of the AMS’s first fifty years without, for the most part, reducing their accounts to the kind of ahistorical approach that Weil would later advocate.

Clearly, anniversaries are often causes for retrospection, and two more prompted new books on the history of American mathematics. In 1977, the Mathematical Association of America, the country’s teaching-oriented professional society, brought out 
The Bicentennial Tribute to American Mathematics, 1776–1976
, an edited volume that, in celebrating the nation’s 200th birthday, called on mathematicians, historians of mathematics, and mathematics educators to take yet another retrospective, albeit this time fairly superficial, look at mathematical developments in the United States 
27
. Since fully half of this just over 200-page book dealt with mathematics teaching, space in it for the history of American mathematics was limited. Still, it did contain several brief essays on historical topics, in addition to a more technically oriented overview that aimed in twenty pages “to describe some major victories of American mathematicians since, and [to] mention the names of the winners, with …enough explanation (but just) to show who the enemy was” 
9
, 
p. 79
. The terminology is noteworthy here. For some of the contributors to the volume at least, the history of American mathematics was about “leaders,” “victories,” and “winners.”

Another anniversary, this one the centennial of the founding of the American Mathematical Society in 1988, prompted the much more substantial, much more widely ranging, 
A Century of Mathematics in America
, in which Merzbach’s essay appeared 
8
. An impressive undertaking of well over 1700 pages, it reflected many aspects—technical, institutional, biographical, social—of the mathematical endeavor in the United States in the form of new scholarship, personal reminiscences, and reprinted sources collected together from disparate publications.

A closer look at the bicentennial and centennial volumes suggests, in fact, a historiographic analysis for the history of American mathematics analogous to that of Kohlstedt and Rossiter for the history of American science. There were the “classic themes” of the technical history of mathematical developments, institutional history, biography, and the history of mathematics teaching. There were the “newer areas” of the cultural history of American mathematics and the history of American mathematical developments after 1950. And, finally, there were primary specializations in the nineteenth century as well as in the first half of the twentieth century. Indeed, from a historiographical point of view, many of the contributions to these volumes suggested extended studies and, whether or not they directly inspired such work, it was not long after the publication of the three centennial volumes that it began to appear. Let’s briefly consider three books and a website that echoed key themes from those volumes and, in so doing, take our historiographical survey of American mathematics from 1989 to roughly the present.

3
.

Three Books and a Website

One study that 
was
 directly inspired by the AMS centennial is perhaps the main reason why I was asked to give the talk on which this article is based. When Peter Duren and Ed Edwards, two of the editors of the centennial volumes, approached David Rowe and me to contribute to their publications, we were presented with a unique opportunity. We had been discussing how interesting it was that our respective research seemed to go far to account for the rise of an American mathematical research community in the last quarter of the nineteenth century. David’s research focused on Felix Klein and his work and students in Germany. Mine centered largely on the United States and, in particular, on James Joseph Sylvester’s animation at Johns Hopkins of what can be thought of as the first school of mathematical research in the United States and on the role of E. H. Moore in establishing and building the Department of Mathematics at the University of Chicago. When we sat down to produce our contribution, the chapter, “American Mathematics Comes of Age: 1875–1900,” we realized that we were essentially writing a narrative outline of a book on that topic 
18
. We decided to go for it, but what were we getting ourselves into?

As sketched above, we were venturing into a complex historiographical ecosystem made up of the history of science, mathematics, and the history of mathematics. As we put it in the preface to our 1994 book, 
The Emergence of the American Mathematical Research Community, 1876–1900: J. J. Sylvester, Felix Klein, and E. H. Moore
, “more than the ideas involved in [the] pivotal scientific discipline [of mathematics] merit historical illumination and …approaches other than the standard internalist techniques will be required to confront [its] diverse historical issues” 
19
, 
p. xiii
. We consciously sought to meld externalist and internalist techniques in describing an “emergence” of research-level mathematics in the United States as the result of the independent efforts of three mathematicians—one English, one German, and one American—on two continents—North America and Europe. As we argued, it was a surprising “confluence of historical trends and events [that] enabled this disparate trio to emerge as the dominant figures in the” story 
19
, 
pp. ix–x
. Moreover, by analyzing their actual mathematical research in their respective institutional and cultural contexts, we showed how they inspired the subsequent work of many of their students and of those students’ students. In other words, we were consciously seeking to bring the history of mathematics out of the “ghetto” that Grattan-Guinness had identified.

In the course of our work, we introduced a periodization—by no means totally new with us—that seemed both meaningful and potent as an analytical tool. We argued that four “reasonably distinct and well-defined” periods had shaped mathematics in the United States, specifically,

the hundred years from 1776 to 1876, during which mathematics developed not separately but within the context of the general structure-building of American as opposed to colonial science as a whole; the quarter-century from 1876–1900, which witnessed the emergence of [a] true research community; the first third of the twentieth century, during which the institutions and research traditions largely established in the previous era consolidated and grew; and the period from 1933 to roughly 1960, which saw the massive influx of European mathematicians beginning from the mid-1930s, the development of various areas of applied mathematics, and the institutionalization of large-scale government funding of basic research both during and after the Second World War. 
19
, 
pp. 427–428

We hoped that this framework would at least serve to establish some sort of historiographical scaffolding on which to craft future research questions. I leave an analysis of the extent to which it did to future assessors of the historiography of American mathematics.

As was the case with David and me, the essay on “Women in American Mathematics: A Century of Contributions” 
13
 that Judy Green and Jeanne LaDuke contributed to the centennial volumes also grew into a book. By 2009, their exhaustive biographical study of 
Pioneering Women in American Mathematics: The Pre-1940 PhDs
 had opened up new vistas onto the lives and careers of women mathematicians and had done much to adjust and better balance the view of the American mathematical research community of the first half of the twentieth century 
14
.

Similarly, although it is not clear the extent to which it may have had a direct influence on mathematician Scott Williams, the third AMS centennial volume contained an essay by another mathematician, James Donaldson, on “Black Mathematicians in America.”⁠
Footnote

 By 1997, Williams had begun populating a new website “Mathematicians of the African Diaspora” that, like Donaldson’s article although much more thoroughly and comprehensively, sought to make known African-American mathematicians, their biographies, and their accomplishments.⁠
Footnote

Like Green and LaDuke’s work, it served and serves to bring to light another traditionally underrepresented group within American mathematics.

These four projects, then, represented four explicit desiderata—lacunae—in the historiography of American mathematics that were already clearly in evidence by the end of the 1980s. Two of them, the book by Green and LaDuke and Williams’s website, fall under the “classic theme” of biography. The other two reflect the “newer area” of the social and cultural history of American mathematics, while incorporating, in the case of Siegmund-Schultze’s book, elements of the “classic” biographical and institutional themes, and in the case of my book with David Rowe, elements of those same themes together with the technical. As the last two examples suggest, the historiographical lines that might have been drawn prior to the 1990s became blurred in a changing historiographical landscape.

4
.

Classic Theme: Biography

Leaving the technical history of American mathematical developments aside for the moment, the “classic theme” that has perhaps remained the most classic over the more than three decades since 1990 has been biography. Despite its waning and waxing within other fields of the history of science, not to mention of history more generally, biography has continued to attract historians interested in American mathematicians. The reason is clear. As a genre, biography tends to be accessible to a relatively wide audience and can give a glimpse into both the mathematical mind and the mathematical endeavor without necessarily going into mathematical detail. Constance Reid’s 
The Search for E. T. Bell Also Known as John Taine
 from 1993 and Robert Parker’s 
R. L. Moore: Mathematician and Teacher
 from 2005 were both published by the Mathematical Association of America, and both aimed at a broad mathematical audience, with the former written almost like a detective story and the latter drawing heavily from remembrances and anecdotes recounted by the students and students of students of Moore.⁠
Footnote
12
 Neither dealt, other than in passing, with the actual mathematics done by their subjects. Similarly, writer Carol Parikh’s 1991 biography, 
The Unreal Life of Oscar Zariski
, published by Academic Press and 
Genius at Play, The Curious Mind of John Horton Conway
 written by Canadian journalist Siobhan Roberts and published in 2015 by the commercial press Bloomsbury Publishing USA, illuminated the lives of major American mathematicians.⁠
Footnote
13
 Aimed at an even larger audience, Sylvia Nasar’s 1998 biography of John Nash, 
A Beautiful Mind
, was made into an Academy Award-winning movie of the same name starring Russell Crowe in 2001 and was seen by hundreds of thousands worldwide.⁠
Footnote
14
12

The Search for E. T. Bell Also Known as John Taine
 (n.p.: Mathematical Association of America, 1993) and Robert Parker, 
R. L. Moore: mathematician and teacher
 (n.p.: Mathematical Association of America, 2005), respectively.

If these and other biographies of American mathematicians that could have been named here have targeted more popular audiences, Joseph Dauben’s 1995 biography, 
Abraham Robinson: The Creation of Nonstandard Analysis, A Personal and Mathematical Odyssey

7
, exemplifies the scholarly mathematical biography that intertwines the technical mathematical, the philosophical, and the geopolitical into the story of an extraordinary life. Robinson lived from 1918 until his premature death at the age of fifty-five in 1974. Dauben thus had a number of advantages as a biographer: he was able to interview many of Robinson’s colleagues, students, and friends; he had the full cooperation of Robinson’s wife, Renée; he had an archive at his disposal at Yale University, Robinson’s final academic home, that consisted of seventeen boxes and contained, as Dauben described it, “Robinson’s collected writings, correspondence, papers, and various miscellanea” 
7
, 
p. xiv
. While more popular biographies of mathematicians—like those of Reid, Parker, Parikh, Roberts, and Nasar—are certainly welcome, it is the kind of biography that Dauben wrote that makes, in my view at least, the most valuable contribution to the historiography of the history of American mathematics. At any rate, as even this short list makes manifest, biography does not represent a lacuna in the history of American mathematics.

5
.

Specialty: Twentieth-Century American Mathematics to 1950

Just as biography has continued to develop over the last three decades, so, too, has the previously well-defined specialty of the history of twentieth-century American mathematics to 1950. Focusing on the Rockefeller Foundation, an American philanthropic organization with roots in the closing decade of the nineteenth century, Reinhard Siegmund-Schultze cast a wide net in his 2001 book, 
Rockefeller and the Internationalization of Mathematics between the Two World Wars

26
. His was not strictly a story about the development of mathematics in the United States, although that was certainly a large part of it. Rather, it was an analysis, based on the extensive archival holdings of the Rockefeller Foundation and elsewhere, of the role that the foundation played through fellowships granted by both its General Education Board and its International Education Board (IEB) in strengthening American mathematics at the same time that it transformed mathematics into an international endeavor. As Siegmund-Schultze put it, “[a]ll facts point to an increasing, politically enforced independence of American mathematics from Europe in the 1930s” with “[t]he continuation of the initial IEB fellowship programs …reflect[ive of] both the rise of American mathematics and, in particular, the decline of traditional German mathematics” 
26
, 
p. 140
.

These interwar developments took place at a historical moment in which the American mathematical research community was undergoing key changes that allowed it to capitalize on the Rockefeller Foundation’s investments as well as on much else. Those are precisely the changes that I analyzed in my 2022 book, 
The New Era in American Mathematics, 1920–1950

17
. In a sense a sequel to the earlier book with David Rowe, 
The New Era
 is divided into three parts—roughly one per decade—that highlight what may be seen as the key components of the transformation of the American mathematical research community from a developing national community into arguably the international leader in the field. In part one on the 1920s, these components were the building of the infrastructure for mathematical research and American efforts to break onto the international scene. By the 1930s, they were the Depression and the influx of mathematical émigrés on American mathematics. Finally, the 1940s witnessed the community first at war and then painstakingly adjusting to new political realities in the postwar United States. And, the book tracks the technical mathematical developments in each of these three decades as well as “shifts in research foci, changing personnel, and the geographical dispersion of programs supportive of mathematics at the research level” 
17
, 
p. xvii
. The book’s argument, moreover, calls for a refinement of the earlier periodization of American mathematics. While “an American mathematical research community 
had
 emerged …by 1900,” and while “the years from 1900 to 1920 might, indeed, be termed a period of consolidation and growth,” “those from 1920 to 1950 were distinct from it in representing a ‘new era’ in which the American mathematical research community emerged as a major player on the international mathematical stage” 
17
, 
p. xxi (emphasis in the original)
. Although much more could certainly be done on the history of American mathematics to 1950, 
26
 and 
17
 went far to fill gaps that could be seen to have existed prior to the AMS centennial.

6
.

Newer Areas

If Siegmund-Schultze and I worked to fill gaps in the collective understanding of American mathematics up to 1950, others have begun moving into the newer areas of the cultural history of American mathematics and the history of American mathematics 
after
 1950. Three examples will suffice to give the flavor of these newer areas and a sense of the work being done in them at present.

In his 2020 book, 
Performing Math: A History of Communication and Anxiety in the American Mathematics Classroom

10
, Andy Fiss takes a cultural historical approach in presenting mathematics education in nineteenth-century America in a new light. Prior scholarship, as noted, had focused on questions such as what was taught and how it was taught, that is, on the subject matter, on the professors, and on the textbook writers. Fiss does not ignore these matters, but he poses different, cultural historical questions: how did 
students
 learn in their classrooms? what was 
their
 actual classroom experience? what exactly did 
they
 take away from their studies? His focus is thus on the students and their in- and out-of-class experiences, and he succeeds in giving his readers a vivid glimpse into nineteenth-century mathematics education from their perspective. In short, he shows that they performed. They read aloud from their textbooks; they marched around their respective college towns participating in elaborate “burial” ceremonies of their mathematics textbooks; they were called to write out and explain solutions at the blackboard; they staged plays in which they mocked but also celebrated their mathematical educations; they were made to participate in public, oral examinations.

Whereas Fiss’s research returned to look at the history of nineteenth-century American mathematics through a new historiographical lens—thereby addressing what, in an ever-changing historiographical climate, had become a lacuna—the next two books move beyond 1950. Christopher Phillips’s 2015 study, 
The New Math: A Political History

20
, preceded Fiss’s 
Performing Math
 in taking mathematics education as its point of departure, whereas Alma Steingart’s 2023 book, 
Axiomatics: Mathematical Thought and High Modernism

23
, utilizes the cultural historical approach to argue how the changing mathematical conception of axiomatization influenced a broad swath of American postwar culture. The United States of Phillips’s and Steingart’s works is one characterized by Cold War angst and its myriad effects. In Phillips’s case, those effects were political, in especially the post-Sputnik era, on mathematics education. In Steingart’s, they were cultural in the form of high modernism, that is, the “sweeping vision of how the benefits of technical and scientific progress might be applied—usually through the state—in every field of human activity.”⁠
Footnote
15
 While both of these valuable and enlightening studies serve fundamentally to illuminate the history of American mathematics after 1950, they both also underscore what is missing, in the context of the historiography of American mathematics laid out here.
15
James Scott, 
Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed
 (Yale University Press, New Haven, 1998), p. 17 as quoted in 
23
, 
p. 17
.
✖

7
.

The Main Lacuna

I would propose that the study of the 
technical history of post-1950 mathematics
 is the main lacuna, in the sense of deficiency as opposed to total absence, in the historiography of American mathematics. I make this statement, however, with some trepidation. At its heart lies the problem of audience. For whom are historians of mathematics, in general, and historians of American mathematics, in particular, writing? Clearly, they are writing for each other, since it is with each other that they share—or should be sharing—a literature and historiographical commitments. It is also to be hoped, however, that depending on the topic, they are writing for a broader audience of historians of science, of historians, of mathematicians, of others.

The three books just mentioned by Fiss, Phillips, and Steingart do just that, but none of them can be construed as technical. Steingart, however, does open her book with a chapter in which she gives a nice sense, from a largely philosophical point of view, of the evolution of “abstraction” in a mathematical context by focusing on Norman Steenrod’s education and career in topology. With a touch light enough, hopefully, not to intimidate a non-mathematical audience, she traces how Steenrod was enthralled as an undergraduate by axiomatically driven point set topology before encountering and eventually being won over as a graduate student by the “other” topology, that is, combinatorial, or what would now be called algebraic, topology. Steingart next charts how algebraic topology changed as mathematicians like Steenrod and Samuel Eilenberg, among others, restructured, in her words, what it “fundamentally 
was
” in the 1940s and 1950s 
23
, 
p. 45 (her emphasis)
. As she put it, “[i]nstead of introducing readers to concepts such as complexes, oriented complexes and chains, which were rooted in geometrical ideas, [Steenrod and Eilenberg] presented the axioms first and then derived these concepts from them. In so doing, they reversed conceptual priorities” 
23
, 
p. 45
. And, as she shows, this high-modernist process of restructuring went hand in hand with a striving for universality and unification as exemplified in the category theory later formulated by Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane. The very important interpretation within the framework of high modernism aside, this is material already familiar to almost all mathematicians. But what about tracing the historical development in the United States of some aspect of mathematics that thrived later? What challenges would the historian face?

Perhaps first and foremost would be the challenge of the technical difficulty of the mathematics itself. It would likely require a PhD or at a minimum a master’s degree in mathematics in addition to a PhD or at a minimum a master’s degree in the history of science. In today’s academic climate, one in which graduate programs have shrunken and pressures to decrease time to degree have increased, that level of credentialing seems unrealistic. And, even if it were feasible, while it would prepare the historian to tackle the question, once answered in dissertation form, to what sort of career might it lead? Perhaps one in a liberal-minded mathematics department, but not likely one in a history department or even one in one of the decreasing number of history of science departments. A manifestation of Grattan-Guinness’s “ghetto subject”?

One way out of this would be the teaming up of a historian of mathematics and a mathematician, with the latter responsible primarily for the mathematical content of the historical argument and the historian responsible primarily for formulating the historical questions and contextualizing the whole. Achieving such a collaboration, across the disciplinary boundaries of the history of science and mathematics, would not be easy. Still, as the current collaboration of historian of mathematics Volker Remmert of the Bergische Universität Wuppertal and group theorist Rebecca Waldecker of the Martin-Luther-Universität Halle-Wittenberg on a history of the classification of the finite simple groups promises, it might not be impossible. It could also provide the antidote for the kind of historiographically uninformed—if mathematically highly informed—“mathematical history” that Weil espoused.

Assuming that the problems of mathematical and historical expertise are solved, however, doing the technical history of American mathematics after 1950 also faces the challenges of sources. The printed sources are certainly there, but whereas historians of, say, American mathematics before 1950 can go to myriad archival repositories—like the Archives of American Mathematics at the University of Texas in Austin—to dig more deeply into the historical record, what does an archive look like in the computer age? “Correspondence” primarily means electronic e-mails not letters penned or typed and carefully housed in archival collections. “Drafts” mean, if one is lucky and computer files have not simply been overwritten, computer files generated on machines and in formats that may or may no longer exist. And, there is the problem of convincing mathematicians to make provisions for their archives.⁠
Footnote
16
 Perhaps a concerted effort to interview mathematicians like the Princeton Mathematics Department’s Oral History Project of the 1980s could help preserve something of what is invariably being lost. Admittedly, though, that would be a big undertaking and one difficult to fund.
16
On this issue, see Steve Batterson, Charles Curtis, Albert Lewis, and Karen Hunger Parshall, 
The value of mathematical archives
, Notices of the American Mathematical Society 
50
 (2003), 1409–1415.
✖

Finally, there is that problem of audience. To write the technical history of American mathematics after 1950, almost by definition, would limit the audience to mathematicians. Are mathematicians interested? Or would this risk being a literature without an audience now?⁠
Footnote
17
 Would it risk finding its audience only fifty or a hundred years from now? Who knows? What this question of audience suggests, though, is the desirability of greater dialogue between historians of mathematics and mathematicians. Maybe historians of mathematics have actually to prime their audience of mathematicians by convincing it that the questions being asked and answered and the approaches historians are trained to take are ultimately more illuminating than the “mathematical history” that Weil espoused. As Jeremy Gray said in his Focus piece in 
Isis
, “we have a lot to do.”
17
As Tinne Kjeldsen of the University of Copenhagen rightly commented to me during the INI workshop, however, advanced students of mathematics could also be a potential and appreciative audience.
✖

8
.

Concluding Remarks

Let me make three points by way of conclusion. First, there are many lacunae—deficiencies—in the history of American mathematics. Talking just about the period from 1920 to 1950, I can think of dozens of directions into which interested researchers could go, like the project that the University of Richmond’s Della Dumbaugh and her collaborators have pursued on the life and work of Princeton mathematician and early twentieth-century American mathematical leader, Oswald Veblen.⁠
Footnote
18
 If the question, however, is what is the 
main
 lacuna, then I hope that the historiographical argument I have put forth here for the technical history of American mathematics after 1950 has been convincing enough.
18
The fruits of this collaboration will soon appear in a special issue of the 
Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society
.
✖

Second, the isolation of the technical history of American mathematics after 1950 as a deficiency is by no means unique to the history of American mathematics. It is a deficiency in the history of mathematics full stop and one that has also been identified, for example, in the history of computing.⁠
Footnote
19
 To be sure, “mathematical history” in Weil’s sense has been done and can be useful to both mathematicians and to historians,⁠
Footnote
20
 but, as should be clear, the lacuna I have identified concerns analyses from a more historically sensitive point of view.

58
 (2015), no. 1, 40–44. I thank Ursula Martin of the University of Edinburgh for pointing out this reference to me—and Thomas Haigh of the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee for sending me a copy—during the course of the INI workshop.
✖

See, for example, Jean Dieudonné,

✖

Third, to paraphrase Armand Borel, a major figure in American mathematics in the period after 1950 and until his death in 2003, “I feel that what [the history of American] mathematics needs least are pundits who issue prescriptions or guidelines for presumably less enlightened mortals” 
4
, 
p. 452
. Let historians follow their own interests and hearts, and the field will be the richer for it.
