Memorial Tribute
In Memoriam: John Bligh Conway (1939–2024)
Frank E. Baginski
Wing Suet Li
Kenneth Stephenson
John Bligh Conway, professor emeritus of mathematics at George Washington University, died on February 17, 2024, at age 84. John is survived by his wife Ann, son Bligh, daughter-in-law Marina, and grandson Stephen.

A Mathematician’s Life
John Conway’s academic career as a mathematician from 1960 to 2024 came during a period of great change. He was a member of the mathematics faculty at Indiana University for over twenty years. He served thirteen years as the mathematics department head at University of Tennessee Knoxville (UTK), three years as a program director at the National Science Foundation (NSF), and five years as mathematics department chair at George Washington University (GWU). Strongly believing that the exchange of ideas is key to the development of mathematics and mathematicians, John organized and was the driving force behind numerous conferences and workshops, promoting new generations of analysts. To colleagues, he was collaborator, mentor, friend, and many combinations thereof. To students who benefited from his wise professional advice, he was a role model whose impact extended well beyond the classroom. In this tribute, we recount some of the stories of mathematician John Bligh Conway. We sort the stories into three chapters: Bloomington (Indiana University), Knoxville (University of Tennessee), and Washington DC (National Science Foundation and George Washington University). We end with stories and anecdotes from his personal life.
1.1
.
Bloomington, Indiana
John B. Conway earned his BS from Loyola University New Orleans in 1961. With fellowship support from the National Science Foundation, John began his graduate studies at Indiana University (IU) and then the Courant Institute before settling on Louisiana State University (LSU).
In 1965, John received his PhD from LSU under the direction of Heron S. Collins. His dissertation topic was
The Strict Topology and Compactness in the Space of Measures
. In 1965, he accepted a one-year appointment as assistant professor of mathematics at Indiana University. Needless to say, this led to a tenure-track appointment during which John initially focused on research related to his dissertation. After a few years, he began to explore new areas of analysis before gravitating to operators on Hilbert spaces and questions about von Neumann algebras, which continued to evolve over time.
John was assigned to teach complex analysis a number of times but was never satisfied with the style of the standard texts. He wrote detailed notes in his own style, got them typed up, and distributed them to his students. With some encouragement from Paul Halmos and students, John agreed to publish
Functions of one complex variable
4
in a new series of graduate texts being started by Springer-Verlag. A second edition was published in 1978
5
. In hindsight, John acknowledged that he would not recommend an untenured junior faculty to undertake the task of writing a book. However, in this instance, everything worked out fine.
Functions of one complex variable
is regarded as one of the classic graduate textbooks on the subject. John authored a total of twelve textbooks, including
A course in functional analysis
6
,
The theory of subnormal operators
7
, and
A course in operator theory
2
.
In the fall of 1970, John was awarded tenure and around that time agreed to be the assistant chair for undergraduate studies. John felt the role of a faculty member and colleague was “[to] do what is in your ability to help the department.” Later in his career, he expanded this point of view to include professional service to the mathematical community. He served as editor of the
Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society
(AMS), was elected to the AMS Council, and served two terms on the AMS Executive Committee.
Returning to Bloomington after his first sabbatical, he taught a two-semester course in functional analysis followed by a summer seminar. As he started to specialize in subnormal operators, he acquired his first two graduate students. In 1977, John was promoted to full professor. In that same year, John and his former student Bob Olin published
A Functional Calculus for Subnormal Operators

1.2
.
Knoxville, Tennessee
John’s first direct encounter with mathematics at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville (UTK) was as one of the principal speakers at a function theory conference in the late 1980s. He made a positive impression on the analysts at UTK, particularly Carl Sundberg and Ken Stephenson. When a search was opening for an outside Department Head, John was approached. This cold call seems to have occurred at just the right juncture. John had over 20 years and a wide variety of roles at Indiana. The headship at UTK seemed to be the right new challenge. Moreover, this was a time of general optimism in academia, and John saw opportunities for growth and for program building.
John lost no time in advancing the department’s case. His research stature and his widely popular textbooks gave an immediate boost to the Mathematics Department’s visibility both outside of UTK and within the administration. At UTK, as at many schools in the South, departments have “heads,” not “chairs,” the implication being that heads are the dean’s representatives in the departments. John sought books about the administrative dynamics of the job, but the references he found were dreadful and none were written by a mathematician. As he had done in the past, when he couldn’t find a text suitable to his liking, he wrote his own:
On Being a Department Head: A Personal View
8
. In this book, John’s shared wisdom extends well beyond being a mathematics department chair and has value to anyone in a leadership position. He clearly valued a diversity of opinions and understood well that leaders inhabit temporary functions. Whoever comes after you may undo what you have done, and you should not be upset about it. As with his previous publications, this book found a ready audience, resulting in invited talks to other, often nonmathematical, departments. The period of growth in his early tenure at UTK did not last. As the economic hardships of the 1990s settled in across academia, John and other heads were fighting fires. Fortunately, John largely shielded the department from a sometimes fickle administration. He had good instincts in pure mathematics and, as opportunities arose, identified areas to build up at UTK, differential geometry being a prime example. He was also very supportive of junior faculty members.
His time was not without some controversy, however, as his instincts did not match well with applied mathematics. The department’s highly regarded group in mathematical biology decamped for a new Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology. This new department went from strength to strength, and today maintains a strong relationship with the Mathematics Department. John learned from the experience, and with the help of a diverse “advisory committee,” he rebalanced the department, laying the groundwork for the combined strengths of today in both pure and applied areas.
John was adept at the daily mechanics of being head (recruiting, grants, Honors and REU programs, statewide math competitions, degree program development, etc.). He ran efficient faculty meetings that leaned toward democracy. For example, he promoted the voting rights and fair treatment of the growing population of nontenure-track faculty, issues that have plagued other mathematics departments. Always a strong proponent of the undergraduate program, he felt no conflict with simultaneously advancing research. While he was good at delegating responsibilities, he also set high standards and led by example.
In the early 1990s, John was one of the organizers of the Southeast Analysis Meeting (SEAM). He, along with Bob Olin, made sure that SEAM would take place every year. For SEAM 1995, John recruited Wing Suet Li as organizer. With John’s encouragement and with leftover funds from SEAM 1995, they co-organized the first YAMS (Young Analysts Meeting of the Southeast) in the Fall of 1995 at the Red Top Mountain State Park, north of Atlanta. With financial support from NSF, YAMS continued in fall of 1996 and 1997. It extended to a weeklong summer meeting at Furman University in 1999, 2001, and 2003. SEAM continues to this day.

Washington, DC
While department head at UTK, John took part in an NSF review panel for analysis research proposals in Fall 2002. At the conclusion of the panel, Bill Rundell, then director of the Division of Mathematical Sciences (DMS) at NSF, contacted John to gauge his interest in coming to NSF as a DMS program director rotator for two or three years. John thought that such a position might be an interesting way to ease into retirement. He accepted an Intergovernmental Personnel Act (temporary) appointment and was hired to manage two programs that were focused on fostering and improving undergraduate major programs and the education of beginning graduate students, programs close to John’s heart. John recounts his experiences in
3
.
John began contemplating other opportunities toward the end of this second year at NSF because moving back to UTK would be complicated. As luck would have it, the Mathematics Department at George Washington University (GWU) was conducting a search for an outside chair. This position had the advantage of postponing retirement, and there would be no need to relocate. To get to work at GWU, John needed only five extra stops on the Orange Line to the GWU-Foggy Bottom station. When offered the position of department chair at GWU, he accepted! John officially retired from UTK in Spring 2006 and began his first term at GWU in Fall 2006.
As part of the terms of the offer, during each of his first three years, John was given a departmental stipend to enhance the mathematics programs and he was promised two additional tenure-track lines, which eventually were honored by GWU administration. During his five years at GWU, John worked to improve the organization of the department staff and the undergraduate and graduate programs. In June 2011, John Conway was awarded emeritus status at GWU. Technically, John did not actually retire. In Fall 2011, he taught undergraduate real analysis at GWU.

Personal Life
John married Ann Flattery, his high school sweetheart, on August 22, 1964, while he was a graduate student at LSU. See Figure
4
. Ann probably had as much to do with John transferring to LSU as did anything else. Although John and Ann did not do much traveling while growing up, a few road trips in the late 1960s infected them with a travel bug that grew with time and continued well into retirement. John’s research collaborations enabled extended stops that began with a research visit to UC Berkeley in the summer of 1968. Their son, John Bligh Conway, Jr., was born in May 1971. Having a young child did little to inhibit their desire to travel.
Figure 4
.
John and Ann wed on August 22, 1964.
Accompanied by Ann and a 9-month-old Bligh, John began his first sabbatical in January 1972 at the Free University of Amsterdam, where, with the help of Rien Kaashoek, he arranged to have an office and faculty privileges. The family’s stay in Amsterdam was preceded by two weeks in Paris. John felt that he introduced the baby pack to France. See Figure
5
. Their time in Amsterdam was interrupted by road trips to France, Germany, and Italy. It was not until they reached Italy that they found a restaurant with infant highchairs. They celebrated Bligh’s first birthday in Portofino, and their travels included visits to Normandy, Ghent, and London before returning to Bloomington.
Figure 5
.
Introducing the baby pack to France in 1972.
By the time John and Ann moved to Knoxville, they had traveled to just about every state in the US and were experienced international travelers, always enjoying fine dining accompanied by excellent wines. In the 1980s, a trip to the former Soviet Union to collaborate with Nikolai Nikolski and his research group at the Steklov Institute in Leningrad (now St. Petersburg) would turn out to have a profound influence on the life of the Conway family. Their son Bligh ended up living in Russia, where he married and had their grandson Stephen. They found that getting together was easiest if John and Ann went to Europe, and for several years, they arranged festive family gatherings that moved among various major European cities during Christmas holidays.
Figure 6
.
French House.
France was always a special favorite of Ann’s. They loved French culture, French restaurants, and especially French wines. This eventually led to the purchase of a small cottage on the Cap Sizun peninsula on the coast of France. See Figure
6
. As they spent more time in France, Ann became quite fluent in French while John worked to keep up. In one instance, this resulted in a remarkable discovery: according to a French pharmacist, the burgundy wine Pommard could cure a wound! John realized afterward that his American ear did not distinguish between the wine name and the French word
pommade
for ointment.
John’s official retirement in 2011 allowed for extended trips that included a cruise to Alaska. In 2022, an excursion by train through Italy took John and Ann back to Portofino, Italy, the city where they had celebrated Bligh’s first birthday.
Figure 7
.
Stephen with John’s model train set in Knoxville.
As much as John enjoyed the mental challenge of doing mathematics, he also stayed anchored by using his hands to build things. On a Christmas Eve when Bligh was five or six, John decided to offer him a train set. He stayed up late to put it together and had the set running when Bligh came down on Christmas morning. Bligh took one look and asked John, “So that’s it?” Over the next twenty-five years, John became a skilled carpenter and electrician. He built more and more elaborate sets, with tunnels through mountains and bridges over valleys, sheep populating the hills and people waiting on the platforms. In Bloomington, the model train set occupied half of a two-car garage, but in Knoxville, John had a two-car garage built for the purpose of housing his increasingly elaborate model train set. John never managed to impress Bligh, but he won the delight and admiration of his grandson Stephen. See Figure
7
. With a clear sense of priorities, John made a point to run his train set every day as a way to refresh his focus.
Over his career John saw many changes in the way university professors lead their lives. To his foreign colleagues, his outlook, his demeanor, and the time he devoted to others characterize the American mathematician of his generation. Once, when discussing France and the US, John was asked what it means to be an American. His answer: “To be optimistic.”
