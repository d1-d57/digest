Education
The Federal Role in Ensuring High-Quality, Useful Educational Data
Morgan Polikoff
Rachel Anderson
Communicated by
Notices
Associate Editor Yvonne Lai
Editorial Note: The goal of this article is to help the mathematical sciences community understand the role of the federal government in the construction of educational databases and to describe some of the questions that such databases can be used to investigate. –YL
The quality of US pre-K to postsecondary education systems is rightly understood to be critically important for both individuals and broader society. Education is seen as a tool for upward mobility, as exemplified by President Johnson upon signing an act that provided landmark federal funding for public education
USC65
:
As a son of a tenant farmer, I know that education is the only valid passport from poverty. As a former teacher—and, I hope, a future one—I have great expectations of what this law will mean for all of our young people. As President of the United States, I believe deeply no law I have signed or will ever sign means more to the future of America.
Education is also seen as a tool for strengthening our shared prosperity—for developing our collective skills and bringing about mathematical, technological, and economic advancement for all of us.
Despite the importance of education, research on education is not—in our opinion—prioritized nor appropriately supported by the federal government. And, as of July 2025, it appears that the federal government will further decrease education research funding in the coming years.
This commentary aims to demystify educational data and research and show how federal education investments provide value, including to the future of the mathematical sciences. The federal role in education is circumscribed, but perhaps the two most important areas that it impacts are a) ensuring that students’ civil rights are protected and b) supporting educational data and research. It doesn’t matter who you are—mathematician, educator, academic, parent or nonparent, taxpayer, employer—there are reasons to support federal investments in gathering high-quality educational data, enabling it to be used for high-quality educational research.
To approach the issue of why federal education research and data efforts are important, we went straight to the source—the website of the National Center for Educational Statistics (NCES), a statistical agency within the federal Institute of Education Sciences (IES). We looked at the projects that they feature on their site and thought about what those projects say about the federal contribution. In the rest of this commentary, we extract four specific examples from this list of projects (the “Nation’s Report Card,” longitudinal national data, state level data, and research and training efforts) and use them to illustrate the sorts of general conclusions that they obtain.
1
.
Outcomes Matter: Only the Federal Government Can Track Them Nationally and Ensure Comparability
The best known federal education function is probably “NAEP,” the National Assessment of Educational Progress, also called the Nation’s Report Card. NAEP includes mathematics tests given mostly to students in the fourth and eighth grades, as well as assessments of English language arts and other subjects and grades in a rotating and evolving cycle. NAEP is the only national assessment that is regularly given to randomly selected, representative samples of American students, which allows for inferences about student achievement at the state and national levels. Because the test is developed with high standards for quality and uses consistent frameworks year over year, results can be compared longitudinally and across jurisdictions in ways that are not possible with any other assessment.
This kind of effort cannot be replicated in any way other than through direct participation by the federal government. In particular, only the federal government can compel sampled schools to participate. It is true that many states administer their own standardized tests, but these cannot be easily compared without a linking assessment. Indeed, NAEP most often provides that link and is responsible for most high-quality efforts that compare student performance using state test data
EOP
. NAEP is overseen by an independent, nonpartisan, congressionally appointed board that is intended to ensure both its technical quality and its independence from political interference.
Why should we care about student scores on NAEP, in mathematics and in other areas? At a high level, students’ standardized test scores matter. Recent analyses make clear that, at both the individual and aggregate level, student test scores are highly correlated with economic outcomes. That is, changes in state-average NAEP scores are strongly associated with the income, educational attainment, and other important long-term outcomes of children born in later cohorts, so improvements in state NAEP scores lead to real and consequential improvements in the life outcomes of later generations
DKPS25
. Test scores also provide a useful external check on student grades and graduation rates, which are often not comparable across schools and districts. We need to know how well our children are learning and whether things are headed in the right direction, and that is only possible nationally through NAEP.
The data from NAEP also have important uses in research and policy evaluation, especially for evaluating the effects of state policy changes, which are often difficult to evaluate using only within-state samples. For example, high-profile evaluations of the federal accountability policy No Child Left Behind used NAEP data to isolate the impact on student math knowledge, finding positive effects that matched the large descriptive increases in national math performance during that period
DJ11
. As another example, recent research has leveraged NAEP data alongside data on the staggered implementation of state spending reforms to show that educational spending strongly affects student performance
Jac20
. If not for NAEP, these kinds of analyses could only have been done using within-state data, limiting the kinds of analyses and the scope of the conclusions.
2
.
Federal Longitudinal Studies Support Rigorous Academic Research
Studying many of the most important questions in education requires a long time horizon. For instance, mathematicians may be interested in students’ long-term mathematical learning, STEM degree attainment, and success in college and in math-related fields in the workforce. Collecting longitudinal data to answer questions of this sort is prohibitive for individual researchers, since the labor and financial costs associated with selecting representative samples, managing follow-up, and overseeing the secure use of such data are enormous.
NCES takes the lead in carrying out these kinds of large-scale studies and making the data available to researchers. These studies often focus on particular age ranges or populations and cover important transitions. For instance, the Early Childhood Longitudinal Studies cover developmental trajectories from birth through elementary school; the Educational Longitudinal Study (and before that High School and Beyond) cover high school transitions into college and beyond; and the National Teacher and Principal Survey (and before that the Schools and Staffing Survey) cover working conditions and attitudes of American teachers. These are all large, complex studies that would have been impossible without government support.
A related type of data collected by the federal government and often used by researchers is federal IPEDS (Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System) data. These are gathered for transparency and accountability purposes, rather than research purposes, but they provide administrative data on every institution of higher education receiving federal financial aid. The data are widely used in research and are essential for understanding trends in college enrollment.
It is hard to overstate the impact of these large-scale, longitudinal data collections on the state of knowledge about American education. For instance, a Google Scholar search for “Early Childhood Longitudinal Study” (ECLS) turns up more than 20,000 studies. The design of ECLS allows researchers to answer questions about many issues, including children’s developmental trajectories from early childhood into K–12 (state datasets rarely go earlier than 2nd or 3rd grade) and the effects of children’s home environments on their educational outcomes (these kinds of data are never included in state datasets and are notoriously difficult to collect at scale). ECLS data have provided essential insights about math-related issues such as: (a) children’s early mathematical achievement trajectories and how they differ for students from various sociodemographic groups (e.g.,
RB11
); (b) the relationships of home environments (e.g., technology access;
ELWS06
) and school policies (e.g., full- versus half-day kindergarten;
PC19
) with students’ mathematics skill development; and (c) the associations of specific parent and teacher behaviors with children’s early mathematics learning
SBCH21
. While some of these findings could be replicated in smaller-scale studies in individual locales, the design and technical quality of these federal studies are the only way to ensure that the conclusions are representative of our diverse nation.
Similarly, a Google Scholar search for “IPEDS math” shows more than 15,000 results, including a study of trends in bachelor’s degree recipients in mathematics
BMV16
and an analysis of participation in alternatives to developmental mathematics courses in college
Nor17
. And the National Teacher and Principal Survey has been used to study teacher shortages in mathematics and other fields, as well as the working conditions of mathematics teachers in schools, again providing nationwide snapshots that would not otherwise be feasible
Cul23
.
These researcher-driven studies are possible because NCES offers researchers both public-use and restricted-use versions of their datasets. Restricted-use licenses require researchers to abide by stringent data security requirements that ensure there is little risk of inappropriate data disclosure. But NCES has also established increasingly useful public versions of the datasets that allow anyone with interest to access many of the most relevant variables and conduct their own analyses. For example, researchers can see the demographics of students seeking degrees in mathematics and statistics by Carnegie Classification, and they can create data dashboards of mathematics and statistics majors by state and Carnegie Classification
TPSE
.
3
.
Federal Investments Support States’ Unique Role
Although the federal data sources described above are illuminating, there is no federal data system that has individual-level data on all K–12 and college students. In fact, in acknowledgement of individuals’ privacy and the limited federal role in the provision of education, there are numerous federal laws that explicitly prohibit this type of database, including the Education Sciences Reform Act 20 U.S.C. § 9572, the Every Student Succeeds Act 20 U.S.C. § 7911, the Higher Education Act 20 U.S.C. § 1015c, and the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act 29 U.S.C. § 3341.
This is where states come in. With financial and technical assistance from the US Department of Education, nearly every state now has a statewide longitudinal data system that securely connects individual-level data sets from different state agencies serving students as they move through school and into the workforce
DQC23
. While states initially built these education data systems to facilitate their federal reporting and accountability activities, most state leaders now embrace their data system as a rich, actionable resource for educators, policymakers, and researchers.
Since statewide longitudinal data systems can securely bring together data from across a state’s early learning, K–12, postsecondary, and workforce agencies, they have the potential to provide valuable insights about different populations of students’ educational experiences and outcomes. State education data systems are invaluable resources for supporting both large-scale research and short-term inquiries from state and district leaders trying to make more immediate curriculum, staffing, and programming decisions for their schools. For example, the data from state systems enables researchers and state and school district leaders to answer questions such as:
•
Which high school students have access to advanced mathematics courses, and how does that access impact their learning and post-high school trajectories?
•
Which K–12 policies predict increased persistence, retention, and completion in college? For example, is this true of dual enrollment programs allowing high school students to take higher education classes, advanced math opportunities such as AP and IB courses, youth apprenticeship programs, etc.
•
How does college enrollment vary across high school students from different K–12 districts, regions, and backgrounds?
•
How do employment and wage outcomes vary for students who complete different training, credential, and degree programs?
States carefully and transparently govern the use of the data in these systems to ensure that individuals’ privacy is always protected and that the research being conducted is beneficial to the public.
Most of these state-level systems would not have been created or maintained at the current level of quality without the federal support that they have received. Over the last 20 years, it has been federal funding, programs, and expertise that have most helped states in building out their own longitudinal data systems
DQC17
.
The US Department of Education’s Statewide Longitudinal Data System grant program was specifically designed to support states in building and improving their education data systems
NCES
. Across eight rounds of funding since 2009, all 50 states plus five territories and the District of Columbia have received at least one Statewide Longitudinal Data System grant. States most often use this relatively small influx of federal funding, historically between $1.5 and $7 million, to supplement their own investments in education data and research and support all types of policy-relevant endeavors. For example, Alabama used a federal Statewide Longitudinal Data System grant and state funds to create the Alabama Terminal on Linking and Analyzing Statistics, a data analysis tool that allows state agencies to conduct research on their education and workforce outcomes. One recent Alabama effort is an analysis of the credentials earned in different regions of the state, including credentials in the science, technology, engineering, and math fields. Utah is another state that has used federal and state funds to support its longitudinal data system. The Utah Data Research Center integrates data from five state agencies
UDRCa
, including K–12 and higher education agencies, with the goal of enabling effective policy solutions. One of the six areas of study outlined in the Center’s 2025 research agenda
UDRCb
is the relationship between taking math courses in high school and outcomes through college and the workforce.
By supporting states to build, maintain, improve, and use these powerful state data systems, federal investments have been tremendously impactful for research and education improvement efforts.
4
.
NCES and IES Directly Support Essential Education Research and Training
Finally, we consider the federal Institute of Education Sciences (IES), which is an important and influential funder of education research. IES invests in education research across roughly a dozen topic areas that cover every grade level from early childhood through adult education. Their grants have historically been focused on research designs that can support causal inferences, e.g., randomized experiments, high-quality quasi-experimental designs, but they also support exploratory and developmental research. According to the IES website, since the inception of the agency they have funded 484 contracts and grants related to mathematics. As of July, 2025, currently open mathematically related contracts and grants include AI-based interventions to improve students’ mathematics understanding and engagement; a meta-analysis of interventions to support early learners struggling with mathematics; and impact evaluations of specific mathematics curricula. They also have typically funded one or two large research centers per year focused on a specific topic of current importance, for example, the lead author was a co-Principal Investigator of a $10 million 2015 center on college- and career-ready standards in mathematics and English language arts.
Beyond directly funding individual research studies, IES funds training grants that go to universities to support targeted training of researchers. These training grants have been hugely important for developing the pipeline of educational researchers who can conduct high-quality education research. (The lead author was trained in IES-funded predoctoral programs at both Vanderbilt and Penn.) These funds allow universities to offer more competitive stipends to prospective students, to create course offerings that are better tailored to specific methodological approaches or fields of study, and to support students and postdoctoral researchers to carry out cutting-edge research, which often leverages the NCES and state datasets mentioned earlier. For instance, during 2009–2014 IES funded a training program on “Research in Cognition and Mathematics Education” at UC Berkeley that trained 35 predoctoral scholars, many of whom are now university-based mathematics education researchers.
Finally, IES has supported the creation of resources intended to more directly marshal evidence to provide concrete guidance to schools and districts about effective policies and practices. For instance, IES has created practice guides on specific topics that offer detailed recommendations, alongside ratings of the strength of evidence. An example of such a guide is “Assisting students struggling with mathematics: Intervention in the early grades.” While there is always room to strengthen and increase adoption of such practical resources, these efforts are already important and influential.
5
.
Key Functions Imperiled by Recent Government Actions
The federal government has long been a singularly important source and supporter of trustworthy and useful data on our complex education systems. From conducting research on what works in classrooms, to making data available for other researchers, to supporting states in building out their own data and research capabilities, the Department of Education has provided the data, research, guidance, expertise, and financial support that simply isn’t available anywhere else. But this federal role is rapidly changing.
As with so many areas of government, the Trump administration’s personnel and programmatic cuts in the first half of 2025 had an immediate impact on our nation’s education sector. The Department of Government Efficiency sidelined scores of US Department of Education staff, terminated dozens of IES programs and contracts, and canceled ongoing data analysis and research. These cuts have already resulted in previously publicly available federal data sets and statistical reports disappearing or no longer being updated, near-complete research projects being halted, and data-related technical assistance for states disappearing.
The pace and scope of these changes is overwhelming, so we find it more useful to focus on whether the federal government is fulfilling its necessary data functions, rather than whether the exact historical structures are being maintained. Unfortunately, even using that focus, it is clear that we are losing necessary resources. For example, the volume of layoffs from the Department of Education has made it all but impossible to carry out core functions of the Department; as of the time of this writing (November 2025), many federal data collections other than NAEP have been terminated, and certain NAEP data collections have been postponed or canceled. And while librarians, archivists, and education policy institutions scramble to download existing federal data sets as they disappear from government websites, this invaluable information on education opportunities and outcomes will likely become increasingly outdated and hard to find.
As we’ve argued here, up-to-date, complete, and reliable education data is critical for conducting both long-term education research and real-time analysis for decision-making. And having access to transparent, complete data also functions as a needed check on official narratives and easy assumptions. So, for mathematicians interested in supporting federal efforts to improve education data and research, what approaches should they advocate?
(1)
Measure what matters.
Federal education data collections should be organized around several key goals. They should help the nation understand whether widely shared education priorities like increasing academic achievement are being met. They should support research into what works well, for whom, and under what conditions. They should protect the civil rights of all students and workers. And they should provide state and local education leaders with the research infrastructure and support necessary for them to determine whether they are using federal dollars effectively.
(2)
Make data use possible.
Federal agencies can take steps to ensure that they are making the data that they collect useful, and they can help states do the same. For their own data, federal policymakers can focus on making sure that their data sets and statistical analyses are timely, thorough, and posted online in formats that are easy to find and use. For states, federal policymakers should continue to support states in building, improving, and using their own data systems and developing their own analytic and research capabilities.
(3)
Protect privacy and build trust.
Education data has been such a valuable tool for supporting student success and systemic improvement in large part because families, educators, and the public can trust that every individual’s privacy is being protected and used ethically. Federal leaders have a responsibility to ensure that they are transparent about what data is collected and how it is used, that there are clear processes in place for researchers to request data, and that data is used only for the purposes for which it was collected.
We conclude where we began, acknowledging that we all have a stake in the success of our education systems. While the federal government’s role in strengthening education is modest, their data and research activities are essential to promoting educational improvement. There is no other organization that can step into the void of a substantially diminished US Department of Education, but we hope at least the core data and research functions of the Department will continue to be supported regardless of the party in power in Washington.
