Recovering a Lost Masterpiece of Analysis: The Burned Third Edition of de La Vallée Poussin’s 
Cours d’Analyse infinitésimale
Jean Mawhin
Augusto Ponce
Michel Willem
Communicated by 
Notices
 Associate Editor Adrian C. Rice

1
.

Introduction

In August 1914, during the first weeks of World War I, the Belgian city of Louvain (Leuven, in Dutch) was the scene of a tragedy whose effects reverberated across the academic world. The university library, one of Europe’s leading scholarly collections (Figure 
1
), was set ablaze and completely destroyed. Its destruction caused an international outcry, particularly in the United States and in several European countries. Along with 300,000 books and countless manuscripts, the fire consumed a mathematical treasure: the third edition of Volume II of Charles–Jean de La Vallée Poussin’s 
Cours d’Analyse infinitésimale
, then still in press at the Uystpruyst printing house adjacent to the library.

Figure 1
.
Baroque interior of the Louvain University Library before 1914.

De La Vallée Poussin himself described the event with characteristic restraint, noting that the nearly completed edition “was supposed to appear at the end of 1914 but has been burnt in Louvain with many other more precious things” 
dLVP15
. This lost edition was not simply a new printing, but the mathematically richest version of a foundational treatise. More than a century later, through the unexpected discovery of surviving galley proofs kept in the archive of the de La Vallée Poussin family, it has become possible to reconstruct a substantial part of what vanished in 1914. This article tells the story of that reconstruction carried out in 
MP25
 and the historical significance of what it restores.

2
.

Charles–Jean de La Vallée Poussin: Mathematician and Teacher

De La Vallée Poussin (1866–1962) was one of the most influential Belgian mathematicians of the early twentieth century. Best known for his proof of the prime number theorem, proven independently by Jacques Hadamard at around the same time, he also contributed decisively to measure theory, Fourier series, complex analysis, approximation, and potential theory. He served as the first president of the International Mathematical Union (IMU) in 1920 and taught analysis at Louvain from 1892 onward.

His 
Cours d’Analyse infinitésimale

dLVP03
dLVP06
—a two-volume treatise first published in 1903–1906—quickly became the foundation for teaching analysis in Belgium and an influential reference abroad. Remarkably, Volume I went through twelve editions (the last in 1959) and Volume II through nine (the last in 1957).

The work is celebrated for its clarity, elegance, and the way it introduced cutting-edge ideas directly into undergraduate education. The 1909 second edition of Volume I was the first textbook to present Lebesgue’s theory of integration, only a few years after its creation. The now-lost third edition (1914) of Volume II represented the culmination of these developments.

After World War I, the textbook largely returned to the content of the first edition and remained essentially unchanged in the many subsequent editions 
Maw14
. Its elegance and rigor remained, but most of the early twentieth-century advances in integration theory were dropped, creating a gap in the history of the subject and in our understanding of how de La Vallée Poussin blended research and teaching.

3
.

The Sack of Louvain and the Disappearance of the Third Edition

In late August 1914, shortly after German troops entered Louvain, they set fire to parts of the historic center, including the university library and the printing house preparing the third edition of Volume II. Photographs of the ruins reveal the remains of the baroque library interior and the devastated façade (Figure 
2
), with the rubble of the nearby workshops 
CDR05
.

Figure 2
.
Ruins of the Louvain University Library in August 1914. Postcard issued as 
The Sack of Louvain
, Brussels: Nels–Thill.

The reaction was especially strong in the United States, where the destruction of a medieval university library was widely perceived as an attack on learning itself, at a time when American universities strongly identified with the European academic tradition. Newspapers across the country reported the loss as a symbol of the war’s assault on learning and culture. In response, American universities organized an unprecedented fundraising effort to rebuild the library. The new building (Figure 
3
)—designed by the American architect Whitney Warren and inaugurated in the 1920s—was financed largely by donations from US institutions, students, and faculty. The reconstruction became a powerful gesture of international academic solidarity, and the library remains today one of the most visible reminders of this American support.

Figure 3
.
Design for the new Louvain University Library, rebuilt with American support after World War I. Watercolor, ink wash, and gouache on board.

As far as de La Vallée Poussin’s 
Cours
 is concerned, the loss was total. His own testimony in his 1915 
Transactions of the AMS
 article 
dLVP15
 confirms that “the third edition was in the press during the fire of Louvain. The Germans burnt this volume: all the materials of my publisher indeed shared the fate of the university library.” In that paper, de La Vallée Poussin reproduced the lectures he delivered at Harvard University during the academic year 1914–15 on portions of the new material related to the Lebesgue integral—material that had just been destroyed. By dedicating the article to Harvard, he acknowledged the generous invitation that had enabled him to leave Belgium following the Sack of Louvain.

Postwar editions of the 
Cours
 did not restore this content 
Maw14
. As de La Vallée Poussin explained in the 
Avertissements
 to the 1920s editions, reconstruction of the lost edition would have required resources and time unavailable in the immediate aftermath of the war, owing to the “serious material difficulties.” He thus removed the sections on the Lebesgue integral not for mathematical reasons, and expected to write a new Volume III that never appeared. The result was that the most advanced treatment of integration theory he ever wrote in a textbook vanished from the historical record.

Figure 4
.
Charles-Jean de La Vallée Poussin, ca. 1900.

4
.

What the Third Edition Contained

Unlike the later editions, the 1914 version of Volume II relied on conceptual improvements based on twenty years of research and extended Lebesgue’s ideas in several directions:

•

a refined theory of multiple Lebesgue integrals;

•

a systematic treatment of additive set functions with bounded variation, influenced by Lebesgue’s 1910 memoir 
Leb10
;

•

new results on nets and derivatives of set functions;

•

a more modern integration-by-substitution theorem;

•

developments related to generalized second derivatives and Fourier series.

The second edition of Volume II (1912) already contained substantial innovations—some preceding or independent of those of Nikolai Luzin 
Luz12
—but the third edition was even more ambitious, incorporating results that de La Vallée Poussin had recently obtained or refined. Had it survived, it would today be recognized as the most advanced early textbook treatment of Lebesgue integration and its applications.

It is worth noting that the third edition of Volume I, completed shortly before the war, did appear in print. In that volume, de La Vallée Poussin refined his exposition of Lebesgue integration, with an emphasis on the set-theoretic foundations of measurability and on the construction of the integral in one dimension. The lost third edition of Volume II was intended as a natural continuation of this program, extending it to several variables.

In the absence of Volume II, the third edition of Volume I of the 
Cours
 remained the most advanced early textbook exposition of Lebesgue integration, but it was essentially limited to the one-dimensional theory and its set-theoretic foundations. The second edition of Volume II came closest to realizing the broader program concerning multidimensional Lebesgue integration, yet it did not incorporate the full range of conceptual and technical developments planned for the third edition. Its disappearance therefore left a genuine gap in the evolution of the 
Cours
, since only part of the intended revision survived.

5
.

The Unexpected Discovery of Surviving Galley Proofs

For decades, mathematicians believed that the third edition had been completely lost. The only glimpse of its intended content came from the final sections of de La Vallée Poussin’s 1915 
Transactions
 paper on the Lebesgue integral 
dLVP15
. This changed when, during the preparation of de La Vallée Poussin’s 
Collected Works

dLVP00
, one of the authors (J.M.) was shown a bundle of old galley proofs preserved by a great-grandson of the mathematician. Among them were several chapters from the lost edition of Volume II.

These proofs included:

•

the end of Chapter II (improper integrals, exact differentials);

•

the complete Chapter III (multiple Riemann and Lebesgue integrals);

•

the beginning of Chapter IV (analytical representation of functions and Fourier series).

This material offered, for the first time, a substantial picture of the missing edition. It also included annotated pages from the second edition that had been prepared for revision (Figure 
5
). We have no information about possible modifications to the remaining chapters of the third edition of Volume II. However, those chapters concerned more classical topics that were largely unaffected by the developments that followed the introduction of the Lebesgue integral. They included, for example, the fundamental theory of ordinary differential equations, introductory material on linear partial and total differential equations, basic notions of the calculus of variations and finite differences, and selected applications of calculus to geometry.

Figure 5
.
Galley proofs from Volume II: annotated second-edition page (top) and corresponding third-edition page (bottom).

6
.

Reconstructing the Lost Edition

Using these galley proofs as a foundation, the reconstruction underlying our edition 
MP25
 followed two principles: to preserve the exact content of the proofs whenever they existed and to insert the annotated second edition when the third-edition proofs were missing.

The surviving proofs covered Sections 67–140. Sections 141–160 were reconstructed entirely from the second edition, incorporating the author’s planned changes visible in his annotations. The result is a volume approximately 28 pages longer than the published 1912 edition.

Most sections—especially later, more classical topics—could not be reconstructed and are reproduced in 
MP25
 as in the 1912 version, for completeness and historical context. While we have endeavored to stay true to the “ghost” third edition of Volume II, the reproduction of these chapters represents our best attempt to remain faithful to the structure and spirit of the original work.

7
.

Why This Reconstruction Matters

Publishing the reconstruction fills a missing link in the evolution of de La Vallée Poussin’s ideas on Lebesgue integration and additive set functions. Until now, the main landmarks in this development were the second edition (and the first volume of the third edition) of his 
Cours

dLVP03
dLVP06
, his Harvard lectures published in the 
Transactions

dLVP15
, and the monograph 
dLVP95
 based on his course at the Collège de France during the winter of 1915–16. As explained, none of these sources fully realized the broader program developed for the third edition of Volume II with regard to multidimensional integration.

More generally, the reconstruction also fills a missing link in the early history of integration theory. It shows:

•

how Lebesgue’s ideas were integrated into the curriculum just a decade after their creation;

•

how de La Vallée Poussin’s research shaped his teaching and vice versa;

•

how ideas on primitives, convergence, and multidimensional integration evolved;

•

how mathematical knowledge can be lost through war, fire, and circumstance.

It clarifies how early analysts understood and taught Lebesgue’s ideas before measure theory took its modern form. It also revives the most mathematically ambitious version of a treatise that profoundly influenced generations of analysts. Many senior mathematicians recall the 
Cours
 as the book that shaped their early mathematical sensibility, praising its balance between clarity, rigor, and originality.

8
.

Conclusion

The reconstructed third edition 
MP25
 of Volume II–published more than a century after its disappearance–restores to the mathematical community a lost piece of its heritage. While it can never replace the original object destroyed in 1914, it brings new light to de La Vallée Poussin’s contributions and to the formation of Lebesgue integration as a central component of twentieth-century analysis.

It also stands as a reminder of the fragile, material nature of mathematical culture. Ideas may endure, but books and manuscripts can vanish. Recovering them, when possible, reconnects us with the lived history of mathematics and with the moments when new theories first entered the world.
