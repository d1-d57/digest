Memorial Tribute

Shmuel Agmon (1922–2025)
Matania Ben-Artzi
Ira W. Herbst
Yehuda Pinchover

Career Overview

Professor Shmuel Agmon passed away on March 21, 2025, at the blessed age of 103. Shmuel was born in Tel Aviv (then in the British Mandate for Palestine, which later became Israel) on February 2, 1922. His father, Natan Agmon (Bistritzky), was a respected writer and Zionist activist. His mother, Haya née Gutman, was a dentist. She ran a clinic in the Arab city of Nazareth, where Shmuel spent the first two years of his life. The family then moved to Jerusalem, where his younger brother Yaakov was born in 1924. In the year 1927, Shmuel’s mother took her two children to visit her parents in Ukraine, and Shmuel well remembered Stalin’s rise.

Figure 1
.
Shmuel Agmon at AgmonFest, 2007.

The boy Shmuel’s first encounter with serious mathematics came by chance. When he fell ill with a prolonged childhood illness, his mother offered to bring him a gift. The average child would probably ask for chocolate or a toy, but Shmuel specifically asked for a book—and not just any book, but a calculus book by A. Baruch, an old-fashioned volume (in Hebrew) that captivated him with its charm.

At the Jerusalem Gymnasium (officially the “Hebrew Gymnasium”), the young Shmuel was already recognized as a brilliant student, including in mathematics of course, but also in classical literature. His attachment to literature remained deep throughout his life, as was well known to all his friends and students.

In 1940, with the sounds of World War II already in the distance, Shmuel enrolled in the mathematics department of the Hebrew University. The university was then a small institution with a total of about 800 students in the Mount Scopus campus. Shmuel chose physics and philosophy as additional subjects and began a direct path to a master’s degree. Employing an ancient Hebrew phrase, he later recalled, “That was my entrance into the orchard. It was only then that I began to understand what mathematics was.”

The small Mathematics Institute had very few but prominent professors: Avraham Halevi Fraenkel, a founder of axiomatic set theory; Michael Fekete, who specialized in complex analysis; Yaakov Levitsky, one of the founders of modern algebra; Benjamin Amira, an assistant of the number theorist Edmund Landau (who founded the Mathematics Institute and then returned to Göttingen).

Before he could enjoy this small, high-quality intellectual environment, Shmuel was called to the flag. It was in 1942 when he voluntarily enlisted in the British Army and was assigned to an antiaircraft unit. He served with the Jewish Brigade in Cyprus, Southern Italy, and Belgium, and was discharged in 1946.

Shmuel returned to the Hebrew University, where he chose to focus on mathematics. He soon wrote an original proof of Hadamard’s theorem, which deals with the representation of analytic functions. Upon completion of his masters degree, he married his girlfriend Galia Yardeni. Galia herself went on to be a scholar and writer in the field of literature, sharing this passion with Shmuel.

Visiting the university at that time was Hadamard’s student and prominent mathematician, Szolem Mandelbrojt, whose nephew is the well-known mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot. S. Mandelbrojt dealt with the theory of complex functions, a subject that greatly intrigued Shmuel. He asked for a leave of absence to pursue his studies in Paris under Mandelbrojt’s supervision. His military service was credited to him, and the university administration granted him the requested leave.

A few months later, Shmuel and Galia found themselves on a ship on their way to the Sorbonne University in France. During his doctoral studies at the Sorbonne, Agmon investigated singularities of Dirichlet series 
1
. His doctorate was a great success, and he completed it in just two years. On Mandelbrojt’s advice he applied to Rice University in the United States. In the United States, Galia and Shmuel had their two eldest sons, who would become scientists themselves: Noam is a professor of theoretical chemistry at the Hebrew University, and Ariel is a professor of neurobiology in West Virginia.

While at Rice University, Agmon realized that research in complex functions was losing its central role in analysis, and he started looking for other fields of interest within mathematical analysis. In 1950 he attended the first World Congress of Mathematics held after World War II. At that event in Cambridge, Massachusetts, his good friend from Paris, Laurent Schwartz, was awarded the Fields Medal.

Schwartz had just published his revolutionary “Theory of Distributions” that gave a new impetus to the field of Partial Differential Equations (PDEs). Schwartz’s work impressed Agmon, leading him to start studying this field. On the way back home, he stopped at New York University, where he met Louis Nirenberg, an extraordinary young mathematician. In a chance conversation between the two, Nirenberg discovered an error in one of Agmon’s proofs, which enhanced their mutual appreciation. They became friends and wrote an article together, in which they corrected the error. Later, they published several additional joint works, the most notable of which dealt with elliptic PDEs 
7
8
.

Figure 2
.
Agmon with L. Nirenberg at AgmonFest, 2007.

In 1952, a vacancy opened that allowed Agmon to return to Jerusalem and accept a position at the Hebrew University. He was appointed to the chair of analytical mechanics, a mathematical topic that was mandatory to all physics students in their second year. The course that he taught touched on several PDEs, including in particular the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and the Euler-Lagrange equation, which allowed him to expand his familiarity with differential equations. During that period, Shmuel and Galia’s third son, Eytan, was born. He later became a professor of musicology at the Bar-Ilan University in Israel.

In the 1950s, Agmon worked on various aspects of elliptic-hyperbolic equations, notably the Tricomi equation. On one of his trips to the Courant Institute at New York University, which was then a young institution and has since become one of the world’s renowned centers of mathematics, Agmon discovered that his old friend Nirenberg and Avron Douglis had published a paper on elliptic equations that avoided some central questions. The collaboration between Agmon, Douglis, and Nirenberg, which took place in correspondence by airmail over many months, gave rise to two seminal works 
7
8
 on the general theory of elliptic partial differential boundary value problems. Agmon’s subsequent 1965 book on linear boundary value problems for elliptic PDEs of general order 
2
 became a foundational text in the field (a revised edition was published in 2010 as a “classical AMS” book 
6
).

During these years, Shmuel was also a very active member of the Institute of Mathematics. He developed and taught the advanced undergraduate yearlong course on “Classical Analysis,” which introduced in a rigorous way all topics from vector analysis to complex functions, ordinary differential equations and special functions. It was published as a book in Hebrew and remains very popular among students to this day. On the other hand, in 1960 he was one of the organizers of an exceptional international symposium on linear spaces at the Hebrew University (see L. Maligranda, 
arXiv:1303.0570
).

Galia Agmon sadly passed away in 1968. In 1972, Shmuel married Nechama de-Shalit, a psychiatrist who passed away in 1998.

From the mid-1960s, Agmon worked on the spectral theory and scattering theory of Schrödinger-type equations, publishing results of great significance in mathematical physics. His early breakthrough results in this area were published as a book in the highly selective Lezioni Fermiane [Fermi Lectures] series 
3
. Agmon’s contributions in this field include precise estimates on the decay of eigenfunctions of Schrödinger operators on unbounded Riemannian manifolds—expressed in terms of an ingenious metric he introduced, now known as the Agmon metric. His results on the structure and behavior of positive solutions of second-order elliptic equations, as well as the limiting absorption principle for resolvents of elliptic operators, had a profound and lasting impact on this extensively studied field. Furthermore, his work advanced the understanding of wavefunction scattering and eigenfunction expansions for both short-range and long-range potentials. Agmon spent several semesters at the University of Virginia over a span of twelve years beginning in the fall of 1980. This is where his book 
5
 was launched. His geometric approach using the Agmon metric was highly influential in the mathematical physics community.

During his distinguished career, Agmon introduced the field of PDEs to the Israeli mathematical community. He mentored a large (by Israeli scale) group of students, who carried on the PDEs tradition and extended it to related fields such as applied mathematics (in economics, fluid dynamics and more) and numerical analysis of PDEs. Among his students: Matania Ben-Artzi, Peter Constantin, Avner Friedman, Shmuel Kaniel, Yakar Kannai, Moshe Marcus, Amnon Pazy, Yehuda Pinchover, and Eli Shamir (see Figure 
3
).

Figure 3
.
Agmon with his former students and H. Brezis, at AgmonFest 2007.

Agmon was a speaker at the International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM) in 1962 and 1970. For his work, he was awarded various prizes including the Weizmann Prize for Exact Sciences (1956), the Rothschild Prize in Mathematics (1959), the Israel Prize in Exact Sciences (1991), and the EMET Prize (2007).

Throughout his long and healthy life, Shmuel Agmon bore witness to many historic moments. Respected around the globe, he was known to his colleagues for his wide-ranging intellect, remarkable memory, modest nature, and keen sense of humor. May his memory continue to inspire and be a blessing.

By
 Ehud de Shalit

Shmuel Agmon and my mother, Nechama de Shalit-Agmon, got married in 1972, when I was 17. Both were widowed—my father had passed away three years earlier, and Shmuel’s first wife, Galia, in 1968. My mother, a psychiatrist by profession and a woman dearly loved by all who knew her, was put in charge of the delicate task of building the new family. My brother, Avner, was 15, and Shmuel’s sons, Noam, Aric, and Eytan, were 22, 21, and 18 respectively. The strong bonds the five of us maintain to this day, and the intimate relationship I developed with Shmuel, were due mainly to my mother and her unsurpassable sensitivity and personal charisma.

My friendship with Shmuel was also cemented by my love for mathematics, although, perhaps subconsciously, I distanced myself from analysis and eventually chose number theory. Two years earlier, when Shmuel started dating my mother, he bought me Bell’s 
Men of Mathematics
 and Courant and John’s 
Introduction to Calculus and Analysis
. I already had the passion for math as a high school student, but it was these books which initiated me to the field. Now, at 17, a few weeks after we had moved to Shmuel’s beautiful big house in Motza Illit, a suburb of Jerusalem, I enrolled in math and physics at the Hebrew University.

Figure 4
.
Galia and Shmuel Agmon (right) with colleagues and friends in Madison, USA, 1966.

My bedroom was next to Shmuel’s study, and to get to it I had to pass through his room. I was under strict orders from my mom not to interrupt him in his work, and to keep quiet in my room while he was sitting at his heavy wooden desk, which practically meant all day long. Had I been a typical teenager, I might have found it difficult. But I was absorbed in the mysteries of math, and Shmuel’s solemn presence across the wall inspired in me a feeling of awe and admiration. It did not take long before I dared to share with him what I had learned. I remember one of our earliest conversations, in which he gently explained to me that analysis concerned problems more fundamental than the theorems in Bromwich’s 
An Introduction to the Theory of Infinite Series
, which I found in his library.

Shmuel was a man of the big world. His father, a writer and a Zionist leader, instilled in him humanistic values and an intense interest in literature and history. These did not fade even at 103. A couple of years before his death he complained to me that his (phenomenal) memory was betraying him, when he discovered he had forgotten who ruled England after Richard III. World War II found Shmuel volunteering in the Jewish Brigade (of the British army), with which he spent four years over large parts of Europe. In 1945, on a vacation from the army in Paris, he rushed to a bookstore and got a hand on Goursat’s 
Cours d’Analyse Mathématique
. In 1972, soon after he married my mom, he and I were patrolling Motza Illit together at predawn, with WWII rifles hung on our shoulders, as part of the local civil guard. Discussing math with him, to the awakening of the birds, is among my most cherished memories.

My taste was in algebra and number theory. Shmuel told me more than once he found algebra a collection of formalities, and believed analysis lay much deeper. I tried to explain to him the beauty of number theory and how algebraic geometry interacted with it. He was open-minded and willing to be persuaded. At the Hebrew University I only took one course with him, a basic course on Lie groups. He was considered an absent-minded professor, and students told jokes about his teaching, but admired his overall command and inspiration.

When Shmuel’s book 
5
 came out in 1982, I was a PhD student in Princeton. He signed the copy he had given me “With theorem-provers fraternity.” The word for “theorem-provers” in Hebrew means also “preachers.” Years later, at 99, I helped him compile his essay on Louis Nirenberg for the 
Notices
. Louis was his closest friend, and Shmuel was very proud that he used to call him “One of my three Fratelli” (the other two being Guido Stampacchia and Peter Lax). It gave me immense satisfaction to learn that in Shmuel’s dedication in 1982 he used the same word Nirenberg had used to describe their relationship.

My mother passed away in 1998. My friendship with Shmuel only intensified after her death. Always vivid, with a great sense of humor and profound modesty, if slow at times, he kept telling me mathematical stories and anecdotes whenever we met. In his last year, the Israeli Academy of Sciences asked me to interview him for a series of films it was making about its members. After having planned the interview, we finally met with the cameramen in his living room. He came prepared with 15 pages of densely packed notes, in case he forgot a name or a date, but never looked at the yellow pad of paper during the two-hour interview. I loved and admired Shmuel as a father, a mathematical beacon, and a human being. I still find it difficult to separate the three aspects.

By
 Yakar Kannai

I first encountered Professor Shmuel Agmon while attending his classes as an undergraduate. His reputation was already sufficient to instill respect. He never used notes; his lectures were very detailed, perhaps to make sure he did not commit trivial errors. He was not very accessible—a student could not meet him in the corridor and ask him for an explanation. He did not meet regularly even with the students he supervised toward their degrees. He once told me that the student must take the initiative in setting a meeting. Those meetings took place usually in his apartment or house (this may be a European tradition). My experience in those meetings was that he was encouraging, even though the beginner could not always fathom his ideas.

His attitude during oral exams was occasionally (probably unintentionally) perplexing. He posed a question and immediately suggested that the student could skip it if he/she wished to, and Agmon could pose another question. He might ask the student for his intuition on a certain problem (saying, just tell me what your opinion is, without an actual proof). I recall a student coming out of an oral exam in several subjects. Agmon asked the student what he thought of a certain question concerning behavior on the circle of convergence (of a power series). The student observed that several of the other examiners (well-known mathematicians themselves) grew pale. Later, it took me and a fellow graduate student (relaxed, under no pressure) twenty minutes to resolve Agmon’s question. I now put this puzzle to people who would like to get a degree under me—giving them a few days to come up with a solution.

Shmuel’s extraordinary mastership of complex analysis is exhibited in the following excerpt from a featured review (MR1639212; the paper was written by Shmuel in his seventies): “Professor Agmon introduces a brilliantly simple new method for turning 
$P$
 into an operator with discrete spectrum.” (The method involves function theory, YK.)

He disliked administrative tasks. So much so, that when he finished his term as chairman (at the Hebrew university the chairmanship rotated, and no faculty member was exempt from serving a term) he invited everyone (including TA’s) to a party at his apartment. Nonetheless, he was very effective as a chairman. For example, it was customary to have PhD students serve as teaching assistants, in lieu of their receiving scholarships. Upon learning at the beginning of the academic year that two new students were admitted to the graduate class, he immediately secured two new TA positions for them.

Being a perfectionist, he edited carefully even administrative notes, let alone scientific manuscripts. On the one hand this makes his papers very clear and readable. On the other hand, this is one of the reasons that his list of publications is relatively short. The major reason for this is his insistence on going public only with polished papers containing high-level research. We, his students, came to realize that there was a lot of “oral law,” including many significant “lemmas” that were never written up, but which were very useful to know and to apply.

The late sixties were difficult for him personally due to the passing away of his wife, Galia. Nevertheless, this did not prevent him from expanding his mathematical interests. During most of the fifties and the sixties, his major contributions were in the theory of elliptic linear partial differential equations. Later he turned his attention more to spectral theory (including supervising my PhD thesis). He studied (and applied ingeniously) the behavior of the resolvent not too close to the spectrum, telling me on one occasion that the behavior near or on the spectrum is too delicate, leading to hyperbolic (wavelike) phenomena, which he regarded as being more difficult. Then he turned his attention to scattering theory for Schrödinger operators, and within a few years (his widely quoted 1975 Pisa paper is fundamental) became a leader in this field. He continued to be a major actor in the field, not letting up even in advanced age (I mentioned earlier a sample achievement).

The great majority of his PhD students (11 out of 13, according to Math Genealogy) became independent research mathematicians. This may be due to a selection bias: perhaps essentially only those who dared to study under a person of such high standards went on to pursue a degree with him. Be that as it may, his impact and legacy will persist in the mathematical community for a long time to come.

[email protected]

By
 Peter Constantin

Shmuel Agmon was my PhD thesis advisor. When I arrived in Israel and went to interview for graduate studies at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, I knew I wanted to work with him. I requested to meet him and asked him whether it was possible for me to work under his guidance. “There are easier things in life,” he informed me. (I use now this line with my prospective graduate students.) I think I became his student right then and there.

I remember wandering in microlocal wilderness for a while. I had some background in the area, and gave a presentation in Agmon’s PDEs seminar about composition of wavefront sets, so Agmon suggested I work on wavefront sets of pseudo-differential operators of principal type. I had not made much progress, when Hörmander solved the problem.

Now Agmon gave me a problem in scattering theory for Schrödinger operators. It was known that scattering wave operators exist and are complete for Schrödinger operators with short-range potentials, in the whole space or in the exterior of a ball. The problem was to determine if scattering continues to be possible in domains where the obstacle has noncompact boundary, extending to infinity. This was a new area for me. Agmon suggested reading materials and the geometric condition under which scattering should work. We did not meet very often. Our relation was strictly professional, serious, no banter, no familiarity, a little distant. So I was shocked one evening when he called me on the phone at home. It was because he found a mistake in something I had said to him earlier. It was an idea for a localization which would have solved many problems in scattering. It was too good to be true. The progress came suddenly later, and the problem was solved showing that his geometric intuition was correct.

Agmon once told me (probably in response to something I must have said) that sometimes an epsilon makes the difference between a great result and a mediocre one. There is of course some truth to this, but more important is the push toward excellence it implies. Agmon’s mathematics is deep, with geometric beauty, technical power, clarity and rigor. It is imperative in these days to strive to uphold his high standards of intellectual truth, quality and integrity.

By
 Percy Deift

Starting in the 1960s, Shmuel Agmon was a rather frequent visitor to the Courant Institute, and I met him informally over lunch on a number of occasions in the Courant lounge. I was familiar with his seminal work together with Douglis and Nirenberg 
7
8
, but our discussions were nontechnical.

In the late 1970’s, together with Hunziker, Simon, and Vock, I began working on the problem of the exponential decay of eigenfunctions of 
-
$N$
body Schrödinger operators. We obtained a number of results, but they were of an ad hoc nature and far from optimal. Then in 1982 Agmon published his remarkable book 
5
. The book gave precise optimal decay rates for eigenfunctions of such operators in terms of the solution of a natural, associated variational problem. In one stroke, Agmon’s result superseded all work in the field.

Figure 5
.
Left to right: Shmuel Agmon, Hans Weinberger, and Louis Nirenberg. Minneapolis, around 1980.

In 1986 I was invited to write a review of Agmon’s book. I read the book in great detail, going through all the proofs. It turned out, however, that there was a gap in one of the proofs. I told Shmuel about the gap, and he quickly filled it. Now the amusing thing was that he never forgot about the gap. Whenever we met he would say, with a twinkle in his eye, “Do you remember the gap you found…?” I think that this was Shmuel’s way of teasing me.

My wife, Rebecca, and I visited Israel often in the ensuing years, and we always tried to meet up with Shmuel and his wife, who were charming and gracious hosts. On our last visit to Jerusalem in 2023, I called up Shmuel and said that Rebecca and I would like to visit him. I was astounded that at the age of 101 he immediately recognized my voice on the phone and invited us over for coffee and cake. After the “gaps” in our conversation were filled, he reminisced about his life. He expressed great remorse that he had not fought in the War of Independence in 1948. He had fought for four years during the Second World War in the Jewish Brigade of the British Army and after the war, impatient to return to mathematics, he chose in 1945 to pursue a PhD in Paris.

Now my dear friend Shmuel is gone. I miss his mathematics, his charm—and even his teasing!

By
 Didier Robert

The first time I met the name Shmuel Agmon was during my PhD thesis in 1970. My advisor asked me to read his beautiful book 
2
6
. His book was for me a constant source of inspiration during all my mathematical life, in particular the last chapters about the spectral theory and eigenvalues estimates.

Ten years later, in the eighties, I met Shmuel at the Poincaré Institute in Paris and we had there our first discussion. After that I met him many times, in France (Saint-Jean-de-Monts PDE conference, L. Schwartz seminar at Ecole Polytechnique) and also in Minneapolis (IMA), Vienna (Schrödinger Institute), and Jerusalem (for his 70th birthday, in 1992). It was always a great pleasure to discuss with him not only mathematics but also many other subjects of life such as our families or social problems in our countries. In particular, I remember some discussions walking along the beach of Saint-Jean-de-Monts on the Atlantic coast.

Our PDE group in Nantes was deeply influenced by him through his contributions in spectral theory. So it was very natural to recommend him for a doctorat honoris causa from the university of Nantes. It was a great honor for us that Shmuel agreed to accept the degree, and a ceremony was organized for June 5, 1990, in the Theater of Nantes. I remember that Professor Brezis and Nirenberg attended.

Shmuel was not only a great mathematician, but was also a very kind and humane person.

By
 Barry Simon

I first became aware of Agmon around events that began in my last year of graduate study in 1970. He was already well established as an expert in the study of general elliptic PDEs, but this work was on the subject of the special case of Schrödinger operators, PDEs of the form 
,
$-\Delta +V$
 an area that he had just moved into and which he focused on for the rest of his career.

I was taking probably the only intermediate quantum mechanics theory class then in the US which discussed positive energy bound states and mentioned Kato’s result (taught by my advisor, Arthur Wightman). Kato had proven no such states occur in the one-body case if the potential, 
,
$V$
 is of either of two types, and it was natural to ask if one could extend that to sums of the two types. I did this and submitted it to the Courant journal, 
CPAM
, where Kato’s paper had appeared. Little did I know that this journal only considered papers that they solicited, although they made an exception in my case, perhaps because they’d heard positive things from Wightman. It was accepted, my first paper in a significant math journal.

About a year later Kato spoke at Princeton. I introduced myself and mentioned my paper motivated by his. He told me I should be aware that a mathematician named Shmuel Agmon had independently proven the same result and announced it in a conference proceedings. I sent a letter to Agmon (this was pre-email!), he wrote a nice letter back and made a point when he published his paper of carefully mentioning my work, so my first exposure to Shmuel showed his generous nature, notably to a fresh postdoc.

I think this was Shmuel’s first work on Schrödinger operators, marking a transition in his research from more general studies of elliptic PDEs. Afterward, he made two major contributions: weighted 
,
$L^2$
 and the understanding of the rate of exponential decay (Agmon distance). The first 
3

$V$
 which obey the inequality (for positive constants 
$C$
 and

$$\begin{equation*} \bigl | V(x) \bigr | \le C \bigl ( 1 + |x| \bigr )^{-\alpha }. \end{equation*}$$

It was known for many years that if 
,
$\alpha >1$
 there is a scattering theory, and Kato’s result above shows there are no positive energy bound states. In the 1960’s, there were extensive efforts to prove that in this regime, there is no singular continuous spectrum, thereby completing the most basic spectral analysis for these operators (proving that in this regime scattering was complete was also worked on and solved by Kato-Kuroda). Agmon informally announced that he had solved the no singular continuous spectrum problem and was invited to give a series of lectures on this work at a conference on spectral theory of Schrödinger operators at Oberwolfach in the summer of 1971.

In characteristic fashion, he started his series by saying (I can’t recall the exact words 50+ years later but this is the basic thrust and approximately correct; where I say

$A,B,C,\dots$

The second major area

 concerns the rate of exponential decay of discrete bound states. I especially appreciated this work since it followed a series of papers that I wrote on the subject. This decay is simple in the one-body case with decaying potential, but is much more complicated for cases where the potential goes to infinity at spatial infinity in a nonisotropic way and for 
-
$N$

$N$

Agmon’s discovery was brilliant, going in a direction that no one else had gone. He introduced what I named the Agmon metric,

Shmuel had a pointed sense of humor that often showed up in his remarks. We saw this in the introduction to his singular continuous talk that was mentioned earlier. In the same series, after its use by Kato and Kuroda, he started calling 
${\langle x \rangle }$

At one conference, he saw me with some of my former students and postdocs: I recall Yosi Avron, George Hagedorn, Evans Harrel, and Ira Herbst (or some subset) and he shouted out “Barry’s boys.” Twenty years later, these mentees still referred to themselves as “Barry’s boys” with grins on their faces. Shmuel was a deep mathematician who was a pleasure to interact with.

$V(x)g_0$
 where 
$g_0$
 is the standard metric on 
.
$\mathbb{R}^n$
 The core of the proof is an energy identity, and its astonishing simplicity surely explains why Agmon’s approach became so successful.

If these decay estimates at infinity play an important role, it is primarily in the semiclassical context that they appear as essential, and the number of papers using these estimates is in hundreds. Hence we only mention below the initial contributions.

Semiclassical analysis in its mathematical version consists in introducing a parameter 
$h > 0$
 (replacing the Planck constant) and analyzing the spectral properties of 
$P(h) \coloneq -h^2\Delta + V$
 (typically the lowest eigenvalues) as 
.
$h \to 0$

The Agmon estimates for the first eigenfunctions in this context appeared first in an announcement by B. Simon 
12
 circulating since 1982, followed one year later by the detailed version of this announcement in 
13
14
 and the preprint by Helffer-Sjöstrand 
10
. B. Simon was the first to realize that it is relatively easy to extend Agmon’s estimates to this case and to apply them to the classical double-well problem, which was only analyzed before in dimension one. Looking at the two lowest eigenvalues 
$\lambda _1(h)$
 and 
$\lambda _2(h)$
 of 
$P(h)$
 and assuming that 
$V$
 has a symmetry with respect to a hyperplane and two nondegenerate minima, it is obtained in 
14
 (for the logarithm of the splitting) and in the form below in 
10
 that, as 
,
$h\to 0$

$$\begin{equation*} \lambda _2-\lambda _1 \sim a(h)h^{1/2} \exp (-S/h), \end{equation*}$$

where 
$S$
 is the Agmon distance associated with the (degenerate) metric 
$(V- \min V)g_0$
 between the two minima and 
$a(h)$
 has a complete expansion in powers of 
$h$
 with 
.
$a(0) > 0$
 One of the key ingredients introduced in 
10
 for obtaining the complete expansion is the comparison, in the case of a potential with a nondegenerate minimum, between the ground state and its WKB approximation modulo 
$O(h^\infty ) \exp (-d(x)/h)$
 where 
$d(x)$
 is the Agmon distance to the minimum.

Over the years, these estimates appear crucially in many problems in mathematical physics: periodic case, degenerate well case, analysis of the Witten Laplacian, infinite number of wells, magnetic semiclassical estimates, decay estimates in superconductivity. Most of the authors of these papers use the name 
Agmon estimate
 for these types of results, although the estimates used are sometimes quite far from the initial context considered by Agmon and not necessarily optimal, as they are for the original problem.
