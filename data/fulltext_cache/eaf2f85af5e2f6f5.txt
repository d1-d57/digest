If one naively tries this approach by forcing all the primes

From an algebraic number theory perspective, this is related to the breakdown of unique factorization in such number fields, as measured by the class group. The size of this group is in turn controlled by the discriminant of the field, as per the 
fundamental theorem of Minkowski

But one can hope that the arguments are robust enough to tolerate a little bit of breakdown in unique factorization, so long as the class group is not too large. The three constructions thus sit on a continuum, with the key differences being the selection of the key parameters

The Mythos construction is a compromise, in which the degree and the number of primes both go to infinity in a coupled fashion. 8 July, 2026 at 12:28 pm

Will Sawin

Interesting! For an arbitrary norm, one can always get a constant times n unit distances by putting the points on a line with each one a unit distance from the previous one.

For the usual norm on the plane, the horizontal amplification beats this by an amount less than any power of n, and this is the classical construction, and the vertical amplification beats this by a small power of n. In higher dimensions, for the usual norm, the vertical amplification beats the trivial bound by a small power of n (for example just by restricting to the plane) but now the horizontal amplification beats the trivial bound by a larger power of n since there are many ways of writing an integer as a sum of squares (basically by pigeonhole), so there’s no clear way to improve on the classical construction.

For a generic norm, one can’t beat the trivial bound by any power of n.

However, you observe that one can find norms with enough algebraic structure that vertical amplification beats the trivial bound by a small power of n, but not so much algebraic structure that horizontal amplification beats the trivial bound by a larger power of n. It might be interesting to understand the set of such norms.

7 July, 2026 at 11:29 am

Will Sawin

The relation to prior work on codes and lattices was pointed out in the paper 
Remarks on the disproof of the unit distance conjecture
. It has been somewhat helpful in followup work – the work of Tsfasman on the unit group lattice was one of the inspirations for arguments in Bloom-Sawin-Schildkraut-Zhelezov, and work on codes arising from curves over finite fields was relevant too. 

Indeed, one way to state the insight of the AI is that some of the constructions that work for these packing and coding problems also work for the seemingly different unit distance problem and for others. The sum-product problem might provide the most intuitive explanation of why that is: The crucial thing there is that both the additive group and the multiplicative group of the same number field give reasonably dense packings, which is used to get a set exhibiting both additive and multiplicative structure.

Sets exhibiting additive and multplicative structure can give good unit distance sets basically because two complex numbers have distance 1 if their difference has norm 1 and difference is, up to sign, the same as addition, while norm 1 complex numbers form a multiplicative subgroup.

Certainly now I think people are thinking about the intersection of these topics.
