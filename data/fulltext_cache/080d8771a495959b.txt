   This is a survey article about the proof of the Kakeya conjecture in three dimensions.
The Kakeya conjecture is a problem about the intersection patterns of thin tubes in
Euclidean space.
   A Kakeya set in Rn is a set that contains a unit line segment in every direction.
Around 1920, Besicovitch gave an example of a Kakeya set in R2 with arbitrarily small
Lebesgue measure. Around 1970, Fefferman gave a counterexample to a well-known
problem about Fourier multipliers which crucially used Besicovitch’s example. The
same idea shows that a number of open problems in Fourier analysis are connected
to Kakeya sets. These problems in Fourier analysis connect to quantitative questions
about Kakeya sets, such as, “What is the infimal Hausdorff dimension of a Kakeya set
in Rn ?” For example, the Stein restriction conjecture in Fourier analysis implies that
                                        1251–02


every Kakeya set in Rn has Hausdorff dimension n. The connection between Fourier
analysis and Kakeya problems is described in the survey article Tao (2001).
   The Kakeya conjecture for Hausdorff dimension says that every Kakeya set in Rn has
Hausdorff dimension n. In the 1980s, Davies proved that every Kakeya set in R2 has
Hausdorff dimension 2, and the proof is only a couple pages. But proving the conjecture
for any n ≥ 3 is much more difficult. Wang and Zahl (2025) proved the Kakeya
conjecture in dimension 3. Their proof builds on important ideas by many people,
including Bourgain, Wolff, Katz, Łaba, Tao, Orponen, and Shmerkin. In dimension
n ≥ 4, the conjecture is currently open.
  In this survey, we will not use the language of Hausdorff dimension. For understanding
the proof, and also for applications in Fourier analysis, the most useful language is in
terms of sets of thin tubes. We set up this language and state the main results in the
next section.




1. Statement of main results

 Suppose that T is a set of δ-tubes in Rn with length 1. We write U (T) for ∪T ∈T T .

Conjecture 1.1. — For every ϵ > 0, there is a constant c(n, ϵ) so that if T is a set

1.1. Notation
  Informally, we write A ≈ B to mean that A and B are approximately the same size.
We write A ⪅ B to mean that either A < B or A ≈ B. And we write A ≪ B to mean

2. The hero: multiscale analysis

  The hero of our story is looking at the problem at many different scales. In this
section, we explain what this means and give a hint about why it will be important.
  Define β to be the infimal exponent so that for every set T of δ-tubes in R3 with
∆max (T) ⪅ 1,


(1)                                    µ(T) ⪅ |T|β .
   The Kakeya theorem, Theorem 1.2, says that β = 0. We say that T is a worst-case
Kakeya set if ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and µ(T) ≈ |T|β . The proof will be by contradiction. We
suppose β > 0. We let T be a worst-case Kakeya set. We will prove that T would
have to have a lot of geometric and algebraic structure. Using this structure we will
eventually get a contradiction.
   To exploit the fact that T is a worst-case Kakeya set, we will compare T with other
sets of tubes T′ . We will find other sets of tubes T′ which are related to T and obey
∆max (T′ ) ⪅ 1. By the definition of β in (1), we know that µ(T′ ) ⪅ |T′ |β . Since T′ is
related to T, this bound leads to information about T. We will find these sets of tubes
T′ by looking at the original set of tubes at multiple scales.
   Suppose that T is a set of δ-tubes. Given a scale ρ ∈ [δ, 1], we let Tρ denote the set of
ρ-tubes formed by thickening the δ-tubes of T. When we thicken two distinct δ-tubes,
we may get nearly identical ρ-tubes. When this happens we identify the ρ-tubes. So we
typically have |Tρ | ≪ |T|.

3. A key obstacle: the Heisenberg group

   Next we introduce one of the key obstacles to proving the main theorem. There are
cousin problems that sound quite similar to the Kakeya conjecture but behave differently.
For example, the natural analogue of Theorem 1.2 in C3 is false. The counterexample
is called the Heisenberg group example. It was first published by Katz, Łaba, and Tao
(2000). The idea that problems of this type may behave differently over different fields
was first noted by Tom Wolff (1999).
   We define a metric on Cn by identifying it with R2n . We define a complex tube in
Cn with radius r and length L by taking a complex line in Cn , intersecting it with a
ball of radius L, and then taking its r-neighborhood. We define a complex δ-tube to be
a tube of radius δ and length 1. The quantities ∆(T, K) and ∆max (T) can be defined
roughly as above.
   In C3 , there is a set of complex δ-tubes T with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and µ(T) ≈ |T|1/4 . This
example shows that the complex analogue of Theorem 1.2 is false. This example is called
                                           1251–06


the Heisenberg group example. It is based on a quadratic real algebraic hypersurface in
C3 . There are a couple choices for this hypersurface. One is the surface H defined by
                       H = {(z1 , z2 , z3 : |z1 |2 + |z2 |2 − |z3 |2 = 1}

This hypersurface contains many complex lines. For example, if α is a unit complex
number, then the line defined by z1 = 1, z3 = αz2 lies in H. All these lines pass through
the point (1, 0, 0) ∈ H. The surface H is very symmetric: it is symmetric under the
action of the group U (2, 1), which acts transitively on H. By symmetry, there are
infinitely many lines through every point of H. Taking δ-neighorhoods of these lines
gives a set of δ-tubes T where ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and yet µ(T) ≈ δ −1 ≈ |T|1/4 .
   Wolff (1995) proved that if T is a set of δ-tubes in R3 with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, then
µ(T) ⪅ |T|1/4 . It has been very difficult to improve on the exponent 1/4. Katz, Łaba,
and Tao (2000) improved 1/4 to 1/4 − ϵ for some tiny ϵ > 0, under an additional
technical assumption. This technical assumption was removed by Katz and Zahl (2019).
The exponent 1/4 − ϵ was the best known exponent before the recent work of Wang
and Zahl, giving the sharp exponent.
   Understanding the structure of the Heisenberg group example is essential to the
proof of the Kakeya conjecture. Roughly speaking, the proof shows that if the Kakeya
conjecture was false, a worst-case Kakeya set would need to have structural properties
similar to those of the Heisenberg group. Finally, these strong structural properties lead
to a contradiction. In the next section, we discuss these key structures.

4.2. Complex conjugation structure
   The definition of H involves complex conjugation, and the structure of H is closely
related to complex conjugation. One connection between complex conjugation and the
geometry of H comes from considering the slopes of grains along a line.
   Fix a line ℓ ⊂ H. We choose coordinates so that the line ℓ is the z1 axis. Each point
z ∈ ℓ lies in a grain, which is a complex 2-plane containing ℓ. Such a 2-plane is given by
an equation z3 = sz2 , where s ∈ C. Suppose the grain of H through z = (z1 , 0, 0) ∈ ℓ
is given by z3 = s(z1 )z2 . So s(z1 ) describes the slope of the grain through (z1 , 0, 0) ∈ ℓ.

(4)                                      s(z1 ) = z̄1

4.3. Stickiness
   If T is the Heisenberg group example at scale δ, then the thicker tubes Tρ are the
Heisenberg example at scale ρ. This leads to some nice numerology about |Tρ |. Let
|TρC | denote the volume of a complex ρ-tube in C3 . Then for the set T of complex tubes
from the Heisenberg group, we have |T| ∼ |TδC |−1 and for each ρ ∈ [δ, 1], |Tρ | ∼ |TρC |−1 .
This numerology is called the ‘sticky’ case, for reasons that we explain below.

  Stickiness. If T is a set of δ-tubes in Rn , then T is sticky if |Tρ | ≈ |Tρ |−1 for each
ρ ∈ [δ, 1].
  In Rn , a ρ-tube has volume |Tρ | ∼ ρn−1 , and so T is sticky if


(6)                           |Tρ | ≈ ρ−(n−1) for all ρ ∈ [δ, 1]

  Recall from (3) that |T| ≈ |Tρ ||T[Tρ ]|, and so if T is sticky, then


(7)                   |T[Tρ ]| ≈ (δ/ρ)−(n−1) for all ρ ∈ [δ, 1], Tρ ∈ Tρ

   For comparison, if T obeys ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, then we have |T[Tρ ]| ⪅ (δ/ρ)−(n−1) . So a
set of tubes T with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 is sticky if |T[Tρ ]| is as large as possible. The name
sticky comes from the following image. If two tubes T1 , T2 lie in the same fatter tube
Tρ , then they are “stuck together”. The tubes in a sticky Kakeya set stick together as
much as possible, given the condition ∆max (T) ⪅ 1.
   To summarize, the Heisenberg group example has three important structures: sticki-
ness, grain structure, and complex conjugation structure.

4.4. Outline of the proof
  Now we can outline the proof of the main theorem. Recall that β is the infimal
exponent so that for every set T of δ-tubes in R3 with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1,


(8)                                     µ(T) ⪅ |T|β .
Our goal is to prove that β = 0. We say that T is a worst-case Kakeya set if ∆max (T) ⪅ 1
and µ(T) ≈ |T|β . The proof is by contradiction. We suppose β > 0. We let T be a
worst-case Kakeya set.
      – Step 1. Since T is worst-case it must be sticky.
      – Step 2. Then T must have grain structure.
      – Step 3. Then T must have something like complex conjugation structure.
      – Step 4. No such structure exists. (There is no operation on R which has properties
        similar to complex conjugation.)
  This is the logical order of the proof but it is not the chronological order. In particular,
Step 1 was the last step to be understood.
  We will explain the proof in chronological order. Here is an outline.
  In the first big part of the paper, we describe the proof of the sticky case of the main
theorem. This proof was outlined by Katz and Tao in unpublished work in the 2000s,
and shared in a blog post (Tao, 2014). It has several steps.
      – In Section 6, we show that stickiness leads to grain structure. (This step is due to
        Katz, Łaba, and Tao, 2000.)
      – In Section 7, we show that grain structure leads to complex conjugation structure.
        (This step is based on unpublished work of Katz–Tao. The details were carried out
        in Wang and Zahl, 2026).
      – In Section 8, we show that complex conjugation structure leads to a contradic-
        tion. (This step is based on work of many people, including Bourgain, Katz, Tao,
        Orponen, Shmerkin, Wang, Zahl)
   After we describe the proof of the sticky case we pause to digest and reflect.
   After that, in Sections 12 and 13 and 14, we describe the proof that the worst-case is
sticky. (This step is based on work of Wang and Zahl, 2025).



5. Avoiding technicalities

   To avoid technical details, we will assume that various quantities are uniform.
   For instance, for each Tρ ∈ Tρ , we will study the set of tubes T[Tρ ]. In general, for
different tubes Tρ ∈ Tρ , |T[Tρ ]| could be very different. But we will assume that all the
sets T[Tρ ] have roughly the same cardinality. Similarly we will assume that µ(T[Tρ ]) is
roughly the same for all Tρ ∈ Tρ .

  For each point x ∈ U (T), we let Tx := {T ∈ T : x ∈ T }. In general, for different

   In this survey, we sketch the proof of the Kakeya theorem assuming some uniformity
of this kind. The full proof has the same main ideas but there are extra technical details
to deal with non-uniformity. For instance, if |Tx | is very different at different x ∈ U (T),
then we subdivide U (T) into subsets where |Tx | has different sizes. Then we have to
keep track of all these subsets. This process is called pigeonholing. There is non-trivial
technical work involved, but we will not discuss it in this survey of the high level ideas.
   When we state lemmas in this survey, the precise statements require some pigeonholing
and/or uniformity hypotheses. To keep the statements simple, we leave out these details.
Unfortunately, it means that the statements of the lemmas here are not completely
precise. But this choice does keep the statements of lemmas simpler. We hope that it
conveys the general strategy to a broader audience.



6. Stickiness leads to grain structure

  In this section, we will consider a worst-case sticky Kakeya set and show that it must
have grain structure.
  We say that T is a sticky Kakeya set of δ-tubes in R3 if:
      – ∆max (T) ⪅ 1.
      – For each ρ ∈ [δ, 1], |Tρ | ≈ ρ−2 and for each Tρ ∈ Tρ , |T[Tρ ]| ≈ (ρ/δ)2 .
  We define βsticky to be the infimal exponent β so that for every sticky Kakeya set of

Theorem 6.1 (Sticky Kakeya theorem, Wang and Zahl, 2026)
  βsticky = 0. In other words, for every sticky Kakeya set T, µ(T) ⪅ 1.

  The sticky Kakeya theorem is the first big part of the proof of the Kakeya theorem.
  We say that T is a worst-case sticky Kakeya set if T is a sticky Kakeya set and

suppose that βsticky > 0 and we let T be a worst-case sticky Kakeya set. By examining

8. Complex conjugation structure leads to a contradiction

   Equation (17) captures some of the structure of complex conjugation and also the
way that the real numbers fit in the complex numbers. This is related to several other
special features of the way that R fits in C. The most fundamental feature is that R
is closed under both addition and multiplication. Starting from Lemma 7.1, the proof
of Kakeya shows that there must be a set A ⊂ R which is approximately closed under
both addition and multiplication and also obeys spacing estimates as in (16). This area
is called sum-product theory, and we give a short introduction to it here.
   Suppose that A ⊂ R. We write A + A for the sumset
                               A + A = {a1 + a2 |a1 , a2 ∈ A}
and we write A · A for the product set
                                 A · A = {a1 a2 |a1 , a2 ∈ A}.
If A ⊂ R is a finite set, it is interesting to consider |A+A| and |A·A|. If A = {1, . . . , N },
then |A| = N and |A+A| ∼ N , but |A·A| ≈ N 2 . Similarly, if A is a geometric progression
such as {2n/N }N                                                        2
                n=1 , then |A| = N and |A · A| ∼ N , but |A + A| ≈ N . Erdős conjectured
                                                             2
that for any finite set A ⊂ R, |A + A| + |A · A| ⪆ |A| . This conjecture is still open,
but we do have some weaker bounds. Erdős and Szemerédi (1983) proved that there is
an exponent α > 0 so that |A + A| + |A · A| ⪆ |A|1+α . The best known value of α was
improved many times, and it is currently a little more than 1/3.
   These results are not exactly what is needed in Kakeya type problems. The set A that
appears in our story is not a finite set of points but a finite set of δ-intervals. Instead of
measuring the sizes of finite sets by cardinality, we need to measure the sizes of sets by
δ-covering numbers: if A ⊂ R, we write |A|δ for the minimum number of δ-balls needed
to cover A. Switching our point of view to δ-covering numbers, it is natural to ask: if
A ⊂ R, then is it true that |A + A|δ + |A · A|δ ⪆ |A|1+α   δ   for some α > 0? The answer
to this question is no. For instance, this inequality fails if A = {1 + jδ}Jj=1 whenever
1 ≪ J ≤ δ −1 . In order to get a non-trivial sum-product inequality, we need to add an
extra assumption saying that A is not packed too tightly into an interval. The first
such theorem was proven by Bourgain (2003), and there is also a closely related result
by Edgar and Miller (2003).

Theorem 8.1 (Bourgain “discretized” sum-product theorem)
  Suppose that 0 < s < 1, and A ⊂ [0, 1] with
   – |A|δ ≈ δ −s .
   – If B(x, r) ⊂ [0, 1], then |A ∩ B(x, r)|δ ⪅ (r/δ)s
  Then |A + A|δ + |A · A|δ ⪆ δ −s−ϵ , where ϵ = ϵ(s) > 0.

   With this background, we can return to discussing the proof of the sticky Kakeya
theorem. Recall from (16) that the set A obeys the hypotheses above with s = 1−2βsticky .
We know by earlier arguments that βsticky ≤ 1/4 and so if βsticky > 0, then 0 < s < 1.
Roughly speaking, we might hope that the complex conjugation structure forces A + A
and A · A to be small, which would give a contradiction. I think this is morally correct
but the actual proof is somewhat more complicated.
   First of all there are some trivial ways that s(x) and s̃(y) can satisfy (17). We could
have s(x) = s̃(y) = 0. Or we could have s(x) = x and s̃(y) = −y. The proof in Wang
and Zahl (2026) first rules out these possibilities. For instance, if s(x) = 0, then it
would mean that all the tubes crossing T1 must lie in a common planar slab, and this
would eventually force too many tubes into a planar slab, violating the hypothesis that
∆max (T) ⪅ 1.
   Starting from the complex conjugation structure, the proof eliminates these trivial
possibilities and then shows that some new set related to A is approximately closed
under both addition and multiplication, which contradicts Theorem 8.1. The argument
has several steps, and we are not able to sketch all the steps in this survey. The ideas
were developed by Katz, Tao, Orponen, Shmerkin, Wang, and Zahl. The ideas are
related to major recent progress in the field of projection theory.
   At the beginning of our discussion, we mentioned that the Kakeya theorem is hard
to prove because the Heisenberg group example shows that the analogous statement
over C is false. The Heisenberg group example is sticky, and so the complex analogue
of the sticky Kakeya theorem is also false. Any proof of the Kakeya theorem or sticky
Kakeya theorem must include a step that distinguishes R from C. The sum-product
theorem, Theorem 8.1, is the crucial step that distinguishes R from C.
   Let us quickly note that the analogue of Theorem 8.1 over C is false. The counterex-
ample comes because R is a subring of C. More precisely, we let A = R ∩ B(0, 1) ⊂ C.
Then |A|δ ∼ |A + A|δ ∼ |A · A|δ , and these are all much smaller than |B(0, 1)|δ .

9. Leveraging the sum-product theorem at many scales

   Let us pause to digest a surprising feature of the proof of the sticky Kakeya theorem.
A key obstacle in proving sticky Kakeya is that the analogue over C is false. The
sum-product theorem (Theorem 8.1) distinguishes R from C. However, the bounds in
Theorem 8.1 are not sharp, and indeed they are very weak. How can a non-sharp and
very weak theorem be used as a key step in the proof of a sharp theorem?
   Over the last several years, there have been a number of sharp results proven using
the non-sharp Theorem 8.1. This body of work has had a major influence in the field
of projection theory. Some of the main contributors are Orponen, Shmerkin, Ren, and
Wang. Roughly speaking, it is possible to prove strong and sharp estimates by applying
Theorem 8.1 many times at different scales.
   The proof we have reviewed can be written in different ways. Let us describe a way
to rephrase the proof to highlight the way that we are exploiting Theorem 8.1 many
times at different scales.
   We define βsticky (δ) to be the least exponent so that for every sticky Kakeya set of
δ-tubes, µ(T) ≤ |T|βsticky (δ) . In this language, the sticky Kakeya theorem says that
limδ→0 βsticky (δ) = 0. We can organize our proof using a key lemma which says that if
δ2 is far smaller than δ1 , then βsticky (δ2 ) is smaller than βsticky (δ1 ) by a definite amount.
Here is the precise statement of the lemma.
Lemma 9.1. — For any β > 0, there are ϵ > 0 and K > 0 so that if δ1 < 1/10
and βsticky (δ1 ) ≥ β and δ2 ≤ δ1K , then βsticky (δ2 ) < βsticky (δ1 ) − ϵ. Also ϵ = ϵ(β) and
K = K(β) are continuous in β.
Iterating this key lemma at many different scales gives the sticky Kakeya theorem.
   The proof sketched in the sections above can be slightly adapted to give a proof of
the key lemma. The proof of the key lemma crucially uses the sum-product theorem,
Theorem 8.1. The exponent in Theorem 8.1 is not sharp, and is only a tiny improvement
on a trivial bound. Partly for this reason, the exponent ϵ = ϵ(β) in the key lemma is not
sharp and is only a tiny improvement on a trivial bound. But applying the key lemma
many times at different scales, we get essentially sharp bounds. In this process, we are
leveraging the sum-product theorem by applying it many times at different scales and
getting a small gain each time.
   This finishes our sketch of the proof of the sticky Kakeya theorem.


10. Sticky vs. not sticky

   The proof of the sticky Kakeya theorem shows a remarkable connection between the
sticky case of the Kakeya problem and mathematical structures like the Heisenberg
group, subrings of R, and sum-product inequalities. It is certainly interesting math-
ematics. But it was not so clear how much progress these results make towards the
                                         1251–24


general Kakeya conjecture. Is the sticky case a crucial case? Or is it just a rare special
case?
   Should we expect the “worst” Kakeya set to be sticky? Analysts have considered
many cousins of the Kakeya problem. For many years, the worst known example for
every cousin problem was sticky. Bourgain (1991) considered a variation of the Kakeya
problem for curved tubes in R3 . In this curved version, the smallest possible volume of
|U (T)| is ∼ δ, and the worst-case example is sticky. Katz, Łaba, and Tao (2000) gave
the Heisenberg group example, which showed that the analogue of the Kakeya problem
with convex Wolff axioms is false in C3 . The Heisenberg group example is sticky. It
seemed plausible that for a broad class of problems of this type, the worst-case is sticky.
   Katz and Tao and others wondered whether the general Kakeya problem could be
reduced to the sticky case, but they didn’t see any way to do it. Orponen (2017) proved
the sticky case of the Falconer conjecture, another longstanding problem in geometric
measure theory which is a kind of cousin of the Kakeya problem. This remarkable
proof had a lot of influence in the field, but no one has managed to reduce the general
Falconer conjecture to the sticky case.
   Then Katz and Zahl (2019) found a new cousin of the Kakeya problem, and gave
evidence that the worse case example is not sticky. They considered the Wolff axioms
version of the Kakeya problem over a different ring – they replaced R by the ring
A = Fp [x]/(x2 ). The ring A has a natural notion of distance with two distinct length
scales. There is a cousin of the Heisenberg group in A3 and it leads to a counterexample
to the analogue of Theorem 1.2. But unlike in C3 , the Heisenberg group cousin in A3 is
not sticky. It appears likely that in A3 , the sticky case of the analogue of Theorem 1.2
is true, but the general case is false.
   As of a couple of years ago, I was quite pessimistic about reducing the general case
of Kakeya to the sticky case.
   The first indication that the sticky case may play a crucial role in problems of this
type was the solution of the Furstenberg set conjecture by Orponen–Shmerkin and Ren–
Wang in 2024. The Furstenberg set conjecture is a central question in the field called
projection theory, which studies the orthogonal projections of sets and measures in Rd .
In the late 90s, Tom Wolff identified the Kakeya problem, the Falconer problem, and
the Furstenberg set problem as cousin problems involving related issues. In particular,
all three conjectures have versions over C which are false, with counterexamples related
to the subfield R ⊂ C. Orponen and Shmerkin (2023b) proved the sticky case of the
Furstenberg set conjecture. Shortly afterward, Ren and Wang (2023) proved the full
Furstenberg conjecture.
   The proof of the Furstenberg conjecture is a major development in the field, and I
think it deserves its own survey article to describe. Some of the key multiscale ideas
in the proof of the Kakeya conjecture grew out of this work, developing over multiple
papers, including Bourgain (2003, 2010), Demeter and Wang (2025), Orponen (2022),
Orponen and Shmerkin (2023a,b), Orponen, Shmerkin, and Wang (2024), Ren and
                                                 1251–25


Wang (2023), Shmerkin and Wang (2025), and Wang and Wu (2024). The proof of the
sticky case in Orponen and Shmerkin (2023b) leverages the sum-product theorem 8.1
at many different scales, just like the proof of sticky Kakeya that we saw here. It also
has its own unique issues and features. The proof of the general case of the Furstenberg
conjecture reduces the problem to two cases: the sticky case and a case which is far from
sticky, which they call the semi-well-spaced case. They resolve the semi-well-spaced case
using Fourier analytic methods building on Guth, Solomon, and Wang (2019). And they
give a short and elegant multiscale argument which reduces the general Furstenberg
conjecture to these two cases.
   It was quite surprising to me that the general Furstenberg problem reduces to these
two cases. This work gave a hint that the sticky case might be a key case in other
problems as well. Then Wang and Zahl (2025) reduced the general case of the Kakeya
problem to the sticky case. The second main part of our survey describes this reduction.

12. The worst case is sticky

  We now begin the second big part of the proof of Kakeya: showing that a worst-case
Kakeya set is sticky. This part of the proof was done in Wang and Zahl (2025). Here we
will follow the exposition in Guth, Wang, and Zahl (2026), which slightly streamlines
the original proof. We describe the proof in Sections 12, 13, and 14.
  Recall that β is the infimal number so that, whenever ∆max (T) ⪅ 1, we have


(19)                                     µ(T) ⪅ |T|β

  Recall that T is a worst-case Kakeya set if ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and µ(T) ≈ |T|β . The
hypothesis that ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 implies that |T| ⪅ δ −2 . For these notes, we focus on the
case that |T| ≈ δ −2 , which shows the main ideas and leaves out some technical issues.
  Our goal is to prove that a worst-case Kakeya set must be sticky. Assuming that
β > 0 and that T is not sticky, we will prove that


(20)                                     µ(T) ≪ |T|β

(Recall we use the notation µ(T) ≪ |T|β to mean that µ(T) is much less than |T|β ).
This inequality contradicts our assumption that µ(T) ≈ |T|β , and so we conclude that
T must be sticky. Then the sticky Kakeya theorem implies that β = 0.
  Since |T| ∼ δ −2 , if T is not sticky it means that there is some scale ρ ∈ [δ, 1] so that
|T[Tρ ]| ≪ (ρ/δ)−2 and |Tρ | ≫ ρ−2 . We will focus on the case that T is not-sticky-at-all-

   Assuming (21), we will sketch the proof of (20).
   In order to use the definition of β, we need to relate T with other sets of tubes.

µ(T′ ) ⪅ |T′ |β . In the not sticky case, it is tricky to find helpful sets of tubes T′ . Recall
that in the sticky case, Tρ and T[Tρ ] were both sticky Kakeya sets. In the not sticky
case, we still have ∆max (T[Tρ ]) ⪅ 1, but we don’t have ∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1, so it is harder to
make use of Tρ directly. One of the main new ideas in the proof is a way to find other
relevant sets of tubes T′ . We will see below a couple different clever ways of doing this.

12.2. A surprising induction
  This is a key philosophical moment in the proof. We are going to try to control TB
using induction. But the set of tubes TB need not obey ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1. This makes it
surprising to try to use TB in an inductive proof of Theorem 1.2.
  To put this moment in context, let us recall some more history about work on the
Kakeya problem. Bennett, Carbery, and Tao (2006) formulated and proved a multilinear
cousin of the Kakeya problem. Their proof was simplified in Guth (2015), and the proof
there is only a few pages long. Multilinear Kakeya involves n sets of tubes Tj in Rn ,
where the tubes of Tj are approximately parallel to the xj axis. Multilinear Kakeya is
important because it is much easier than Kakeya but still has many applications - for
instance in work of Bourgain and Demeter (2015) on decoupling theory.
                                        1251–29


   The key feature that makes multilinear Kakeya much easier than Kakeya is that if we
intersect the tubes of each Tj with a small ball B, then the resulting sets of tubes Tj,B
obey the hypotheses of multilinear Kakeya. Therefore, we can easily apply induction to
study the intersections of tubes in each small ball B. In contrast, the hypotheses of the
Kakeya problem do not behave well when we restrict the tubes of T to a small ball B.
   People in the field (including me) tried to adapt the inductive proof of multilinear
Kakeya to the original Kakeya problem and we all gave up. Since the set TB does not
obey the hypotheses of the Kakeya conjecture, how can we control it by induction?
   In the Wang–Zahl proof, we assume nothing at all about the set of tubes TB . But
no matter how TB behaves, they find a way to take advantage of it and prove that
µ(T) ≪ |T|β . There are several scenarios and we will discuss the most important
scenarios and how to take advantage of each one. This proof outline reminds me of a
game that was popular in my elementary school called “heads I win, tails you lose.”
   Before turning to details, let us think through the philosophical issue of how to apply
induction to TB . While TB itself does not obey ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1, Wang and Zahl manage
to relate TB to another set of tubes T′ with ∆max (T′ ) ⪅ 1, and then we can use that
µ(T′ ) ⪅ |T′ |β . As we go, we will see how to locate this new set of tubes T′ in various
scenarios.

12.3. Organizing the different cases
  If ∆max (TB ) ⪅ 1, then Lemma 12.1 gives us our goal: µ(T) ≪ |T|β . So we have to
consider the case that ∆max (TB ) ≫ 1. By definition, this means that there is some
convex set K ⊂ B so that ∆(TB , K) ≫ 1. We consider the set K that maximizes
∆(TB , K).
  Notice that for any set K ′ ⊂ K, ∆(TB , K ′ ) ≤ ∆(TB , K). This condition plays an
important role in the story, so we give it a name.

Definition 12.1. — If T̃ is a set of tubes all contained in a convex set K, then T̃ is
Frostman in K if for any convex K ′ ⊂ K,
                                 ∆(T̃, K ′ ) ⪅ ∆(T̃, K).

   In other words, T̃ is Frostman in K if the tubes of T̃ are contained in K and
∆max (T̃) ≈ ∆(T̃, K).

in K. (Wang and Zahl picked the name Frostman because the condition is similar to
the bound that appears in Frostman’s lemma in geometric measure theory.)
   Now the proof divides into cases depending on the shape of K. Since K is a convex
set which contains some tubes of TB , K is essentially a rectangular box of dimensions
a × b × r, where r is the radius of B. The proof divides into cases according to the
values of a and b.
   One important case is when K = B. In this section we focus on this important case.
Then in Section 13 we consider other possible shapes of K.

13. Other possible shapes of K

  Suppose that ∆max (TB ) = ∆(TB , K). In general K is a convex set of dimensions
a × b × r. So far we have discussed the two extreme cases: K = TB and K = B. Now
we turn to various intermediate cases. By doing some pigeonholing, we can assume
that there is a set K of convex sets K ⊂ B, all with the same dimensions, so that
∆(TB , K) ≈ ∆max (T) for each K ∈ K and so that each TB ∈ TB lies in one K ∈ K.
  Essentially this set K is formed by using the greedy algorithm. First we find the
convex set K1 which maximizes ∆(TB , K1 ). We call K1 the densest convex set for TB .
Then we remove TB [K1 ] from TB , and we let K2 be the densest convex set for the
remaining tubes. We continue in this way until all (or most) of the tubes of TB belong
to one of the sets K ∈ K.
  From the nature of this procedure, it follows that µ(K) ⪅ 1. The reason is that if many
sets K ∈ K pack into a larger convex set L, then we would have ∆(TB , L) ≫ ∆(TB , K),
                                          1251–32


and so the greedy algorithm would have chosen L instead of K. Also, for each K ∈ K,
TB [K] is Frostman.
  The argument divides into cases according to the shape of K.

   Case 1: Fat planks.
   If a ≫ δ, then we can adapt the high density argument from the last subsection to
show that U (T) ∩ K fills a surprisingly large fraction of K and hence U (T) ∩ Ba fills a
surprisingly large fraction of Ba . This requires some technical care, but the high level
ideas are the same as in the case K = B above, and the most ingredient is the high
density lemma, Lemma 12.2.
   This leaves the case when a ≈ δ, so K has dimensions roughly δ × b × r. When
we study how the tubes TB [K] sit inside of K, we essentially have a two-dimensional
Kakeya problem. Using the L2 method, it follows that |U (TB [K])| ⪆ |K|, and so TB [K]
essentially fills K.
   If b ≪ r, then we call K a thin plank, and if b ≈ r, then we call K a thin slab. In
the thin plank case, we have to study how the planks of K intersect each other. Each
plank K ∈ K has a two-dimensional tangent plane, spanned by the two longest axes
of K. When two planks intersect, we call the intersection tangential if the two tangent
planes are equal and we call the intersection transverse if the two tangent planes are
transverse.

   Case 2: Thin planks intersecting transversely.
   When most intersections are transverse, then the L2 method gives strong bounds. If
we intersect a plank with a ball of radius b, we get a slab. Inside such a ball Bb , we
would see several slabs intersecting transversely. Using the L2 method, it follows that
U (K) essentially fills Bb and so U (T) essentially fills Bb . Then we can finish the proof
as in Subsection 12.5.

   Case 3: Thin planks intersecting tangentially.
   In this case, we will relate µ(T) to µ(K). We will bound µ(K) by changing coordinates
so that the planks become a set of tubes T′ , and we bound µ(T′ ) using induction. Here
we sketch how to relate K to T′ .

intersections are tangential, then µ(K) ≈ µ(KS ).

13.1. How do we reduce to the not-sticky-at-all-scales case?
   We saw in the argument above that it was important to reduce to the not-sticky-at-
all-scales case. In this subsection, we explain how to do that. We start by recalling the
sticky case, the not-sticky case, and the not-sticky-at-all-scales case.
   Recall that T is a set of δ-tubes in B1 with ∆max (T) ⪅ 1 and that |T| ≈ δ −2 .
   The sticky case means that for every ρ ∈ [δ, 1], ∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1. Since |T| ≈ δ −2 , this
is equivalent to |T[Tρ ]| ≈ (δ/ρ)−2 .
                                        1251–34


  If T is not sticky, it means that there is some ρ ∈ [δ, 1] so that |T[Tρ ]| ≪ (δ/ρ)−2 .
Such a ρ must lie in the range δ ≪ ρ ≪ 1.
  We say that T is not-sticky-at-all-scales if |T[Tρ ]| ≪ (δ/ρ)−2 for every ρ in the range
δ ≪ ρ ≪ 1.
  Here is the rough idea how to reduce the not-sticky case to the not-sticky-at-all-scales
case. Suppose that there is some scale ρ so that |T[Tρ ]| ≈ (δ/ρ)−2 . It follows that
∆max (Tρ ) ⪅ 1. Now we can bound
                                 µ(T) ⪅ µ(T[Tρ ])µ(Tρ ),
which reduces our original problem to two similar problems at smaller scales. We try to
keep reducing in this way. If one of the smaller problems is not-sticky-at-all-scales then
we are stuck and we cannot reduce further. Otherwise we can reduce further. If we
can keep reducing in this way to very small problems, it means that our original set of
tubes T was sticky, and we can handle it using the sticky Kakeya theorem. Otherwise,
we get stuck with a problem that is not-sticky-at-all-scales and we can handle it using
the argument we have sketched in the last two sections.


14. The high density lemma

the statement of the high density lemma, W will be a set of tubes, but later in our
discussion we will see more general convex sets.

14.1. Looking for sticky Kakeya sets

contains a sticky Kakeya set T′ . In this case, |U (T)| ≥ |U (T′ )| ⪆ 1, and we are done.

14.2. Analyzing the case that T[Tρ ] is not Frostman
   If T[Tρ ] is not Frostman, then it means by definition that there is some subset W ⊂ Tρ
so that ∆(T[Tρ ], W ) ≫ ∆(T[Tρ ], Tρ ). As in Section 13, it is helpful to organize T[Tρ ] by
choosing sets W that maximize ∆(T[Tρ ], W ). As in the beginning of Section 13, we can
find a set W(Tρ ) of such maximal W by choosing them one at a time until each tube
T ∈ T[Tρ ] lies in ≈ 1 W ∈ W(Tρ ).
  We can assume that each W ∈ W(Tρ ) has roughly the same dimensions: each W has
dimensions roughly a × b × 1, where δ ≤ a ≤ b ≤ ρ. We will see that if T is a worst-case
Kakeya set, then it strongly constrains the geometry of W .

Lemma 14.2. — If T is a worst-case example for Lemma 12.2 as in (31) and W[Tρ ] is
defined as above, then a ≈ b. So each W ∈ W(Tρ ) is approximately a tube Ta of radius
a and length 1.

Proof sketch. — For each Tρ ∈ T[Tρ ], we have defined W(Tρ ). We gather all these sets

  Moreover, T[W ] and W are each Frostman. The set T[W ] is Frostman because we
chose W to maximize ∆(T[Tρ ], W ). To see that W is Frostman, we arrange by some
pigeonholing arguments that the sets T[W ] have roughly the same cardinality. Now if
K ⊂ B1 has ∆(W, K) ≫ ∆(W, B1 ) it would imply ∆(T, K) ≫ ∆(T, B1 ). Since T is
Frostman, it follows that W is Frostman too.
   Since T[W ] and W are each Frostman, it almost looks like we could bound µ(T[W ])
and µ(W) by induction using (30). That’s not quite possible because (30) applies to a
set of tubes which is Frostman in a ball, whereas T[W ] is a set of tubes that is Frostman
in a convex set W , and W is a set of convex sets which is Frostman in a ball. In order
to use induction, we generalize Lemma 12.2 and (30) to a set of convex sets which is
Frostman in another convex set. This more general version then applies to both T[W ]
and W.
   Suppose that V is a set of convex sets which is Frostman in a convex set U . By a
linear change of variables, we can reduce to the case that U = B1 . Then we examine
the dimensions of V ∈ V. Say each V ∈ V has dimensions aV × bV × 1. If aV ≈ bV , then
V is a tube and we can apply (30). The other extreme example is when V is a slab of
dimensions aV × 1 × 1. Sharp bounds for intersecting slabs have been known for a long
time by the L2 method. So in the slab case, we get very strong bounds for µ(V). This
leaves an intermediate case when V has dimensions aV × bV × 1 with aV ≪ bV ≪ 1. In
this case, the shape of V is intermediate between a tube and a slab. In fact, at large
scales V looks like a tube, but if we intersect V with a small ball, then it looks like
a slab. In this case, µ(V) can be bounded by a multiscale argument that combines
estimates for tubes from (30) with estimates for slabs from the L2 method.
  We apply this method to bound µ(T[W ]) and µ(W) and then plug those bounds
into (32) to bound µ(T). The computation shows that µ(T) ≪ (δ −2 )β (δ 2 |T|)γ unless
a ≈ b. In other words, if T is a worst-case example for Lemma 12.2, then a ≈ b.
   Morally, the reason that the computation works out this way is that the bounds for
slabs are sharp. When we unwind the argument above, we bound µ(T) by applying (30)
at some scales and the L2 bounds for slabs at other scales. Since the bound for slabs is
so strong, this improves on (30) as long as we use the slab bound at some scales. When
a ≈ b, then all the convex sets in the argument are tubes, and the slab bound never
appears. But if a ≪ b, then each W ∈ W looks like a plank and there are some scales
where the slab bounds come into play.

the Furstenberg set problem in Keleti and Shmerkin (2019) and Ren and Wang (2023).



15. Final recap

  At the beginning of the survey, we said that the hero of the proof was multiscale
analysis. We begin with a worst-case Kakeya set T, with µ(T) ≈ |T|β . We then relate T
                                         1251–40


to many other sets of tubes T′ obeying ∆max (T′ ) ⪅ 1. By definition of β, we know that
µ(T′ ) ⪅ |T′ |β , and this gives us information about T.
  As a final recap, let us look back and see how we found all these sets of tubes T′ .
We used several different ways to relate a set of tubes T to a new set of tubes, or more
generally to a new set of convex sets W. If we start with T, we can

   – Look at thicker tubes Tρ .
   – Look at T[Tρ ].
   – Intersect tubes of T with a smaller ball B and look at TB .
   – Find convex sets K that maximize ∆(T, K). Then we can look at T[K]. Also, we
     can form a set K of these convex sets K and look at K. Sometimes we can change
     coordinates to convert K to a set of tubes.

   These operations can be chained together. For instance, starting with T we might
first look at TB . Then we might find convex sets K that maximize ∆(TB , K) and
study K. After some coordinate changes, we might be led to a new set of tubes T′ . Or
starting with T we might first look at TB and then look at a thicker set of tubes TB,ρ .
Starting at T, we may need to use several operations to arrive at a new set T′ that
obeys ∆max (T′ ) ⪅ 1. By combining information from many such sets of tubes T′ , the
proof shows that if β > 0, the set T would have to have special geometric and algebraic
structure, closely matching the Heisenberg group.
   We also note that the argument is essentially by induction. If we unwind the induction,
then the argument effectively uses the above operations many times to get from the
initial set of tubes T to other sets of tubes T′ .
  At the beginning of the survey, we said that the proof of the Kakeya problem is based
on studying the problem at many scales. Looking at a problem at many scales has
been a central theme in harmonic analysis for a hundred years, and this proof can be
regarded as part of that tradition. But when we said that the proof is based on studying
the problem at many scales, we really meant that the proof is based on bringing into
play all the sets of tubes T′ that can be built from T by the operations above. This
version of multiscale analysis is much newer. It was built over the last twenty-five years,
with important parts of the picture appearing just in the last few years.
  To finish this essay, let us return to the Katz–Zahl example. As we mentioned in
Section 10, Katz and Zahl found a cousin problem to the Kakeya problem where the
analogue of Kakeya does not hold but the analogue of the sticky case appears likely
to hold. That example made me think it was unlikely that the Kakeya problem could
be reduced to the sticky case. Wang and Zahl did reduce the general Kakeya problem
to the sticky case, and so it is natural to ask why their method does not apply to the
Katz–Zahl example.
  The Katz–Zahl example concerns a cousin of the Kakeya problem where R is replaced
by the ring A = Fp [x]/(x2 ). The ring A has a natural notion of distance with two
                                         1251–41


distinct length scales. If a + bx ∈ A with a, b ∈ Fp , we define

   Looking back at the proof of the Kakeya conjecture, the key distinction between R
and the ring A is that the ring A has only two distinct non-zero scales. The proof of
the Kakeya conjecture requires discussing many scales in order to run the multiscale
analysis. In the ring A, we do not have the rich range of related sets of tubes T′ that
are used in the proof of the Kakeya conjecture.
